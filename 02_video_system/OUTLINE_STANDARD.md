# Outline Standard

**Status:** Canonical portfolio-wide standard  
**Purpose:** Convert research into a compelling long-form story architecture before full script drafting.

---

## 1. Role of the Outline

The outline is where information becomes a video.

It should prove that the story works before Green Knight invests in line-level writing.

A strong outline is not a table of contents.

It is a beat-by-beat narrative plan.

---

## 2. Required Inputs

The outline should use:

- approved video brief
- research packet
- source log
- channel context
- relevant packaging territory
- any required runtime constraints

---

## 3. Required Header

Include:

- video ID
- current working title
- current thesis
- target viewer question
- expected runtime
- intended payoff
- current packaging promise if known

---

## 4. Beat Structure

For each major beat or section include:

### Approximate Runtime
A range or expected duration.

### Narrative Function
What job does this section perform?

Examples:

- hook
- establish puzzle
- create stakes
- explain mechanism
- introduce complication
- reveal
- escalation
- counterpoint
- payoff
- implication
- ending

### Core Content
What information or story appears here?

### Viewer Question
What question is active in the viewer's mind?

### New Information
What does the viewer learn here that they did not know one beat earlier?

### Evidence
Relevant source or claim references.

### Visual Opportunity
What can the viewer see?

### Transition
Why should the viewer continue?

---

## 5. Hook Requirements

The opening should establish value quickly.

It should generally communicate:

- what this is about
- why it is interesting
- what is unusual, unresolved, or consequential
- what promise the viewer can expect fulfilled

Avoid generic throat-clearing.

Do not repeat the title without adding value.

---

## 6. Narrative Progression

The outline should create movement.

Each section should ideally do at least one of the following:

- answer a question
- create a better question
- raise stakes
- complicate the explanation
- reveal mechanism
- introduce consequence
- change the viewer's understanding

Sections that merely contain relevant information are not automatically useful.

---

## 7. Reveal Management

Do not artificially hide obvious facts simply to manufacture suspense.

At the same time, avoid explaining the entire conclusion in the opening if the story depends on discovery.

Reveal information in the order that creates the clearest and most satisfying understanding.

---

## 8. Background Context

Historical or technical context should earn its place.

Ask:

> Does the viewer need this now to understand what comes next?

Avoid front-loading background because it feels academically complete.

---

## 9. Counterpoints

Where the topic is contested or the thesis has meaningful limitations, incorporate them at the point where they improve understanding.

Do not bolt nuance onto the end as a disclaimer.

---

## 10. Ending

The ending should feel earned.

Possible ending functions:

- resolve the opening question
- reveal a larger implication
- reframe something familiar
- show consequence
- return to an opening image or idea
- leave the viewer with a durable insight

Avoid generic summaries that simply repeat the video.

---

## 11. Outline Critique

A critic should evaluate:

- hook strength
- story clarity
- escalation
- section necessity
- repetition
- pacing
- evidence
- channel fit
- visual feasibility
- thesis delivery
- payoff
- runtime realism
- whether a better structure exists

The critic should identify structural problems, not merely rewrite sentences.

---

## 12. Permitted Review Outcomes

- APPROVE
- REVISE
- REFRAME
- RESEARCH_MORE
- KILL

---

## 13. Failure Modes

Avoid:

- chronological dumping without narrative purpose
- "intro / history / business model / conclusion"
- one section containing all of the interesting material
- sections that repeat the same point
- weak hooks followed by long context
- artificial suspense
- outline beats without evidence
- interesting facts that do not advance the story
- conclusions that were obvious from the first minute
- packing too many distinct theses into one video

---

## 14. Depth

The outline should be detailed enough that the script is mostly an execution task rather than a structural invention task.

For a 12 to 20 minute video, 1,000 to 2,500 words may be reasonable depending on complexity.

Do not optimize to a fixed word count.

---

## 15. Completion Gate

The outline passes when a reviewer can answer:

1. Why will the viewer click?
2. Why will the viewer continue?
3. What changes in their understanding?
4. Where is the strongest payoff?
5. Does every major section earn its runtime?
6. Can the story be visualized?
7. Does the evidence support the structure?
