# Video Brief Standard

**Status:** Canonical portfolio-wide standard  
**Purpose:** Define what a selected video is intended to become before substantial research and production resources are committed.

---

## 1. Role of the Video Brief

The video brief is the first serious artifact in the lifecycle.

It converts a promising idea into a specific editorial hypothesis.

It should answer:

> What video are we trying to make, for whom, why should it exist, and what must research establish?

The brief should be concise enough to remain directional.

It should not prematurely become a script or research dump.

---

## 2. Required Inputs

Before drafting the brief, the system should have:

- channel context
- original video idea
- content pillar
- relevant channel history where available
- any obvious comparable videos or prior internal examples
- known constraints

---

## 3. Required Sections

### Video ID
Permanent identifier.

### Working Title
A temporary description, not final packaging.

### Topic
What subject is being covered?

### Core Thesis
The strongest current statement of what the video is actually saying.

The thesis should be falsifiable where possible.

### Viewer Question
What question should the viewer want answered?

### Why This Video Should Exist
Explain why the subject is worth spending time on.

### Why This Viewer Should Care
Connect the idea to the channel's defined audience.

### Channel Fit
Explain why this belongs on this channel specifically.

### Intended Framing
Describe the angle.

A topic can support many framings.

State the one being pursued.

### Core Surprise or Payoff
What changes in the viewer's understanding?

### Narrative Engine
What keeps the viewer moving through the story?

Potential engines include:

- mystery
- mechanism
- escalation
- chronology
- conflict
- transformation
- comparison
- investigation

### Initial Packaging Territory
Capture promising title or thumbnail directions without locking final packaging.

### What Must Be Proven
List the factual or analytical claims that research needs to establish.

### Key Research Questions
List the questions research must answer.

### Known Risks
Examples:

- thesis may be false
- numbers may be unavailable
- story may lack tension
- subject may be visually weak
- framing may be too technical
- topic may be too familiar to feel novel

### Kill Conditions
Identify discoveries that would make the proposed video not worth continuing.

### Open Questions
Preserve unresolved issues.

---

## 4. Depth

Typical target: approximately 300 to 900 words.

Longer is acceptable when complexity justifies it.

The brief should not contain every fact the researcher already knows.

It should create direction.

---

## 5. Strong Brief Characteristics

A strong brief:

- has a real thesis rather than only a topic
- clearly identifies why the audience cares
- gives research falsifiable questions
- identifies the expected payoff
- distinguishes channel fit from general interestingness
- acknowledges uncertainty
- does not pretend packaging is final
- allows research to change the conclusion

---

## 6. Failure Modes

Weak briefs often:

- merely rename the topic
- contain generic claims like "this is interesting"
- assume the thesis is true before research
- over-specify the narrative before evidence exists
- contain no viewer question
- ignore channel fit
- offer ten possible angles instead of choosing one
- use a final-sounding title to hide a weak thesis
- provide no kill conditions

---

## 7. Completion Gate

The brief passes when a researcher can answer:

1. What am I trying to establish?
2. What could prove the premise wrong?
3. Why does the audience care?
4. What would make the story satisfying?
5. What evidence is most important?
6. What is intentionally unresolved?

If those answers are unclear, do not advance to research.
