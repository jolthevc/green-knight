# Script Standard

**Status:** Canonical portfolio-wide standard  
**Purpose:** Define the standard for final narration that is accurate, compelling, recordable, and native to the channel.

---

## 1. Role of the Script

The script is the final spoken editorial product.

It should transform the approved outline into narration without changing the core thesis, evidence, or story architecture casually.

A script should be ready to record.

It should not contain unresolved research placeholders, internal debates, or instructions meant for the editor.

---

## 2. Required Inputs

Use:

- approved outline
- research packet
- source log
- channel `VOICE.md`
- channel `AUDIENCE.md`
- channel `CONTENT.md`
- approved outline Promise Lock
- pronunciation notes where relevant

---

## 3. Primary Requirements

The script should be:

- faithful to evidence
- clear when heard aloud
- compelling throughout
- appropriate for the defined audience
- recognizably in the channel's voice
- efficient
- visually producible
- aligned with the approved Promise Lock

---

## 4. Spoken Language

Write for the ear.

Prefer language that can be understood on first listen.

This often means:

- cleaner syntax
- fewer nested clauses
- deliberate repetition only when useful
- clear references
- manageable numbers
- explicit transitions when needed
- natural rhythm

Do not mistake conversational writing for casual filler.

---

## 5. Opening

The opening should fulfill the approved Promise Lock immediately.

It should establish:

- subject
- tension or question
- value
- reason to continue

Do not waste the opening on:

- generic greetings
- channel housekeeping
- broad historical context
- empty scene-setting
- restating the title without advancing it

---

## 6. Information Density

The script should feel rich, not crowded.

Every fact should earn its place by doing something useful:

- advancing the story
- establishing scale
- proving a claim
- creating surprise
- clarifying mechanism
- deepening stakes
- adding texture

Cut facts included only because they were interesting in research.

---

## 7. Explanation

Complex ideas should be simplified without becoming false.

Prefer:

- concrete examples
- intuitive comparisons
- staged explanations
- clearly defined terminology
- explicit causal chains

Avoid:

- jargon used for status
- unexplained acronyms
- analogy that distorts the mechanism
- oversimplification that changes the conclusion

---

## 8. Evidence and Uncertainty

The script must distinguish:

- fact
- estimate
- inference
- allegation
- contested interpretation
- speculation

Do not hide uncertainty to make the story sound stronger.

Do not introduce unnecessary legalistic hedging when evidence is clear.

---

## 9. Numbers

Numbers should be spoken in ways humans can process.

Ask:

- Does the exact number matter?
- Is comparison more useful?
- Should the number be rounded?
- Does the time period need to be stated?
- Is the population or geography clear?
- Can the viewer understand why the number matters?

---

## 10. Transitions

Transitions should create logical or narrative momentum.

Avoid repetitive AI-style connective phrases.

A transition should usually answer:

> Why are we moving to this next idea now?

---

## 11. Pacing

Vary density.

Not every sentence should carry equal weight.

Use:

- short sentences
- pauses
- reveals
- scene changes
- explanation stretches
- moments of synthesis

according to channel voice.

---

## 12. Ending

The ending should deliver a meaningful payoff.

Avoid:

- generic summaries
- obvious morals
- forced inspiration
- repetitive calls to action
- introducing a new thesis too late

If the channel uses calls to action, they should be governed separately and not overwhelm the editorial ending.

---

## 13. Source Integrity and Fact Gate

Every consequential claim should remain traceable to the source log.

Script polish must never sever factual traceability.

Before the script receives `APPROVE`, apply the script fact gate in `FACT_CHECK_STANDARD.md`.

Unsupported or materially over-strengthened claims should route to `REVISE` or `RESEARCH_MORE` rather than reaching narration and production.

---

## 14. Pronunciation and Narration Readiness

Before approval:

- spell or annotate difficult pronunciations as needed
- normalize awkward numbers
- identify acronyms
- remove notes not intended to be spoken
- check proper nouns
- read difficult passages aloud or simulate spoken delivery

---

## 15. Script Critique

The critic should assess:

- hook
- voice
- clarity
- pacing
- retention risk
- repetition
- evidence
- unsupported claims
- channel fit
- package fulfillment
- ending
- spoken naturalness

The critic should not rewrite merely to make the prose different.

---

## 16. Permitted Review Outcomes

- APPROVE
- REVISE
- RETURN_TO_OUTLINE
- RESEARCH_MORE
- KILL

---

## 17. Failure Modes

Avoid:

- polished but generic AI prose
- essay voice where spoken storytelling is needed
- excessive scene-setting
- fake certainty
- fake excitement
- repetitive rhetorical questions
- saying the same thing several ways
- burying the thesis
- overloading with numbers
- editorial notes left inside narration
- script sections that cannot be visualized
- title promise not fulfilled

---

## 18. Completion Gate

The script passes when:

1. it can be recorded immediately
2. the story still works when heard without seeing research notes
3. important claims are supported
4. the channel voice is recognizable
5. the approved Promise Lock is fulfilled
6. there are no obvious unresolved editorial decisions
