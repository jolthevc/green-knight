# Audience Definition

**Status:** Canonical channel-template artifact  
**Purpose:** Define the human being this channel is designed to serve.

---

## How to Use This File

Do not reduce the audience to demographics.

Age, geography, income, occupation, or gender can matter, but they rarely explain why somebody chooses a video.

The primary purpose of this document is to describe the viewer's psychology, existing knowledge, motivations, tastes, and behavior well enough that an AI or creator can make better editorial decisions.

The audience definition should be specific enough to guide choices without becoming so narrow that it artificially limits the channel.

---

## 1. Primary Viewer

Describe the core viewer in plain language.

Write this as a recognizable person, not a media-planning segment.

Cover:

- what they are naturally curious about
- how sophisticated they are about the subject
- why they use YouTube
- how much effort they want to expend while watching
- what they find rewarding
- what they find tedious
- what makes something feel worth 10 to 20 minutes of their attention

---

## 2. Viewer Motivation

Why does this person click?

Potential motivations include:

- curiosity
- surprise
- self-improvement
- entertainment
- status
- usefulness
- explanation
- nostalgia
- aspiration
- fear
- humor
- discovery
- identity
- professional relevance

Rank or describe the motivations that matter most for this channel.

---

## 3. Desired Viewer Payoff

What should the viewer feel, know, understand, or be able to say after a strong video?

Examples:

- "I had no idea that was how it worked."
- "That was much more interesting than I expected."
- "I finally understand this."
- "I need to send this to someone."
- "I want another story like that."

Use channel-specific language.

---

## 4. Knowledge Baseline

Define what the channel can reasonably assume the viewer already knows.

Also define what should be explained rather than assumed.

This is especially important for technical, financial, historical, scientific, or industry-specific content.

Avoid two opposite failures:

- over-explaining basic concepts until the content feels slow
- assuming so much expertise that the channel excludes a broader interested audience

---

## 5. Sophistication Level

Describe the intended level of intellectual complexity.

Consider:

- vocabulary
- data density
- conceptual complexity
- assumed domain knowledge
- tolerance for nuance
- willingness to follow multi-step explanations

Explain how the channel should simplify without becoming simplistic.

---

## 6. Attention Profile

Describe how this viewer consumes long-form YouTube.

Consider:

- likely viewing context
- tolerance for slow openings
- desired pacing
- need for visual novelty
- tolerance for sidebars
- willingness to watch 10, 20, or 30 minutes
- whether the viewer prefers concise explanation or immersive storytelling

---

## 7. Emotional Profile

What emotional states should the content create?

Examples:

- amused
- fascinated
- surprised
- reassured
- energized
- outraged
- inspired
- unsettled
- nostalgic
- impressed

Also document emotional tones that would alienate the audience.

---

## 8. What This Viewer Finds Boring

Be explicit.

Examples might include:

- jargon-heavy exposition
- generic biography
- obvious conclusions
- lengthy setup before the core question
- corporate language
- repetitive statistics
- excessive moralizing
- content that feels like school
- obvious AI phrasing
- unnecessary historical background

Use channel-specific examples.

---

## 9. What This Viewer Clicks

Describe the subject and packaging patterns likely to earn attention.

Consider:

- recognizable names
- surprising mechanisms
- stakes
- mysteries
- transformations
- hidden systems
- conflict
- status
- numbers
- comparisons
- impossible-seeming claims
- familiar objects viewed differently

This should guide `PACKAGING.md`, but should not duplicate its detailed rules.

---

## 10. What Creates Subscription

Explain what makes this viewer want a relationship with the channel rather than merely one video.

Consider:

- recurring subject
- recurring perspective
- trusted explanation
- entertainment format
- personality
- consistent surprise
- utility
- identity

---

## 11. Adjacent Audiences

Identify meaningful secondary audiences that may also respond.

For each, explain:

- why the channel could appeal to them
- what changes would broaden appeal
- what changes would damage the core audience

This helps the channel expand without losing coherence.

---

## 12. Comparable Media Consumption

List channels, publications, podcasts, shows, creators, websites, communities, or genres the viewer may already enjoy.

For each reference, record what is relevant:

- topic
- pace
- voice
- depth
- visual language
- humor
- storytelling
- packaging
- audience relationship

Do not use references as permission to imitate.

---

## 13. Anti-Audience

Describe viewers the channel is not primarily optimizing for.

This is useful when serving one audience well requires disappointing another.

Examples:

- professionals seeking technical certification-level depth
- viewers who only want breaking news
- viewers seeking explicit investment advice
- very young audiences
- viewers who require an on-camera personality

---

## 14. Audience Assumptions to Test

List beliefs about the viewer that should be tested through early content and analytics.

For each:

- **Assumption**
- **Why it matters**
- **How we might observe it**
- **What would change if it is wrong**

---

## Completion Standard

This file is ready when a writer can make a difficult editorial choice and answer:

> Which option will better serve the person this channel exists for?

If the audience still feels like "everyone interested in X," this document is not finished.
