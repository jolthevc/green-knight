# Content Definition

**Status:** Canonical channel-template artifact  
**Purpose:** Define the editorial universe, recurring content engine, and boundaries of the channel.

---

## How to Use This File

This document governs what the channel makes.

It should be broad enough to sustain a large library and flexible enough to allow creative discovery, but specific enough that an AI can reject ideas that technically relate to the subject yet do not fit the brand.

This is not a list of video ideas.

It is a system for generating and judging video ideas.

---

## 1. Editorial Territory

Describe the overall subject universe in which the channel operates.

Define:

- central territory
- adjacent territory
- subjects that are theoretically related but outside the brand

---

## 2. Core Content Engine

State the repeatable editorial mechanism that creates videos.

A useful content engine describes the type of story or discovery, not merely the category.

Explain why this engine should remain interesting over many videos.

---

## 3. Content Pillars

Define the major recurring pillars.

For each pillar include:

### Pillar Name
[NAME]

### Definition
What belongs here?

### Viewer Appeal
Why does the audience care?

### Strong Topic Characteristics
What makes a topic in this pillar promising?

### Weak Topic Characteristics
What tends not to work?

### Example Concepts
Provide representative examples.

### Expansion Potential
Where can this pillar grow over time?

Repeat for each pillar.

---

## 4. Sub-Pillars and Recurring Motifs

Document recurring topic classes, story structures, or questions that may appear across multiple pillars.

Examples:

- hidden economics
- failed systems
- unexpected monopolies
- design tradeoffs
- business incentives
- strange historical accidents
- extreme edge cases

Use channel-specific motifs.

---

## 5. Recurring Formats

If the channel uses recognizable episode formats, define them.

For each format include:

- purpose
- ideal subject
- typical structure
- expected length
- strengths
- risks
- how often it should appear

Do not invent recurring formats merely for the sake of consistency.

---

## 6. Topic Selection Principles

Define what tends to make a strong topic for this channel.

Potential dimensions include:

- familiarity
- surprise
- stakes
- visual richness
- emotional resonance
- novelty
- explanatory depth
- evergreen value
- timeliness
- search interest
- recommendation potential
- title potential
- thumbnail potential
- source availability

Explain how these factors interact.

---

## 7. Framing Principles

A topic can support many videos.

Define how this channel should turn subjects into compelling editorial theses.

A good framing should generally answer:

- What is the actual question?
- What is surprising?
- What tension drives the story?
- What changes in the viewer's understanding?
- Why is this worth watching now?

Include examples of weak topic-only concepts versus strong framed concepts.

---

## 8. Evergreen vs Timely Content

Define the intended balance.

Explain:

- what qualifies as evergreen
- when timely content is appropriate
- how quickly timely content becomes stale
- whether breaking news is permitted
- how a timely subject can be reframed into durable content

---

## 9. Content Depth

Define how deep the channel should go.

Consider:

- research depth
- length
- number of ideas per video
- amount of historical context
- use of data
- technical explanation
- competing interpretations
- narrative complexity

The goal is to avoid both superficiality and unnecessary complexity.

---

## 10. In-Scope Examples

Provide 10 to 30 example video concepts that clearly belong.

These are examples, not a fixed backlog.

For each, include a one-line explanation of why it fits.

---

## 11. Near-Miss Examples

Provide examples that look relevant at first glance but should probably not be made.

Explain why.

Near-misses are especially useful for training judgment.

---

## 12. Out-of-Scope Content

Define content the channel should not make.

Include both obvious and non-obvious exclusions.

---

## 13. Content Failure Modes

Examples may include:

- generic topics with no thesis
- repeating the same mechanism under different names
- chasing search traffic that does not fit the brand
- making videos because competitors made them
- selecting topics that cannot be visualized
- over-indexing on obscure subjects
- over-indexing on recognizable subjects
- confusing expertise with complexity
- becoming news-dependent
- relying on controversy without substance

Tailor this section to the channel.

---

## 14. Content Expansion Map

Describe plausible future adjacencies if the initial wedge works.

Separate:

- natural extensions
- experimental extensions
- extensions that would likely require a distinct channel

This prevents early overexpansion while preserving strategic optionality.

---

## 15. Content Hypotheses to Test

List channel-specific editorial hypotheses.

For each include:

- hypothesis
- proposed test
- expected signal
- potential confounders
- decision implication

---

## Completion Standard

This file is ready when:

1. an AI can generate many relevant ideas without becoming generic
2. an AI can reject superficially related but off-brand ideas
3. the channel has enough content supply to support a meaningful library
4. the pillars are distinct but collectively coherent
5. the framing philosophy is clearer than the subject category alone
