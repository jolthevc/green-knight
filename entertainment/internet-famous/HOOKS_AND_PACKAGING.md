# Hooks, title writing, thumbnails and audience promises

## The hierarchy
**Topic -> unresolved paradox -> documented surprise -> emotional consequence -> title/thumbnail -> first frame -> 0:00-0:30 hook -> rest of film -> earned payoff.**

Think of the title as a contract. It should be almost impossible for a curious viewer to resist, but the script must fulfill its specific promise.

## Respectful packaging rule
Use **mystery, paradox, scale, contrast, social behavior and consequential turns**, not insults or unverified allegations. A thumbnail may never mock appearance, imply criminality or wrongdoing without strong support, weaponize an out-of-context emotional image, or republish harassment as its own punchline. Make the INTERNET's behavior the dramatic event, not the target's alleged defects. See [PEOPLE_AND_FAIRNESS.md](PEOPLE_AND_FAIRNESS.md).

## HIGH-CURIOSITY PACKAGING MANDATE (2026-10-19)

**Owner feedback:** Prior channel titles sound like competent documentary descriptions, not titles that stop a scrolling viewer. Correct this before every future script, not as a final cosmetic step. Aim for high-impact, emotionally immediate, accessible, unusually specific curiosity *within factual and fairness limits*.

### The title is an advertisement for a question, not a summary of the episode

A clickable working headline should create a **consequential unanswered question** or **surprising contradiction** in very few words. It should provoke one of these responses: "Wait, what happened?", "How is that possible?", "I remember that; I never knew this part", "Why would they do that?", or "That can't be true. Is it?"

Prefer:
- A documented **reversal**: famous -> gone; beloved -> costly; universal -> split.
- A startling **verified number or ultimatum**, when the story actually explains it.
- A provocative **specific event**: the photograph everyone argued about; a real negotiation that came before a shutdown.
- A **recognizable name, platform or object** up front, unless the thumbnail supplies unmistakable identification.
- A plain-English **consequence, conflict or extreme contrast** over abstract jargon.
- A short sentence with strong verbs. Use punctuation for contrast when natural. Avoid formulaic capitalization, ALL CAPS, repetitious "SHOCKING", and invented scandal.

**Avoid:** "The Story of...", "A Look Inside...", "The Rise and Fall of..." by default; names of generic business concepts; perfectly accurate but emotionally flat descriptions ("The App That Made Superstars, Then Disappeared"); titles so abstract that only existing fans grasp the stakes.

**Do not overcorrect:** "EVERYONE", "THE ENTIRE INTERNET", "EXPOSED", "STOLE", "KILLED", "SECRET", "BILLIONS", and similar dramatic language cannot be used when they convey a misleading literal claim. Vague hyperbole may work stylistically, but the package must not assert an untrue outcome, villain, motive or direct causation.

### Mandatory packaging sequence

1. Write the **hookable fact first**: the strongest verified concrete scene, dispute, surprise, number or contradiction.
2. Draft at least **10 titles** across three styles: broad nostalgic/mystery; specific insider revelation; human/cultural paradox.
3. Pair the best **three titles** with **three distinct phone-readable thumbnails**. These are nine pairings only if useful; prefer coherent complements over mechanical combinations.
4. Cold-read as a nonfan. The named subject and *why to care* must register in roughly a second. Remove context required to understand the headline.
5. Score curiosity, surprise, stakes, specificity, simplicity, accuracy, dignity and **payoff in the first 30 seconds**. A title that wins only by deception fails.
6. Select one **recommended** package and keep other strong candidates internal until actual A/B testing or owner request. If YouTube offers thumbnail testing, its outcome informs thumbnails; never claim title testing or CTR evidence that has not occurred.
7. Build/revise the first 30 seconds around what the title promises. The 15-minute documentary must fulfill that promise and take the viewer somewhere more interesting.
8. If research cannot support a tantalizing, true hook, change the **story angle or topic**, not merely its adjectives. Mark packaging as pending owner approval until selected.

**Soft craft target:** Use the shortest title that carries the curiosity. Do not enforce an arbitrary character limit if a slightly longer highly specific headline works better, but scrutinize long titles for truncation on mobile.

### Examples for currently drafted films (CANDIDATES, not final titles)

**Vine:** The existing working title *The App That Made Superstars, Then Disappeared* explains the episode but lacks a punch. Candidate:
- **Vine Was Everywhere. Then It Vanished.** (broad, emotionally clear but not very differentiated)
- **Vine's Biggest Stars Gave It an Ultimatum. Then It Died.** (specific insider conflict; verify the negotiated demands and DO NOT suggest the ultimatum alone caused closure)
- **The $21 Million Ultimatum Before Vine Died** (rounding from the reported 18 x $1.2m proposal; clarify that negotiations included creator-safety and product requests, not an accepted payment)
Recommended for editorial testing: *Vine's Biggest Stars Gave It an Ultimatum. Then It Died.* Proposed thumbnail: green Vine icon with fracture / "ULTIMATUM" or "$21.6M" / simple creator silhouettes. Actual visuals must not misrepresent people or documentary evidence.

**The Dress:** The old *The Photo That Made Millions Question Their Own Eyes* is precise but reads like an informative science article.
- **This Photo Made the Internet Lose Its Mind** (broadest emotional package)
- **The Photo That Divided the Internet** (recognizable, lower conflict specificity)
- **Why Millions of People Couldn't Agree on This Photo** (puzzle-forward)
Recommended for editorial testing: *This Photo Made the Internet Lose Its Mind*. Proposed thumbnail: clearly identifiable dress presented with color disagreement and short text "WHAT COLOR?". Original photo requires documented rights evaluation/clearance before actual use.

**Non-negotiable:** Title/thumbnail must be calibrated to what the released cut ACTUALLY proves. If the approved script does not justify a headline, fix and reapprove the script; never change the final title without matching the first-frame/hook/payoff and source review.

## Title frameworks
1. The Internet Made [Person] Famous for [Unexpected Reason]
2. How [Creator] Turned [Apparently Small Thing] Into [Surprising Outcome]
3. The [Viral Moment] That Changed [Platform/Career] Forever
4. Everyone [Believed X]. The Internet Did [Y].
5. [Person] Had Millions of [Views/Fans]. Then [Verified Reversal].
6. Why [Popular Internet Phenomenon] Actually Worked
7. The [Documented, Unusual] Cost of Becoming Internet Famous
8. The [Platform] Star Who [Unexpected Consequence]
9. The [Picture/Sound/Video] That Made the Internet [Unexpected Action]
10. How [Tiny Meme/Format] Became [Worldwide Ritual/Unexpected Cultural Effect]
11. Why Everyone Started [Bizarre Online Behavior]
12. The Viral Trend That [Surprising Documented Result]
13. How a [Six-Second Clip/Single Photo/Repeated Phrase] Took Over the Internet

They are starting shapes, not formulas that must be used. Replace generic phrases with accurate, vivid details.

## Packaging craft
- Lead with a person, platform, object or cultural moment people can identify in one glance.
- One dramatic contradiction, not three competing promises.
- Thumbnails: one face/object, strong contrast and one visual relationship. Text 0-3 words only if indispensable; no fake crying faces, fabricated messages or misleading money figures.
- Title adds context or stakes; thumbnail adds a DIFFERENT visual question rather than repeating title words.
- Make versions for phone-size examination. If the subject is obscure, make the stakes instantly understandable.
- Candidate working titles are not confirmed factual statements until research verifies every implication.
- Internally produce 8-12 title candidates and 3 thumbnail sketches; choose ONE for the final editor packet.

## Opening hook formula, flexible
**0:00-0:06:** the startling image or fact, without intro/branding.
**0:06-0:18:** complicate it with a contradiction, surprising scale, human cost, unexpected reward, conflict or reversal, never a demeaning punchline.
**0:18-0:30:** give a real partial answer or evidence, crystallize the deeper mystery and promise the payoff.
**0:30-0:60:** transition into the first concrete scene, not a lengthy thesis statement.

Alternate structure is allowed when it plays better; first 30 seconds must never be a generic chronology, 'in this video we'll explain,' or list of claims about how remarkable someone is.

## Sample development language, NOT researched or approved narration
Rebecca Black case:
- Proposed title: 'Why Millions Shared a Song They Said They Hated'
- Thesis to test: hostile curiosity and peer sharing can create enormous reach without creating a loyal audience.
- Rough hook shape: a teenage singer becomes widely recognized; online audiences share a performance while publicly deriding it; investigate *the audience's distribution behavior* and the difference between attention and admiration. Narration never mocks her or reenacts hostile commentary as entertainment.
- IMPORTANT: verify precise age/date, scale, reception, harassment and eventual career before script lock; do not state an invented exact ratio of critics to fans.

Vine case:
- Proposed title: 'The App That Made Everybody Famous, Then Disappeared'
- Thesis to test: format creates portable stars while the host platform may struggle to capture the value it generates.
- Avoid the false implication that literally everybody became famous.

Dream case:
- Proposed title: 'The Face Reveal That Changed Everything'
- Thesis to test: anonymity creates collective imagination and identity expectations, making a reveal unusually consequential.
- Define and prove what 'changed' or reject title.

## Phenomenon-led packaging examples (working, unresearched)
- 'Why One Photo Made the Entire Internet Disagree' (The Dress): the viral puzzle is collective interpretation, not a person.
- 'How a Simple Challenge Took Over the Internet' (Ice Bucket Challenge): unpack participation, altruism and imitation; verify actual public impact.
- 'The Video Format Everyone Copied' (Harlem Shake): explain replication and distribution; do not confuse one account's fame with the format's spread.
- 'The Internet Joke That Refused to Die' (Rickrolling): why a participatory gag remains recognizable.

## No empty manipulation
Forbidden devices: 'They never wanted you to know' without documented concealment; fabricated 'deleted tweets'; 'the shocking truth' with no specific insight; fake betrayals; unverified criminality or debt; implying an outcome before it happened; false allegations by juxtaposing unrelated images; delaying an answer the narrator already has through repetitive teases.

## Internal packaging scorecard
Rate 1-5 each:
- Instantly comprehensible curiosity
- Strong documented contradiction or stakes
- Recognizable subject and visual hook
- Accuracy of implication
- Respectfulness, fairness and absence of reputational insinuation
- Distinctiveness from prior coverage
- First 30 seconds pay the promise
- Final payoff rewards whole watch

Any factual-accuracy score below 5 or first-30-second promise below 4 blocks release regardless of overall score. Scores are editorial judgments, not predicted CTR data.
