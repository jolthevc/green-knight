# Internet Famous: human dignity, fairness and legal-risk editorial standard

**Mandatory and non-negotiable.** Internet Famous investigates how virality works. It is not a vehicle for sneering at people, harassment, pile-ons, rumors or humiliation. Be intensely entertaining about the *story and system*, never cruel toward the human subject.

## Tone: sharp on phenomena, humane toward people
- Criticize **verifiable actions, choices, incentives, business models, platforms, institutions and public claims** rather than mocking someone’s looks, body, voice, perceived intelligence, disability, race, ethnicity, gender, sexuality, religion or personality.
- No insults disguised as analysis: no “idiot,” “pathetic,” “loser,” “ugly,” “cringe freak,” “deserved it,” or demeaning punchlines targeted at individuals. Avoid using hostile audience comments as entertainment even if the comments really existed.
- Humor can target internet absurdity, platform incentives, public contradictions and our collective behavior. Punch at the spectacle, not at someone's vulnerability.
- When someone was bullied or became a meme against their wishes, describe the **bullying as part of the phenomenon**, do not recreate or endorse it. Do not make humiliation the viewer reward.
- Be empathetic without becoming PR or pretending everyone acted perfectly. Documented misconduct can be examined firmly, proportionally, and fairly.
- A fair portrayal would be recognizable to a reasonable viewer familiar with the evidence, including relevant subject responses, context and credible counterevidence.

## Factual and reputational-risk rules
- Treat reputational allegations as **high stakes**. Any implication of wrongdoing or a damaging factual assertion must have strong sourcing, meaningful context and precise attribution. Use original public records or statements and reputable contemporaneous reporting where practical.
- Separate **established fact, allegation, dispute, opinion, inference and speculation**. Attribution alone does not make an otherwise false or defamatory statement safe. Do not recycle internet rumors merely because someone else posted them.
- Give the subject's relevant on-record explanation, denial or later correction reasonable and proportionate consideration. Do not edit public comments to reverse their meaning.
- No guessing private motives, inventing dialogue, intimate details, secret messages, personal finances, psychological diagnoses, crimes or private history. Never create faux “leaks” or screenshot facsimiles that could be mistaken for authentic documents.
- Avoid identifying private individuals unnecessarily. No doxxing, private addresses, contact information, or identifying minors merely for virality. Take special care with children, victims, deceased persons, medical crises, trauma, and nonconsensual online exposure.
- Do not encourage audiences to attack, contact, shame or track down anyone. Do not include hostile commentary or search prompts likely to incite dogpiling.
- Verify quotations, chronology, dates, view counts, evidence excerpts, on-screen text, third-party screenshots and visual juxtapositions. A defensible spoken line can be undermined by a misleading thumbnail or insinuating edit.
- If a key allegation is weakly sourced, **remove it, research more or reject the episode**. “Allegedly” and a disclaimer are not magical legal shields.
- Keep source URLs, quote context, retrieval date, rights assessment, disagreements, corrections and the exact factual support for title/thumbnail in the episode's internal claim ledger.
- For sensitive allegations, disputed conduct, minors, privacy or especially reputation-damaging claims, escalate for **qualified legal/editorial prepublication review** and delay release if unresolved. This process reduces risk; it does **not guarantee protection from a lawsuit**.
- Separate intellectual-property rights from defamation/privacy review. Publicly visible posts, music or footage are not automatically cleared for reuse.

## The respectful clickbait test
Choose high-voltage language around *verified scale, paradoxes, turns, unintended consequences and internet behavior*, not personal degradation.

**Prefer** “How the Internet Turned a Song Into a Punchline” to calling its teenage performer a punchline.
**Prefer** “Why Everyone Argued About the Same Photo” to “The Idiots Who Couldn't See the Color.”
**Prefer** “Why the Internet Wouldn't Stop Sharing This” to “The Most Embarrassing Person on the Internet.”

These are editorial examples, not approved final titles or independently verified episode claims. Final packaging must survive factual scrutiny in the specific case.

## Required episode-level review (PASS / REVISE / STOP)
1. Who might suffer reputational or privacy harm? Are they a public figure, incidental private individual or minor?
2. Which precise lines/images imply wrongdoing, dishonesty, malice or personal defect? What evidence supports each?
3. Does the subject's relevant response or contradictory context change the viewer's inference?
4. Is the joke aimed at a system/behavior, or is the viewer being invited to mock a person?
5. Would a thumbnail, screen grab, title or dramatic sound imply more than the evidence?
6. Does this story have a public-interest explanation beyond merely replaying humiliation?
7. Is sensitive outside legal/editorial review required? What remains unresolved?

**Editorial stop:** Any factual allegation or visual implication without adequate support, any dehumanizing/mocking treatment, private invasive material without a compelling and reviewed justification, or a title that sells an untrue accusation. Never label such a film ready for editor delivery.
