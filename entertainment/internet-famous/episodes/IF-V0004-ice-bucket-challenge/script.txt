TITLE: How Ice Buckets Raised $115M

On August fourteenth, 2014, a man who could no longer speak challenged the president of the United States, from a wheelchair at Fenway Park. The challenge? Dump a bucket of ice water on your head. The reason was a disease he was living with: ALS.

A few weeks later, Facebook had seen more than seventeen million ice bucket videos. The ALS Association received one hundred and fifteen million dollars. Then organizers tried to bring the challenge back the following summer. By late August, the repeat had raised just five hundred thousand dollars.

How did an ordinary bucket become a fundraising phenomenon? Why was the magic so hard to repeat? And when the videos stopped, what happened to all that money?

You remember the videos. A friend stands in a driveway. Somebody counts to three. Ice water hits. There is screaming, laughing, maybe a phone that almost gets soaked. Then comes the important part: they name the next people who have to do it.

Bill Gates built a contraption for his bucket. Oprah took part. Athletes challenged other athletes. The appeal was absurdly simple. It was a dare you could film with a phone—and a punch line everyone knew was coming.

But the challenge was attached to something that wasn't funny. ALS—amyotrophic lateral sclerosis—is a progressive disease that damages the nerve cells controlling voluntary muscles. It can take away a person's ability to walk, speak, swallow, and breathe independently. There is still no cure.

The man at Fenway was Pete Frates. He had been a captain of Boston College's baseball team. He was twenty-seven when ALS was diagnosed in 2012. By the time the world was laughing at bucket videos, Pete and his family had been fighting for people to take the disease seriously. And the story of how they got the world's attention starts nowhere near Fenway Park.

July fifteenth, 2014. In Florida, a golfer named Chris Kennedy films himself pouring ice water over his head. Ice-water dares were already spreading online for different causes. Kennedy didn't invent the stunt. He made a choice that turned out to be much more important: he connected it to someone in his own family.

His cousin Jeanette Senerchia lived in Pelham, New York. Her husband, Anthony, had ALS. He'd lived with the disease for more than a decade. Chris challenged Jeanette as a way to show support. She took the dare, filmed her own bucket, and nominated friends. They knew the family. They knew why the challenge mattered.

Now the crucial coincidence. A little way away, in Yonkers, Pat Quinn was also living with ALS. Diagnosed in 2013, just after turning thirty, Pat had begun building his own community of supporters. The challenge reached him through overlapping social circles. He recognized that the videos could carry more than a joke, and he pushed the idea through his network.

That network led to Pete Frates. Pete had friends and former teammates throughout the Boston sports world, and his family was already organizing a serious campaign for ALS awareness. They were not starting from zero. The tiny ice-water chain was about to collide with years of relationships, organizing, and determination.

This matters because the origin story isn't a mastermind inventing the perfect viral stunt. The bucket existed. A family gave it a cause. One community passed it to another. And when it reached people with a powerful reason to spread it—and a network ready to help—it took on an entirely different life.

On July thirty-first, Pete posted a challenge video. The circle widened. His supporters kept commenting, tagging, and sharing. The next wave would reach beyond ALS communities and into the feeds of people who had never heard his name.

Look at the rules. Get nominated. Put a bucket over your head. Post the video. Challenge other people. In many versions, you had twenty-four hours to respond. You could donate, you could encourage donations, or you could do both. There wasn't one central referee enforcing a perfectly uniform set of instructions.

And that might be why it worked. Your friend wasn't sharing another charity appeal you could scroll past. They were calling you out by name. Publicly. Suddenly the story wasn't only about a disease you might know very little about. It was also about you.

Imagine seeing a video in which your colleague nominates you and two others. You don't just watch the splash; you watch to hear the names. If you take part, your own friends become the next audience. In other words, each video could recruit the cast of the next video.

Then famous people entered the chain. Facebook's Mark Zuckerberg challenged Bill Gates. Gates made a machine to deliver the water. Oprah joined in. So did musicians, athletes, and politicians. The celebrities made the spectacle bigger, but the basic unit was still a person nominating someone they knew—or desperately wanted to reach.

By the start of September, Facebook said more than seventeen million challenge-related videos had been shared on its platform. Those videos had been played over ten billion times by more than four hundred and forty million people. Ten billion plays, not ten billion different people. Still, try to imagine the attention contained in those numbers.

And money followed. The ALS Association in the United States received about one hundred and fifteen million dollars in 2014. Across organizations and countries, the challenge raised more than two hundred and twenty million. The same action had crossed languages, time zones, generations, and celebrity circles.

Which leaves a fascinating problem. Thousands of internet trends get copied. Most don't raise anything close to that amount. What did this one have that made people actually participate?

A normal viral video gives you a reason to watch. The Ice Bucket Challenge gave you a reason to make the next video. That's the difference. It turned an audience into a supply chain of participants.

The nomination was the engine. It was personal, public, and slightly difficult to ignore. And because you had to name someone after you, the clip included its own instructions for how the story should continue. The next episode was already being cast before the first one ended.

The entertainment helped. Nobody needed to sit through a lecture before taking part. The surprise wasn't what would happen; everyone knew the water was coming. It was how each person would react, and who they would challenge next. The format worked for a teenager in a driveway and a billionaire with a bucket machine.

It also let different motives coexist. Some people were deeply committed to ALS research. Others wanted to support a friend. Some enjoyed making a funny video, or didn't want to ignore a public challenge. We shouldn't pretend every participant gave money, or that every single video educated its audience about the disease. That's not something the viral numbers tell us.

But the combination was powerful. A reason to care. A reason to perform. A reason to share. And an easy way to ask someone else to join. The appeal travelled inside something people were already delighted to watch.

If that sounds like a formula anyone could copy, the next summer provided a brutal reality check.

In 2015, ALS advocates invited people to pick up the buckets again. The slogan was clear: every August until a cure. The disease was still there. The need was still urgent. And by then everybody knew what an Ice Bucket Challenge was.

That was the problem. In late August 2015, the ALS Association told the Chronicle of Philanthropy that the new campaign had brought in about five hundred thousand dollars that month. It wasn't a final annual total, and half a million dollars mattered. But the comparison with the previous summer's one hundred and fifteen million was staggering.

A year earlier, getting nominated made you part of an event people were discovering in real time. In 2015, it could feel like reenacting an event everyone had already seen. Same bucket. Same rules. Very different social energy.

That doesn't mean the original campaign was a lucky accident, or that ALS supporters disappeared. People kept fundraising. The challenge continued in smaller circles. What the second summer exposed was a hard limit: you can copy the instructions for a viral moment. You cannot schedule the exact network, surprise, timing, and urgency that carried it the first time.

But the dip in views created another, more serious question. If the crowd moves on, what happens to the money it left behind?

A hundred and fifteen million dollars arriving almost overnight creates a strange responsibility. You can spend it fast and hope it helps. Or you can build research programs that take years before anyone knows whether they worked. With ALS, a disease that moves faster than most clinical trials, neither path is easy.

The ALS Association committed money to research, services for people living with the disease, and advocacy. The charity later published details of its spending and commitments. It expanded research grants and supported more clinics and scientists. But that's still an accounting answer. What did those investments actually make possible?

In 2016, researchers supported by Ice Bucket Challenge donations helped identify an ALS-associated gene called NEK1 through an international effort known as Project MinE. Finding a gene is not the same as inventing a cure. It gives researchers another clue about what causes the disease, and another place to investigate.

Then there is the longer view. A 2024 assessment by research organization RTI International, commissioned by the ALS Association, found that over the decade following the challenge, the Association had awarded almost one hundred and fifty-five million dollars in research grants across five hundred and sixty projects. Its network of ALS clinics had grown from roughly one hundred to more than two hundred and twenty.

Those grants were awarded over ten years; they were not all paid out of the original one hundred and fifteen million dollars. Later fundraising and other sources supported the larger effort. RTI also reported that grant recipients attracted substantial additional financing. That is a different kind of multiplication from a chain of videos: one research grant helping a laboratory qualify for the next.

There are limits to what these reports can prove. They document spending, infrastructure, and progress, but they can't recreate a world where the Ice Bucket Challenge never happened to measure every difference precisely. Even so, they show something the viral clips could not: the organization developed a much larger ability to fund research and support patients after that summer.

The easiest version of this story ends right there. A silly trend became serious money and serious science. But there is a harder and more revealing chapter. Because one of the highest-profile treatments backed by that momentum looked like a breakthrough—until it didn't.

In 2022, the FDA approved a new ALS treatment called Relyvrio. The ALS Association said it had invested two point two million dollars from Ice Bucket Challenge funds in its development and trials. After years of work, the news looked like an answer to what all those buckets had paid for.

But in 2024, the manufacturer withdrew Relyvrio from the market. A larger clinical trial had failed to show the expected benefit. The early promise hadn't held up when tested again. That isn't the ending anyone wanted, especially patients and families who need effective treatments now.

It is also not evidence that all the donations were wasted. Science is full of ideas that survive the early tests and fail the later ones. Discovering that a treatment doesn't work is painful, but it matters. It stops a false promise from being mistaken for progress. The money also supported other research, clinical networks, and care. The honest scoreboard includes advances and disappointments. ALS still has no cure.

And now the unexpected epilogue. The buckets didn't vanish forever. In the summer of 2026, former NFL running back Chris Johnson, who had disclosed his own ALS diagnosis, helped inspire a new revival. An earlier video made in his honor by a fan, Hunter Mecum, helped spark it. The ALS Association reported hundreds of fresh videos in a matter of days, with former NFL players and families participating.

That is not a second hundred-and-fifteen-million-dollar summer. The available evidence doesn't support calling it that. But notice what brought the challenge back: once again, an identifiable person living with ALS, a group of people who cared about him, and a chain of public invitations. The same human logic that carried it through Pelham, Yonkers, and Boston in 2014.

The original advocates never stopped caring when the trend cooled. Anthony Senerchia died in 2017. Pete Frates died in 2019. Pat Quinn died in 2020. Their families and supporters kept fundraising, organizing, and calling attention to the disease. In Yonkers, Pat's father continued an annual challenge in his son's memory.

Return to Fenway Park in August 2014. Pete can't deliver his challenge in his own voice, so a recording does it for him. His wife Julie pours the water. Around him are players, teammates, family, and friends. Across the internet, people who have never met Pete are about to repeat the same ridiculous act.

Most viral trends disappear when the joke gets old. This one left behind money, research networks, new knowledge, difficult lessons, and families who never stopped pushing. The buckets were never the point. They were how millions of strangers were persuaded to do something for people they'd never met.

A bucket lasts a few seconds. What people do after they put it down is the real story.
