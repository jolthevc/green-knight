# Internet Famous: scope, subject types and story engines

## Core proposition
Internet Famous is a study of **virality, fame, cultural contagion, social behavior and the internet's attention economy**. It is NOT restricted to famous people.

**When a person is central:** the person is the protagonist, the internet is the subject.
**When no person is central:** a meme, sound, product, challenge, platform feature, object, fandom or online moment is the protagonist, and the internet remains the subject.

The show should look at the human decisions, design choices, culture and distribution machinery that transformed something into a sensation. Episodes can explain explosive attention, lasting relevance, surprising failure or why a seemingly trivial format became part of everyday culture.

## Eight equally legitimate subject formats
1. **People and creator stories:** fame, identity, audience conversion, reinvention and costs.
2. **Memes and shared jokes:** where a joke begins, how it gets remixed and why its meaning changes.
3. **Viral videos, songs, photos and objects:** why something impossible-to-ignore gets repeatedly shared.
4. **Challenges and mass participation:** why millions choose to *do* rather than merely watch.
5. **Platforms and product formats:** Vine, short video, loops, recommendations, anonymity, duet/stitch and design effects.
6. **Online communities and fandoms:** collective rituals, insider language, moderation and identity.
7. **Games, sounds, filters and creative tools:** how affordances become mass culture.
8. **Internet-era social behaviors:** controversy cycles, apology formats, nostalgia waves, co-creation, trends and collective memory.

A film may mix formats, but identify ONE primary subject and ONE meaningful mechanism. Avoid redundant episodes that use different celebrities to explain the same shallow point.

## Four interchangeable narrative engines
- **The mystery:** The same image/sound was everywhere. Why this one, and why now?
- **The cascade:** Small event -> surprising amplifier -> scale -> feedback effects -> consequences.
- **The paradox:** The thing that made a phenomenon go viral was also its limitation.
- **The transformation:** A meme, behavior or format changed meaning as it spread across communities.

For person-led stories add lived consequences, agency and fairness. For phenomenon-led stories reconstruct origin, path of spread, evidence, different participants, competing explanations and what the pattern reveals. Never force a human celebrity arc onto a meme or an explainer into a gossip arc.

## Non-person pilot-grade candidates (not researched or commissioned)
- **The Dress:** Why a single image became a global disagreement about perception, certainty and social sharing.
- **Ice Bucket Challenge:** How a participation template turned an online challenge into a major charity phenomenon, with verified impact vs viral hype examined carefully.
- **Harlem Shake:** How a replicable video format spread faster than any one personality.
- **Rickrolling:** How a prank became a durable social ritual.
- **Doge:** How a meme changes meaning, community, ownership and value across different contexts.
- **TikTok sounds:** Why a short piece of audio can become a reusable template for millions of distinct videos.
- **The internet apology video:** How repeated social expectations create a recognizable genre, without targeting unrelated individuals.
- **Reaction GIFs and emotes:** How micro-expressions become an informal language.
- **Viral filters and challenges:** Why participating is psychologically different from watching.
- **Online fandom terminology:** How insiders create status through shared language.

Do not assume dates, exact metrics, first-mover origin, unique cause or wider consequences without research. The illustrative topics require primary-source evidence, mechanism differentiation and sufficiently engaging 15-minute material.

## Selection rule
**One familiar thing + one strange question + one sourced explanation + one bigger insight.**
A subject can be a person, an app, a meme, a clip, a phrase, a movement, a sound, a game or something else native to online culture.

## Examples of story framing
- Biography-only: “The Story of Rebecca Black” -> case study: “Why Millions Shared a Song They Said They Disliked.”
- Meme recap: “What Was the Harlem Shake?” -> case study: “How One Simple Format Took Over the Internet.”
- Trend summary: “The Dress Was Everywhere” -> case study: “Why One Photo Made the Entire Internet Disagree.”
- Platform history: “What Happened to Vine?” -> case study: “How an App Could Make Stars but Struggle to Keep Them.”

Those are **unresearched working angles**, not publication-ready claims.
