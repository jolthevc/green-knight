# Voice, sound and visual identity: Studio Confidential

## The sound of the channel
An articulate, captivating friend who is **obsessed with films and TV** and has done the serious research. Not a film-school professor, gossip commentator, accountant, dry news anchor or ominous trailer announcer.

**Emotional palette:** genuine movie-nerd delight, delightfully specific curiosity, nostalgia, suspense, wit, humane seriousness, measured awe and occasionally righteous disbelief when evidence warrants.

## Narration rules
- Love the storytelling craft, performances and cinematic moments. Speak with a sense of discovery, not professorial certainty.
- Warm, confident and conversational; sharp and energetic when a conflict unfolds; allow carefully earned silence.
- Explain industry vocabulary (development hell, greenlight, back end, syndication, streaming residuals) by showing what it meant to the people involved.
- Avoid constant gravitas, performative dramatic growling, mocking creators, "little did they know" clichés, relentless rhetorical questions and artificial urgency.
- Don't impersonate actors/directors or suggest unrecorded private conversations are archival.
- At least one earned moment of pure artistic appreciation, grounded in a real scene, character, image, design or performance.
- Natural narrator audition remains outstanding. No voice ID, provider or accent is approved.

## Visual principles
1. **Make the subject visible immediately.** Familiar film/TV imagery and real production evidence, appropriately sourced and cleared.
2. **Let archival evidence carry drama.** Real set photographs, storyboards, cast interviews, industry reports, actual production documents, dated articles, trade ads, recorded press appearances and images from relevant locations.
3. **Show craft where relevant.** Camera setups, edit comparisons, costume sketches, practical effects or behind-the-scenes images when provenance is reliable. Never fabricate what was photographed.
4. **Use selective, clean explanation.** Rights diagrams, timelines, box-office charts or distribution maps only when they genuinely clarify dramatic stakes. Keep the unit/context and source visible.
5. **Use footage intelligently.** A clip must have an original analytic/narrative purpose, not simply fill time or replace the script. Copyright claims and monetization restrictions remain possible even with commentary.
6. **No fake history.** AI-produced re-enactments, fake script pages, false studio memos or edited reaction shots can mislead. Synthetic illustrations must be transparently labeled and should be avoided for documentary evidence.
7. **Keep pace earned.** Vary images according to plot, tension and emotional register. No mechanical cut every two seconds, excessive animated captions or endlessly reused generic film reels.

## Rights and sourcing
- Default to owned/original graphics, properly licensed archival or stock, documented permissions and approved photos/interviews where feasible.
- Fair use is fact-specific and not established by merely adding a voiceover, shortening a clip or displaying copyright attribution.
- Films, TV episodes, posters, trailers, studio stills and soundtrack material require case-specific review. Publicly accessible does not mean freely reusable.
- Maintain an asset ledger: timestamp/paragraph -> asset description -> source URL/holder -> license or legal rationale -> review status -> permitted use/attribution -> fallback.
- Do not use famous score cues, unauthorized dialogue clips, copyrighted music or purported genuine on-set audio without a rights plan.
- Editorial requirements should fit the economical stock/archival-led outsourced production model; no assumption that a $60 editor can clear expensive Hollywood IP.

## Narrator audition segments
Ask the candidate narrator to read the **same** short original draft in three modes: playful Hollywood rivalry, hushed artistic appreciation, and a complicated studio decision explained in plain English. Compare natural voice, emotional range, precision, pronunciation, pacing and credibility. Owner must approve one before production.

## Final sound and visual review
Check footage corresponds to the correct film/season/version/creator, archive provenance is genuine, photos are not presented as firsthand footage from an unrelated set, onscreen economic figures are documented, spoken text matches approved script, and the story remains entertaining even when a film clip cannot be licensed.
