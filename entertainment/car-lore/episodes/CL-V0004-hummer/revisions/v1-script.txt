TITLE: How the Hummer Became the SUV America Loved to Hate

In early 2003, LeBron James was eighteen years old.

And for his birthday, his mother bought him a Hummer H2. Not an ordinary one, either. This thing had three televisions inside.

LeBron was still in high school. His new truck was so extravagant that Ohio athletics officials looked into whether it violated the rules for amateur athletes. He was ultimately cleared.

Now picture the vehicle at the center of all this.

A massive chrome grille. Windows like gun slits. Tires that look prepared to climb over a small country. And a silhouette so square that a ruler would feel intimidated.

It looked like something you'd send into a war zone. Yet here it was, carrying a teenage basketball phenomenon to school.

That same year, General Motors reportedly spent three hundred thousand dollars helping put Hummer H2s in a rap music video.

And before the decade was over, the brand had become a symbol of everything critics hated about American excess. Its sales collapsed. General Motors tried to sell the name. And the original Hummer business disappeared.

So how did a military vehicle become a celebrity must-have, a cultural villain, and then a badge that somehow came back as an electric truck?

The answer starts long before chrome wheels or red carpets.

It starts with a military requirement.

In the late nineteen-seventies and early eighties, the United States military needed a new generation of versatile light trucks. Something capable of moving people and equipment across terrain where an ordinary vehicle would surrender.

AM General eventually won a major Army production contract in 1983 for a vehicle with one of the least glamorous names imaginable.

The High Mobility Multipurpose Wheeled Vehicle.

H-M-M-W-V.

Fortunately, people called it the Humvee.

A Humvee wasn't designed to impress anyone at a nightclub. The requirements were about ground clearance, mobility, durability, and getting work done under difficult conditions.

You can see that purpose in its proportions. The body is brutally wide. The wheels are pushed toward the corners. The drivetrain and suspension are designed to keep vulnerable components farther away from rocks and rough ground.

The design included remarkable technology, such as a central tire inflation system on many versions, allowing the driver to adjust tire pressures for different surfaces.

And here's an enthusiast detail I absolutely love: portal-geared hubs helped give the original vehicle its extraordinary clearance without requiring a comically oversized wheel diameter.

The Humvee isn't one of those machines that's pretty despite its engineering. It looks the way it does because of its engineering.

That squat stance. That huge track width. The near-vertical windshield. The flat panels. The sense that there's no surface on the thing that cares what a fashion designer thinks.

It is fantastically ugly.

And somehow, completely magnificent.

But it wasn't a tank. The original Humvee was a utility vehicle, and military versions varied in armor and role. Its later vulnerabilities in combat are not something to gloss over.

Its celebrity, though, began with the images seen by the public during conflicts such as the Persian Gulf War in 1991. News coverage showed these strange, wide vehicles moving with military convoys across the desert.

And at least one man watching wanted one very badly.

Arnold Schwarzenegger.

Before the Gulf War had even finished, Schwarzenegger had reportedly been pushing AM General to sell him a civilian Humvee. A contemporary Los Angeles Times report described his persistence and eventual early ownership.

This was a man whose movie career had made him famous for playing a cyborg. Of course the vehicle he wanted for the road looked like a mechanized piece of the apocalypse.

AM General realized there might be a small civilian market for people who wanted serious off-road capability, a distinctive machine, or simply the most conspicuous vehicle imaginable.

So in 1992, the company introduced the civilian Hummer. Later we'd call this the H1, to distinguish it from the models that came afterward.

And this is important.

The H1 really did share its basic military-vehicle DNA with the Humvee. This wasn't a typical luxury SUV with a military costume glued to the outside.

You paid for that authenticity in many ways.

It was enormous outside, yet its interior could feel surprisingly divided up by the massive central drivetrain tunnel. Getting in and out wasn't elegant. The ride and noise were not something you'd confuse with a luxury sedan.

Parking one of these in a normal city space was the kind of activity best attempted with patience and a sympathetic audience.

Yet for the right buyer, none of those were defects. They were evidence.

This was the real thing.

An H1 had the mechanical attitude of a specialist off-road machine. It looked at a rut or muddy trail and seemed personally offended that anyone would call it an obstacle.

There is something irresistible about a vehicle designed around an extraordinary job that barely bothers to disguise it.

But the H1 had a serious business problem.

It was expensive, very thirsty, and deeply uncompromising. Only a relatively small number of buyers truly wanted to live with one.

GM saw a fascinating opportunity hiding inside that problem.

What if people wanted the Hummer feeling, without quite so much Hummer reality?

In December 1999, General Motors acquired rights to the Hummer name from AM General. AM General would continue to build certain vehicles, but GM could turn the brand into something bigger.

And in 2002, the Hummer H2 arrived.

Just look at this absurd machine.

The upright grille. The enormous front bumper. The exposed-looking details and vertical sides. The windows seem almost nervous about letting too much daylight in.

It's as if somebody drew the original Humvee from memory, made it more comfortable, and turned the volume knob all the way clockwise.

But underneath, the H2 wasn't simply a military Humvee with better seats.

It was a purpose-developed GM civilian truck based on components from the company's existing truck family, built by AM General under contract. It shared architecture with GM's large trucks rather than the H1's military platform.

And that's not automatically a criticism. It made the H2 easier to use on the road, to sell through dealerships, and to maintain like a conventional large American SUV.

It also made the basic promise accessible to a much broader group of buyers.

One period Car and Driver preview contrasted the roughly one-hundred-thousand-dollar H1 with the H2, which arrived at a price below fifty thousand dollars before options.

Still serious money. But suddenly, Hummer wasn't an exotic military oddity for a tiny group of eccentrics.

It was a luxury purchase someone might cross-shop against other expensive SUVs.

The irony is that you weren't really buying speed.

In a contemporary Car and Driver test, the H2 took ten point seven seconds to reach sixty miles an hour. And the test vehicle weighed around six thousand seven hundred pounds.

The magazine also recorded a stopping distance of two hundred and forty-four feet from seventy miles an hour.

None of those numbers screams precision sports car.

But this was never trying to be a sports car.

The H2 was about presence. About towering over surrounding traffic. About that wonderfully ridiculous sight of a bright yellow or black Hummer pulling up outside a perfectly ordinary restaurant.

And to its credit, the H2 wasn't merely a poseur. The same contemporary test took it off-road in Baja California and found that it retained genuine capability, though it demanded more care over obstacles than the H1.

This wasn't an incapable suburban prop. It had real four-wheel-drive equipment, big tires, useful clearance, and hardware meant for rough terrain.

The moment you stepped out, you were making an entrance. Sometimes it seemed like you were making an entrance just by being stuck in traffic.

What you could not do was fail to notice it.

And General Motors understood that perfectly.

In March 2003, the Los Angeles Times reported that GM had spent about three hundred thousand dollars to place the H2 prominently in a music video by rapper Ms. Jade.

The manufacturer didn't just hope celebrities would be photographed beside the thing. It was paying serious money to get the truck into pop culture.

Hummer wasn't simply advertising horsepower or towing capacity.

It was trying to become the sort of object people wanted to be seen with.

And the strategy landed in a culture already in love with enormous luxury SUVs, oversized wheels, booming sound systems, and glamour that did not believe in understatement.

The Hummer was perfect for that moment.

A Mercedes could signal luxury. A Porsche could signal taste and performance.

A Hummer signaled that subtlety had been cancelled.

Then there was LeBron's eighteenth birthday. Three televisions in one H2. State athletics officials examining whether a high-school athlete's new vehicle broke eligibility rules.

Celebrities and athletes were appearing with these monsters at events. The trucks showed up in music videos, on television, outside clubs, and in conversations about status itself.

An engineering machine had become a social signal.

But social signals have a nasty habit of working in both directions.

To people who loved the Hummer, it represented strength, freedom, fun, and a kind of gloriously unreasonable individuality.

To people who despised it, the exact same silhouette represented waste, fuel consumption, militarism, and a culture of taking up more space than you needed.

The H2 didn't have to be a technically terrible car for critics to hate what it symbolized. It just had to look like the most extreme expression of an argument about American consumption.

In 2003, some Hummer dealerships and SUVs were vandalized and set on fire in California by extremists claiming environmental motives. Those attacks were criminal and dangerous, not a defensible form of criticism.

At the same time, ordinary public debate over fuel economy and the size of American vehicles was growing louder.

Period road testers commonly saw fuel consumption around ten miles per gallon in the H2. In an era of concern about gasoline prices and emissions, that was a difficult number to make charming.

The Hummer was one of the rare vehicles where people could form a fierce opinion about you before they'd even met you.

As the decade went on, GM tried expanding the idea. The smaller, less expensive H3 arrived, making Hummer available to more buyers.

And for a brief period, it worked.

The Hummer brand recorded more than seventy-one thousand American sales in 2006. But most of those sales were the newer H3. The bigger H2 had already begun to fade from its earlier high.

That's an important distinction. The enormous H2 created the cultural spectacle. The smaller H3 helped the brand reach its sales peak.

But the tide was turning.

Fuel prices climbed. The American economy weakened. More buyers questioned the wisdom of driving large, expensive trucks that demanded frequent visits to the pump.

In a 2008 report, the Los Angeles Times described the H2 as a lightning rod for the idea of excess. That same year, GM publicly considered selling or radically changing the Hummer business.

And in 2009, as General Motors reorganized through bankruptcy, Hummer was one of the brands slated for disposal.

The company found a potential buyer: China's Sichuan Tengzhong Heavy Industrial Machines.

For a moment, it looked as though the story might continue under new ownership.

Then that deal failed.

On February twenty-fourth, 2010, General Motors announced the acquisition could not be completed and that it would wind down Hummer operations.

This wasn't the end of military Humvees, which were a separate AM General business. And it wasn't proof that every original Hummer owner had stopped loving their trucks.

It was the end of the GM-era Hummer brand as a standalone civilian business.

You still see H1s at off-road gatherings, H2s on enormous wheels, restored originals, and owners delighted to explain every bizarre detail of their machine.

The H1 had extraordinary mechanical purpose. The H2 turned that look into a form of automotive theater.

Which is why the last twist is almost too ridiculous to be real.

In 2020, GM announced Hummer was coming back.

Not as a gasoline-guzzling luxury SUV brand.

As a GMC electric supertruck.

And if you're expecting the new Hummer to have learned moderation, I have disappointing news.

The first electric Hummer pickups were capable of roughly one thousand horsepower in their highest-output configurations. GM advertised acceleration from zero to sixty in around three seconds.

Yes. Three seconds.

This enormous battery-powered machine could launch like a supercar, with available four-wheel steering that allowed it to move diagonally at low speeds. GM called that feature CrabWalk.

And the 2022 Edition One weighed roughly nine thousand pounds, depending on the published specification.

Nine thousand pounds.

It's electric. It has zero tailpipe emissions. But it's still an enormous, resource-intensive machine, and electric doesn't automatically mean small or efficient.

Which makes the reincarnation strangely perfect.

GM didn't revive the Hummer by making it a restrained little commuter car. It revived the Hummer by making it the most outrageous version of itself that new technology allowed.

The Humvee began with a job to do. The H1 made that military-derived engineering available to civilians. The H2 transformed the look into a celebrity accessory and a cultural argument.

The original brand collapsed when the market and the moment changed.

But the essential idea survived.

Not that a Hummer is sensible. Or efficient. Or something everybody ought to own.

It's that sometimes a vehicle becomes famous because it refuses to be ordinary.

And whether you love the Hummer or can't stand the sight of one, I'm willing to bet you have never mistaken it for anything else.

That's quite an achievement for a car that started with a military acronym nobody could pronounce.
