TITLE: How a Tractor Maker Challenged Ferrari

The most famous rivalry in supercar history supposedly began with one extremely annoyed customer.

The customer was a wealthy Italian tractor manufacturer. His Ferrari was giving him trouble. So he went to the great Enzo Ferrari to complain. And, according to the legend, Ferrari basically told him to stick to tractors.

The tractor manufacturer was Ferruccio Lamborghini.

It's the perfect origin story. One insult. One bruised ego. And two of the most beautiful badges ever put on a car, destined to hate each other forever.

There's just one little problem. People who actually worked at Lamborghini disagreed about whether the famous confrontation happened the way it's told.

But the real story is better. Because Ferruccio didn't just decide to make a Ferrari with different badges. He built a whole new car company, hired engineers from Ferrari's world, and accidentally helped unleash a machine he hadn't originally wanted at all.

And when you see what they created, it's almost impossible not to fall in love with cars.

First, we need to appreciate the man Ferruccio was daring to challenge.

Enzo Ferrari wasn't just another Italian businessman with a factory. Ferrari had grown out of racing. The team's roots went back to 1929, and the first car bearing Ferrari's own marque arrived in 1947.

Competition was baked into the company's identity. The red cars had history, prestige, and victories behind them. A Ferrari wasn't just a fast machine. It was a connection to the most glamorous, dangerous end of motorsport.

And the road cars? Those were extraordinary, too. Long hoods, twelve-cylinder engines, interiors that smelled of leather and expensive decisions. Beautiful Italian machines for people who could afford to buy a piece of the racing legend.

Let's be clear: Ferruccio Lamborghini loved cars like that. He didn't enter this story as some outsider who thought sports cars were silly. He was a genuine enthusiast.

The difference was that he had spent his career building machinery for people who expected it to work.

Ferruccio had grown up in a farming family and trained as a mechanic. During the war he learned to keep machines running with the parts he could find. Afterward, in a country rebuilding itself, he saw a business opportunity in turning surplus military equipment into useful farm machinery.

Those early tractors weren't glamorous. They were valuable. Farmers needed them. And Ferruccio knew how to make mechanical things both practical and desirable.

His tractor business prospered. He moved into heating and air-conditioning equipment. By the early sixties, he had the sort of money that lets a car enthusiast develop very expensive tastes.

Ferraris. Maseratis. Jaguars. Mercedes. He tried the best the grand-touring world could offer.

And this is where the trouble started.

Ferruccio wasn't impressed by everything that came with the prestige. He complained that the cars could be uncomfortable, too hot, poorly finished, or troublesome to use. They were spectacular when they worked. But he wanted something that worked beautifully all the time.

And yes, there was the famous Ferrari clutch story.

In versions told over the years, Ferruccio complained about clutch trouble, confronted Enzo, and received a cutting remark about his tractor-building credentials. Former Lamborghini test driver Valentino Balboni said he heard the story from Ferruccio himself. Another early test driver, Bob Wallace, questioned it.

So I won't pretend we know the exact conversation, or that one alleged insult single-handedly created an automobile company.

What we do know is that Ferruccio became convinced he could build a better kind of grand tourer. One with exotic performance, but also comfort, refinement, and attention to the customer.

Which, honestly, is an even bigger act of confidence than getting angry about a clutch.

Imagine looking at the automotive world, seeing Ferrari, and thinking: lovely cars, but I can improve on this.

In 1963, Ferruccio formed Automobili Lamborghini in Sant'Agata Bolognese. Not on the other side of the world. In the very region where Italy's performance-car talent was concentrated.

And then he did something deliciously provocative.

He hired Giotto Bizzarrini, an engineer who had worked at Ferrari, to design his new company's twelve-cylinder engine.

Think about the statement that makes. You're challenging the most famous V12 sports-car company in Italy, and one of your first moves is to recruit someone who knows that territory intimately.

The brief wasn't timid. Ferruccio wanted a proper twelve-cylinder engine, the sort of mechanical centerpiece that could make the new badge worthy of comparison.

The prototype was called the 350 GTV. Franco Scaglione designed its distinctive body, and it appeared at the Turin Motor Show in October 1963.

It looked like a serious attempt to enter an impossibly exclusive club. But starting a car company isn't the same as designing a gorgeous concept. The original prototype needed significant work before it could become a usable production car.

Engineers Gian Paolo Dallara and Paolo Stanzani helped turn that ambition into something roadworthy. Carrozzeria Touring refined the styling. The highly strung V12 was adapted into a more civilised road engine.

And in 1964, the 350 GT arrived.

Look at this car for a second. It isn't shouting for attention. The proportions are long, low, and elegant. The roof flows into the rear quarters. The front-mounted V12 sits under an impossibly graceful hood.

It's a gentleman's grand tourer with a mechanic's brain.

That was Ferruccio's original idea. Not to out-race Ferrari at Le Mans. Not to build a stripped-out competition machine. To create something an owner could take on a long journey, enjoy, and trust.

Lamborghini even promoted a customer-service philosophy more attentive than the image people often associated with exotic Italian cars.

And the remarkable thing is that, for a brand-new manufacturer, the 350 GT was real. This wasn't a drawing on a wall. It was a production V12 sports car made by a company that hadn't existed a few years earlier.

But if you think that's the car that made Lamborghini a legend, you haven't seen what the engineers were cooking.

This is my favorite twist in the entire story.

Ferruccio didn't want to build his company around racing. Enzo Ferrari was a racing man. Ferruccio wanted the ultimate road car.

His young engineers, however, were interested in something much more radical.

Dallara, Stanzani, and New Zealand test driver Bob Wallace imagined a machine that borrowed its layout and engineering drama from motorsport. Something with the engine behind the driver, not stretched out under a conventional long hood.

Today, when you think of a supercar, a mid-mounted engine feels normal. In the mid-sixties, it was still an audacious idea for a glamorous road-going twelve-cylinder machine.

Putting the engine behind the seats could concentrate major mass closer to the center of the car, making its proportions and handling very different. But fitting a giant V12 there creates another problem: where does everything else go?

The answer was gloriously strange.

The young team placed the four-liter V12 sideways across the car, just behind the cockpit, combining it tightly with the transmission. It was compact, technically daring, and full of problems an ordinary GT designer would prefer not to have.

Ferruccio was skeptical at first. But the engineers persuaded him to let the idea move forward.

And in November 1965, Lamborghini unveiled something remarkable at the Turin Motor Show.

Not a finished sports car.

A chassis.

A low, black metal skeleton, pierced with holes to save weight. Four white exhaust pipes. And, behind the space where the seats would go, a V12 with twelve vertical intake trumpets standing up like the pipes of an organ.

Imagine walking through a motor show filled with polished paint and chrome, then stopping in front of this bare, almost indecently exposed piece of engineering.

It told you everything and nothing. You could see exactly how extreme the new machine was. You had absolutely no idea how beautiful its body might become.

And that's when Nuccio Bertone entered the story.

His design house agreed to create the body. Marcello Gandini, then working at Bertone, gave the radical chassis its astonishing shape.

In March 1966, at Geneva, the finished car appeared.

The Lamborghini Miura.

Oh, come on. Look at this thing.

The nose is impossibly low. The headlights are framed by those wonderful dark eyelashes. The roofline barely seems high enough to accommodate actual human beings. The rear haunches swell over the wheels like the car is gathering itself for a leap.

And unlike the elegant 350 GT, the Miura's great V12 doesn't live under the front hood. It's right behind the passengers.

That means the car can look almost impossibly compact, with a short nose, a dramatic cabin, and this extraordinary sense that the whole machine is wrapped tightly around its engine.

You can explain the layout with an engineering diagram. But the shape tells the story better than any diagram ever could.

This is the difference between seeing a fast car and seeing an object that rearranges your imagination.

Even the name was perfect. Miura came from a famous breed of Spanish fighting bulls. That bull identity would become inseparable from Lamborghini.

Ferrari had the prancing horse.

Now it had the raging bull to contend with.

And remember the original ambition: a refined GT that would address Ferrari's shortcomings.

The Miura went beyond that. It was emotional theater made out of advanced engineering.

Its early four-liter V12 produced about 350 metric horsepower in the original P400. It was breathtakingly fast for its time, and the combination of mid-engine layout and exotic styling helped define what people would come to call a supercar.

Not every historian agrees about which machine was the first supercar. That's not a title you can settle with a stopwatch.

But the Miura undeniably helped establish the template that generations of exotic cars would follow.

And there is something wonderfully ironic about it.

Ferruccio founded a company partly because he wanted a more usable alternative to Ferrari. His team became famous by creating one of the least ordinary road cars in the world.

The Miura had compromises. Its transverse engine and gearbox arrangement created technical challenges, and early examples required development and care. This was no effortless, perfect machine.

But perfection isn't really the word that comes to mind when you see one.

It's desire.

The Miura became an object of fascination for wealthy buyers, performers, filmmakers, and enthusiasts. The car appeared in the opening of the 1969 film The Italian Job, cementing its place in automotive cinema.

You can see the entire rivalry changing shape.

Ferrari's prestige had been earned in racing and reinforced on the road. Lamborghini was finding a different route to the same impossible dream-car territory: visual audacity, mechanical spectacle, and the pure emotional punch of seeing something no one else had built.

And then, just as the world was adjusting to the Miura, Lamborghini did it again.

At the 1971 Geneva Motor Show, the company revealed the Countach LP500 prototype.

Forget elegant curves for a moment.

The Countach looked like someone had taken a ruler to a spaceship. Sharp edges. A windshield that seemed to melt into the roof. A wedge shape so extreme it made other exotic cars look almost apologetic.

It was designed by Gandini at Bertone, the same extraordinary design world that helped create the Miura.

The name itself came from a Piedmontese expression of amazement. Which seems about right.

Because if you had grown up with the Miura and then saw a Countach, the natural reaction was probably a noise that didn't qualify as an actual word.

Here was the next phase of Lamborghini's identity: not simply competing with Ferrari's established image, but creating its own language for the exotic car.

And this is where you'd expect the founder to stand in triumph. Ferrari had his racing victories. Ferruccio had a new generation of bedroom-wall machines.

Except that's not how this story ends.

The 1970s brought a harsh economic change. Trouble in Ferruccio's tractor business was compounded by a cancelled large order from Bolivia and financing pressure. Labor disputes created additional strain.

The car business also faced growing costs, demanding regulations, and a market that was anything but guaranteed for expensive exotic machines.

In 1972, Ferruccio sold a majority stake in Automobili Lamborghini to Georges-Henri Rossetti.

In 1973, he sold his tractor business. By 1974, he had withdrawn from the automotive company altogether.

The Countach entered production under new ownership in 1974.

Just think about that.

Ferruccio had started the carmaker in 1963, certain he could build something better than the exotic cars he owned.

In a little over a decade, the company had made the 350 GT, unleashed the Miura, and revealed the Countach.

And the man who started all of it was no longer there to run the next chapter.

He didn't get an uncomplicated fairy-tale victory over Ferrari. He left amid serious commercial pressures, and the company endured years of ownership changes and financial trouble.

But he had done something much more interesting than merely annoy a famous rival.

He had made Lamborghini matter.

Imagine the automotive world without that decision. No Miura that made every car designer rethink what a road-going exotic could look like. No Countach turning walls into shrines for twelve-cylinder machines.

And no raging bull sitting opposite the prancing horse as one of the most instantly recognizable badges on Earth.

The rivalry is often told as a feud between two impossible Italian personalities. There is truth in the difference between those men, but the exact insult that supposedly started everything remains contested.

And honestly, I don't think the feud needs it.

Enzo Ferrari built a legend from racing.

Ferruccio Lamborghini looked at the road car itself and asked whether the experience could be better.

Then a group of astonishingly talented engineers and designers pushed that question so far that it produced the Miura and, eventually, the Countach.

Two completely different visions. Two badges. And some of the most outrageous cars humans have ever built.

If you want to call that rivalry, fine.

I call it one of the greatest things ever to happen to people who love cars.
