TITLE: The Tractor Maker Who Took On Ferrari

In 1965, the Italian car industry was supposed to be putting on a show. Shining paint. Expensive suits. Beautiful machines competing for attention.

Then a company that had been building sports cars for barely two years turned up with this.

No bodywork. No doors. Not even a windshield.

Just a black chassis, four wheels, and an enormous twelve-cylinder engine sitting sideways behind the seats. Twelve intake trumpets poked up into the air. Four white exhaust pipes reached out from the back. It looked like someone had wheeled the mechanical skeleton of a race car into a luxury-car showroom.

A few months later, that skeleton would become the Lamborghini Miura, one of the most extraordinary shapes ever put on a road.

And the entire company existed because a man who had made his fortune building tractors believed he could do better than Ferrari.

But there's a twist. By the time Lamborghini's other great poster car, the Countach, entered production, that man had already left the company.

So how did a tractor maker challenge Ferrari, help redefine what a supercar looked like, and then walk away from his own creation?

The familiar answer starts with one insult.

You probably know the story. Ferruccio Lamborghini owns a Ferrari. The clutch keeps giving him grief. He goes to Enzo Ferrari to complain, and Ferrari tells him, more or less, to stick to tractors.

It's perfect. A wounded Italian ego. A very expensive grudge. A new supercar company born out of pure spite.

And it may not have happened quite that way.

Former Lamborghini test driver Valentino Balboni said he heard Ferruccio recount the clash. Another early test driver, Bob Wallace, disputed the story. Decades later, the precise words remain impossible to establish with confidence.

But the feud makes more sense when you look at what these two men wanted from cars.

Because Ferruccio wasn't some farmer who had never seen a sports car and decided to show Ferrari who's boss.

He loved the things.

By the early nineteen-sixties, he'd owned expensive grand tourers from several famous makers. He had the money, the taste, and the mechanical knowledge to be a very demanding customer.

Enzo Ferrari, meanwhile, had built his world around motorsport. Scuderia Ferrari dated back to 1929. The cars wearing his own badge arrived after the war, with racing prestige at the center of their identity.

And honestly, what prestige. The long-nosed V12s. The Italian coachwork. The unmistakable red. Ferrari could sell you a machine that felt connected to the most glamorous form of mechanical combat on earth.

Ferruccio wasn't immune to any of that. He simply thought paying a fortune should buy you more than glamour and a badge.

His complaint was not that a Ferrari wasn't special.

It was that something so special could still be uncomfortable, troublesome, or imperfectly finished.

He wanted the performance and theater, but in a car he could actually enjoy traveling in.

That sounds fairly reasonable. Until you hear his proposed solution.

Build my own.

Where did he get that confidence?

Before sports cars, Ferruccio was an engineer and entrepreneur. Born into an Italian farming family, he'd developed a knack for making mechanical things work. During the Second World War he serviced equipment. After the war he turned that knowledge toward the enormous demand for agricultural machinery.

His tractors helped build a thriving business. He expanded into heating and air-conditioning equipment. By the nineteen-sixties, he'd made the kind of money that can support an extremely extravagant hobby.

But tractors leave fingerprints on the way you think.

A farmer doesn't admire an engine because its valve covers look beautiful. They care whether the thing starts on Monday morning and keeps working when it's needed.

Ferruccio carried some of that attitude into a world full of temperamental, hand-built grand tourers.

He didn't want a racing team with a sideline in road cars. He wanted a grand tourer that would make the Ferrari owner wonder what, exactly, they'd been putting up with.

In 1963, he founded Automobili Lamborghini in Sant'Agata Bolognese, deep in Italy's performance-car heartland.

And right away, he went shopping for talent.

For the engine, he commissioned Giotto Bizzarrini, a gifted engineer who had worked at Ferrari, to produce the sort of V12 that could look Ferrari's best straight in the eye.

Think about that opening move.

You decide to challenge the world's most famous Italian V12 sports-car manufacturer, and then you bring in one of the men who knows how its engineering world works.

This wasn't a joke with a tractor badge. It was an enormous, expensive technical gamble.

The first prototype was the 350 GTV. It appeared at the Turin Motor Show in 1963, wearing an angular Franco Scaglione body.

But here's a deliciously imperfect detail about the birth of a company devoted to getting things right.

The prototype wasn't yet a functioning production-ready car. Accounts of the Turin display describe the ambitious V12 being shown separately because the engine and body weren't fully sorted out for the show.

You can almost hear the awkward explanation: we've built a beautiful twelve-cylinder sports-car prototype. At the moment, some assembly is still very much required.

That is the difference between designing a dream and manufacturing one.

Bizzarrini's racing-minded V12 needed taming for road use. The car needed extensive engineering and body revisions. Gian Paolo Dallara and Paolo Stanzani helped turn the prototype into something customers could buy. Touring refined the bodywork.

And in 1964, Lamborghini presented the 350 GT.

Look at it. Low, long, almost impossibly elegant. That grand front-engine shape. Those slim pillars. The sense of a car designed to travel a long distance very quickly and arrive without looking as though it's broken a sweat.

The 350 GT's V12 produced about two hundred and seventy metric horsepower in Lamborghini's official specification. More importantly, it was an actual production grand tourer.

Ferruccio had gone from critic of exotic cars to maker of one in roughly a year.

And his company was serious about customers. Contemporary recollections describe Lamborghini sending mechanics out, sometimes even by plane, to look after an owner whose car had problems.

I love that detail. You've built a glamorous Italian V12, and your customer-service philosophy sounds like it came from a man who sold farm equipment.

The 350 GT proved the bull belonged in the room.

But it wasn't yet the car that would change the room.

For that, we need to go back to that bizarre chassis in Turin.

Ferruccio didn't set up Lamborghini to go racing. But Dallara, Stanzani, and New Zealand test driver Bob Wallace were interested in what happened when you borrowed the logic of racing and applied it to a road car.

Their idea was radical for a luxurious twelve-cylinder machine: move the engine from the long hood to a position just behind the driver.

Simple to say. An absolute nightmare to package.

A V12 is a long piece of machinery. Put it lengthwise behind two seats and suddenly you need an extremely long rear section. Put it sideways, and you've got a different collection of headaches.

The engineers went with sideways.

Four liters. Twelve cylinders. The transmission packed tightly alongside the engine. A purpose-built chassis to hold it all together.

It created the foundations for a car that wouldn't look like a conventional grand tourer at all.

Ferruccio gave the project the green light. This wasn't a secret mutiny in the factory, whatever a dramatic retelling might suggest.

But the idea had come from people who wanted to push the company's new reputation far beyond elegant front-engined cars.

Which brings us to the 1965 Turin Motor Show.

The team showed the chassis without a body. Satin black metal, drilled through with circles to save weight. Exposed suspension. Wire wheels. That enormous transverse V12, all its mechanical intentions impossible to hide.

This is one of my favorite decisions in car-show history.

Normally, a carmaker tries to conceal the awkward machinery under the most seductive possible shape.

Lamborghini did the opposite.

They gave the crowd the machinery first. Almost a dare to imagine what might go on top.

The chassis caught the attention of Italian design houses. Nuccio Bertone's company got involved, and Marcello Gandini created the body that would make the whole project unforgettable.

In March 1966, the completed car arrived at Geneva.

The Miura.

All right. Forget the backstory for a second. Just look at this thing.

The nose is so low it seems to skim the ground. The headlights have those gloriously strange black eyelashes. The cabin is pushed forward, and the rear shoulders swell like the car is tensing a muscle.

And then there's the shape of the whole thing. Nothing like the long-hooded 350 GT. Nothing like the usual idea of an exotic road car.

It's a car wrapped tightly around the fact that the engine is right behind your shoulders.

That one layout decision changed what a dream machine could look like.

And if you could hear a real Miura V12 at work, that would tell the story in a language a specification sheet never could.

The original P400 had around three hundred and fifty metric horsepower. But the number isn't what makes people stare at a Miura fifty or sixty years later.

It's the realization that real people actually built something this outrageous.

Ferruccio's company had spent its first few years proving it could make a civilized V12 grand tourer.

Then it presented a mid-engined sculpture with twelve cylinders behind the seats and a face like nothing else on earth.

No wonder the Miura helped establish the road-going supercar as we now imagine it.

Was it perfect? Not remotely.

The tightly packaged powertrain was mechanically demanding. Early cars had compromises and needed development. A beautiful layout isn't automatically a comfortable one.

Which makes the irony delightful.

Lamborghini began as a quest for a less troublesome alternative to the exotic grand tourer.

It became famous for an astonishing machine that was every bit as temperamental and dramatic as its looks suggested.

And people absolutely adored it.

The Miura wasn't just admired by car reviewers. It became a cultural object, appearing in the opening of the 1969 film The Italian Job, and attracting musicians, actors, collectors, and people who had probably never given a moment's thought to chassis design.

For the first time, Lamborghini had given the world something Ferrari's racing pedigree couldn't simply substitute for: this specific feeling, this specific silhouette, this particular mechanical audacity.

The bull had found its own personality.

You might think that was the end of the escalation.

It wasn't even close.

In 1971, Lamborghini returned to Geneva with the Countach LP500 prototype.

And if the Miura was a gorgeous piece of automotive sculpture, the Countach looked like the drawing of a car from a future that hadn't happened yet.

A vicious wedge. Sharp edges where the Miura had curves. A windshield almost disappearing into the roofline. Doors that opened upward, because apparently opening sideways was now much too ordinary.

Again, the design came from Marcello Gandini and Bertone.

Even the name reportedly came from an exclamation of astonishment in the Piedmontese dialect. Which feels perfectly appropriate.

Try to imagine seeing one in 1971 without knowing what it was. You would probably forget whatever car you'd come to the show to look at.

The Countach didn't just continue the Miura's design language. It threw the whole vocabulary out and wrote its own.

And the great joke is that Ferrari's tractor-building challenger had given us not one but two completely different visions of automotive insanity.

This was the moment Ferruccio should have been standing at the factory gates, smiling at the future of the company he created.

Except his own future was changing.

The early seventies were tough years for manufacturers selling expensive, low-volume machines. New regulations increased complexity. The U.S. market was difficult. Production programs cost money long before the finished cars brought it back.

Outside the car factory, trouble was brewing in the business that had made Ferruccio rich.

A major tractor order for Bolivia fell through, leaving his tractor business with inventory and financial strain. Labor problems and the broader economic environment added pressure.

The man who had built an automotive brand with his own money was discovering that building incredible cars and running a healthy industrial business are not always the same skill.

In 1972, he sold a majority stake in Automobili Lamborghini.

He sold the tractor business the following year. And by 1974, he had left the car company entirely.

That's the twist nobody puts on the poster.

The tractor manufacturer who challenged Ferrari helped create the Miura and showed the world the Countach, but he didn't remain to lead Lamborghini into the Countach's production years.

The LP400 began production in 1974, after Ferruccio's departure.

And the company he founded did not enjoy an easy, uninterrupted triumph. It went through financial trouble and changing owners before eventually securing a much stronger footing decades later.

Ferruccio's original dream of a perfectly refined grand tourer had somehow produced a badge that came to represent the most extroverted, improbable, outrageous road cars imaginable.

And now that badge had a life of its own.

There is one more thing I love about this story.

The famous line about Ferrari telling Ferruccio to stick to tractors might be true in some form. Or it might be a story that became more satisfying each time someone repeated it.

But the cars don't need the insult.

The 350 GT was proof a new manufacturer could build a worthy Italian V12 grand tourer. The Miura was proof that a radically different layout could create an entirely new kind of beauty. And the Countach proved Lamborghini wasn't going to settle for one hit.

The battle was no longer just Ferrari versus Lamborghini.

It was Ferrari's deep racing identity against a rival that was inventing its own idea of automotive theater.

And in the end, nobody who loves cars really needs either side to lose.

Because without the prancing horse, there might never have been the same challenge.

And without the raging bull, the world would be missing two of the greatest shapes ever built.

Ferruccio set out to make a better grand tourer.

What he helped create was something far less sensible.

And thank goodness for that.
