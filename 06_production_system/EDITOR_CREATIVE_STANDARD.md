# Editor Creative Standard

**Status:** Canonical portfolio-wide production standard  
**Purpose:** Define what Green Knight entrusts to human editors, where creative judgment is expected, where editorial intent is fixed, and what quality threshold every finished video must clear.

---

## 1. Core Principle

Green Knight does not hire editors to function as mechanical assemblers.

Editors are expected to bring:

- visual taste
- production judgment
- pacing judgment
- design judgment
- problem-solving
- creative ideas
- knowledge of what looks good on YouTube

At the same time, editors are not expected to rediscover the video's thesis, rewrite the story, or invent the channel identity.

The intended relationship is:

> Green Knight defines the product. The editor helps make that product excellent.

A production packet is therefore a strong creative brief, not a prison.

The editor should understand what is fixed, what is preferred, and what is open to interpretation.

---

## 2. Division of Responsibility

### Green Knight owns

Green Knight is responsible for:

- channel strategy
- audience definition
- video selection
- thesis
- research
- factual claims
- story architecture
- script
- packaging strategy
- visual identity
- brand standards
- important graphic requirements
- final editorial approval

### Editor owns

The editor is responsible for:

- sourcing strong usable visuals
- selecting among visual options
- visual assembly
- narration generation and delivery using the locked channel voice when assigned
- pacing
- rhythm
- transitions
- motion
- music
- sound design
- graphic execution
- typography execution
- visual continuity
- production problem-solving
- proposing better visual treatments
- thumbnail execution when assigned
- first cut
- revisions
- final production delivery

The editor should improve execution when they see a better way to accomplish the intended objective.

---

## 3. Creative Freedom Levels

The production packet should distinguish among three levels of direction.

### REQUIRED

The visual or treatment is important to factual meaning, story logic, brand consistency, or a deliberate reveal.

The editor should not materially change it without flagging the issue.

Examples:

- a specific chart must show the correct data
- a map must establish a particular geographic relationship
- a document needs to be shown because it is evidence
- a reveal depends on timing
- a visual must not imply something the narration does not claim

### PREFERRED

Green Knight has a strong suggested treatment, but the editor may improve it.

Examples:

- suggested archival clip
- suggested transition
- recommended visual metaphor
- proposed chart treatment
- preferred montage structure

If the editor has a clearly better solution, they should use judgment.

### OPEN

The editor has broad discretion.

Examples:

- secondary B-roll
- scene texture
- transitions
- minor motion
- montage composition
- pacing within a section
- music selection within brand rules
- stylistic flourishes

The production packet should not pretend to know better than a good editor about every second of the video.

---

## 4. The Quality Bar

Every Green Knight video should feel:

- intentional
- coherent
- modern
- visually literate
- trustworthy
- polished
- appropriately dynamic
- native to its channel
- better than generic outsourced faceless content

The viewer should not think:

> This was assembled cheaply from random internet assets.

The efficiency of Green Knight's operating system should be invisible to the viewer.

---

## 5. The Anti-Slop Rule

Green Knight explicitly rejects the visual language associated with low-quality automated or outsourced video.

Avoid:

- generic stock used merely because a noun was mentioned
- endless corporate-office footage
- random handshake clips
- meaningless drone footage
- obvious filler
- cheap slideshow pacing
- templated motion graphics repeated without purpose
- excessive zooms and transitions
- visually unrelated footage
- AI imagery inserted simply because it is easy
- uncanny or low-quality generated humans
- cluttered thumbnails
- unconsidered fonts
- inconsistent graphic styles
- obvious template packs that overpower the brand
- visuals that technically match a word but add no understanding

The editor should ask:

> What is this visual doing for the viewer?

A useful visual should generally do at least one of the following:

- clarify
- prove
- orient
- explain
- establish scale
- establish place
- establish emotion
- create contrast
- reveal information
- maintain intentional visual momentum

If it does none of these, it may be filler.

---

## 6. Stock Footage Standard

Stock footage is allowed.

Generic stock thinking is not.

Strong stock usage:

- depicts the actual environment or mechanism being discussed
- establishes scale or setting
- gives texture to a real process
- supports an emotional beat
- illustrates a concept naturally
- works as part of a considered montage

Weak stock usage:

Narration: "Companies wanted to grow."

Visual: generic people shaking hands.

Narration: "Money was pouring in."

Visual: anonymous hands counting cash.

Narration: "The business faced pressure."

Visual: stressed person at laptop.

These substitutions make the product feel cheap because they illustrate vocabulary rather than meaning.

When strong real footage exists, prefer it.

---

## 7. Real Evidence and Specificity

Specific visuals are generally more valuable than generic visuals.

Prefer, where appropriate:

- the actual company
- the actual product
- the actual building
- the actual location
- the actual interface
- the actual document
- the actual person
- the actual historical image
- the actual chart
- the actual headline
- the actual process

Specificity increases trust and makes the video feel researched.

Use illustrative footage when specific evidence is unavailable or when illustration is the better storytelling tool.

---

## 8. Visual Variety

A premium faceless video should not depend on one visual source class.

A strong edit may mix:

- archival footage
- contemporary footage
- still photography
- screenshots
- maps
- charts
- diagrams
- product imagery
- documents
- headlines
- typography
- motion graphics
- tasteful stock
- editorial illustration
- selective AI-generated visuals

The mix should serve the story.

Do not force variety merely to hit a quota.

---

## 9. AI-Generated Visuals

AI-generated visuals are permitted when they improve the product.

They are not the default substitute for real visual research.

### Strong uses

Examples:

- editorial illustration
- conceptual imagery
- stylized scenes
- hard-to-source illustrative moments
- visual metaphors
- controlled historical reconstruction when clearly presented as illustrative
- background or compositional elements
- thumbnail ideation and selective thumbnail execution
- diagrams or stylized explanatory assets

### Weak uses

Avoid:

- fake documentary evidence
- generated imagery that viewers may reasonably interpret as authentic footage
- fake photographs of real events without clear context
- inaccurate recreations presented as fact
- uncanny faces
- low-detail generated filler
- inconsistent generated art styles within one video
- replacing readily available real assets with inferior generated versions

### Standard

AI should be used because it creates a better visual, not merely because it is faster.

If a generated image lowers perceived trust or quality, do not use it.

---

## 10. Graphics

Graphics should communicate.

They should not exist simply to make the edit feel busy.

A good chart or diagram should make the idea easier to understand than narration alone.

Graphics should have:

- clear hierarchy
- readable labels
- correct numbers
- appropriate units
- simple visual logic
- channel-consistent treatment

Avoid:

- tiny labels
- dense dashboards
- excessive animation
- decorative charts with no insight
- presenting uncertain estimates as precise measurements
- visual complexity that exceeds the sophistication of the underlying point

---

## 11. Motion and Transitions

Motion should feel purposeful.

Use movement to:

- direct attention
- reveal information
- create energy
- connect scenes
- establish spatial relationships
- support rhythm

Avoid movement that exists only to demonstrate editing.

Green Knight should generally prefer confident, clean motion over constant visual gimmicks.

The exact level of energy remains channel-specific.

---

## 12. Pacing

The editor is expected to exercise substantial judgment over pacing.

Pacing is not equivalent to "cut faster."

A good editor should know when to:

- cut
- hold
- accelerate
- pause
- allow an image to register
- let a chart breathe
- create a montage
- use silence
- simplify a visually dense passage

For synthetic-narration channels, narration delivery is part of pacing judgment. The editor may regenerate approved passages, create pauses, and adjust delivery against picture while preserving the approved wording and channel narrator identity.

The appropriate pacing should reflect:

- channel identity
- audience
- narration density
- emotional beat
- story structure

Do not enforce arbitrary rules such as changing the visual every two or three seconds.

---

## 13. Music and Sound

Music and sound should support the viewing experience rather than announce themselves constantly.

Use sound to:

- create tone
- build momentum
- support transitions
- emphasize reveals
- create contrast
- improve immersion

Avoid:

- generic inspirational music by default
- music too loud under narration
- constant sound effects
- comic sound effects in serious channels unless deliberately on-brand
- dramatic scoring that overstates the story

---

## 14. Thumbnail Creative Responsibility

When the editor is responsible for thumbnail production, they are expected to contribute creative judgment.

Green Knight should provide:

- packaging strategy
- core viewer promise
- selected title territory
- thumbnail concept or territories
- channel packaging standard
- channel visual references

The editor or thumbnail designer should improve:

- composition
- cropping
- hierarchy
- visual clarity
- lighting
- color treatment
- object placement
- typography where used
- polish
- one-second comprehension

They may propose a better concept if the brief's concept does not work visually.

The final thumbnail remains subject to Green Knight approval.

---

## 15. Brand Consistency

Editors should not reinvent the channel's visual language every episode.

Consistency should come from the channel's:

- `VISUAL_STYLE.md`
- `PACKAGING.md`
- `EXAMPLES.md`
- prior approved videos
- prior approved thumbnails

Consistency does not mean every episode looks identical.

The goal is recognizable family resemblance.

---

## 16. First Videos Establish the System

The first approximately 3 to 5 videos of a channel should receive more active review.

These videos help establish:

- pacing norms
- thumbnail language
- typography
- graphics
- visual density
- motion language
- music tendencies
- acceptable AI usage
- quality threshold

Strong internal examples from these early videos should be added to the channel's `EXAMPLES.md` and, where appropriate, `VISUAL_STYLE.md` or `PACKAGING.md`.

As the channel stabilizes, editors should receive greater autonomy.

---

## 17. Editor Improvement Obligation

An editor should not knowingly execute a weak instruction merely because it appeared in the production packet.

If an instruction:

- looks bad
- feels generic
- cannot be executed well
- would mislead viewers
- conflicts with available assets
- has a substantially better visual solution

the editor should either:

1. improve it within their creative discretion, or
2. flag it for review when the change would affect factual meaning or major editorial intent

Green Knight wants editors who think.

---

## 18. What Editors Should Not Change Silently

Editors should not materially alter:

- thesis
- factual meaning
- narration wording or channel voice identity
- story structure
- claims
- statistics
- chronology
- major packaging promise
- channel positioning
- required evidence

If production exposes an editorial problem, flag it.

Do not solve factual or structural problems through visual substitution.

---

## 19. Revision Standard

Revision notes should distinguish:

### Must Fix
Factual, structural, brand, technical, or material quality issue.

### Strong Preference
Green Knight's desired creative treatment.

### Optional Thought
An idea the editor may use if they agree it improves the piece.

This preserves clarity without turning revision into micromanagement.

---

## 20. Hiring Implication

Green Knight should prefer editors who demonstrate:

- strong taste
- narrative editing ability
- visual research ability
- restraint
- typography competence
- basic motion competence
- ability to work across real footage, documents, graphics, and imagery
- comfort proposing solutions
- ability to follow a brand system
- willingness to flag weak instructions

The cheapest technically competent editor is not necessarily the economically cheapest editor.

A slightly more expensive editor who materially improves retention, trust, and packaging may create much more value.

---

## 21. Success Standard

The editor relationship is working when:

- the editor consistently produces on-brand work
- Green Knight does not need to specify every visual
- the editor improves weak suggestions
- revisions become more focused over time
- production remains transferable
- visual quality feels intentional and premium
- the final video does not reveal the efficiency of the backend operation

The desired end state is not maximum control.

It is reliable creative alignment.
