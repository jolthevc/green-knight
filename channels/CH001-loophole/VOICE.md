# Voice and Writing Standard

## 1. Voice
The narrator is a dry, well-read fan telling a great story at the bar.
- Precise about rules.
- Openly delighted by clever exploitation.
- Never contemptuous of the sport or its officials.
- Energy is controlled, and absurd facts are left to land on their own.
- Humor comes from understatement.
- Confidence comes from quoting sources, not from superlatives.

## 2. Relationship
The narrator is a knowing guide speaking to a fellow fan who doesn't follow this particular sport. The narrator is not a professor, a hype man, or a moral judge. The viewer decides between genius and shameful, though the narrator may lean toward admiration.

## 3. Sophistication
- Translate each sport-specific term in one sentence on first use.
- Quote the rule, then restate it plainly.
- Explain engineering only as far as the exploit requires: downforce, yes; CFD, no.

## 4. Sentences
- Short-to-medium sentences in active voice.
- Fragments are allowed for punchlines.
- Narration paragraphs of about three sentences.
- At most one rhetorical question per section.
- Causal transitions: so, which meant, until.

## 5. Openings
Open on the absurd outcome or the moment of the exploit. State the promise within 20 seconds. No era-setting preamble.

- **Strong:** "Barbados were winning. That was the problem. With minutes left, they needed a goal, so they scored one. Into their own net."
- **Weak:** "Soccer has a long and storied history of dramatic tournaments."

## 6. Explanation and Story
- Order: rule, then gap, then exploit.
- Use at most one analogy.
- The exploiter is the protagonist, and their reasoning gets its moment.
- The backlash escalates.
- The rule change is the climax.
- Adaptation is the epilogue, often with an ironic callback.
- Attribute competing accounts: "Most accounts credit this game, though the league had been discussing a clock for years."

## 7. Humor
- Dry and observational, a light touch roughly once a minute.
- Never during a mechanism explanation.
- Mild sarcasm is acceptable toward institutions, never toward injured or disgraced people.
- No memes.

## 8. Numbers
- Scores and dates carry the story.
- Put other numbers in comparisons: "fewer points than most teams now score in a quarter."
- Never present estimates as precise figures.

## 9. Certainty
- State facts flatly.
- Mark disputed motives: "he later claimed," "it's never been proven."
- Never invent dialogue.
- Only quote real, sourced statements.

## 10. Favor
- Rule-text quotations.
- "Nothing in the rulebook said..."
- "technically," used sparingly.
- Concrete verbs.
- Period details.

## 11. Avoid
- "changed the game forever"
- "shocking" and "insane"
- "But here's the thing"
- "In the world of sports"
- Moral lectures.
- Calling a legal move cheating.

## 12. Strong Example
> "The rules allowed a fan, as long as its primary purpose was cooling. Brabham's fan did cool the engine. It also sucked the car onto the track like a vacuum cleaner. Which purpose was primary depended on who you asked, and Brabham was very happy to be asked."

This works because it paraphrases the clause, shows the gap, and lets understatement do the humor.

## 13. Anti-Example
> "In a shocking move that would change F1 forever, genius engineers unleashed an insane innovation nobody saw coming!"

This fails because it is all hype, superlatives, and no mechanism.

## 14. Narration Notes
- Write dates and scores for speech.
- Mark pronunciation of foreign names.
- No parentheticals.
- Put punchlines at the end of sentences.
- Use line breaks for pauses before reveals.
- The dry tone is delivery-dependent. If narration is synthetic, reject any take where understatement reads flat or the punchline timing is lost.

The narrator sounds interested in clever exploitation, not emotionally hyped by the event.
- Smart, dry, understated, slightly mischievous.
- Conversational, with crisp diction.
- Neutral American accent, medium vocal register.
- Confident but not theatrical.
- Not a sports-announcer voice. Not a movie-trailer voice.
- Humor is underplayed.
- Mechanisms are explained patiently.
- Default pace roughly 145-155 spoken words per minute, as a guide rather than a hard rule.

Video-specific pronunciation, pauses, emphasis and pacing should be carried in the production packet / editor handoff as needed. They never change the locked narrator identity, and no dedicated narration-automation document is required.
