# Packaging Standard

## 1. Philosophy
Lead with a recognizable anchor plus an impossible-seeming act. Choose specificity over mystery. The viewer should grasp the sport and the absurdity instantly, then wonder about the mechanism. The mechanism is the only thing withheld.

## 2. Viewer Questions
A strong package triggers one of these:
- How was that legal?
- Why would a team do that?
- What did they outlaw, and why?
- Why does this rule I see every week exist?

## 3. Title Principles
- Keep titles under about 60 characters.
- Use proper nouns when they are recognizable.
- Declarative is the default. Use a question only when the question itself surprises.
- Use numbers only when they are the hook, such as 19-18.
- No all-caps words.
- Use "cheating" only if the video concludes the move was cheating.
- Use "banned" only if the thing was actually banned, not merely amended.

## 4. Thumbnail Principles
- One focal object, scoreboard, or diagram.
- The redline or stamp device is optional and must be earned. Never use it by default.
- At most three words of text.
- Licensed archival photos only; logos only when licensed and necessary.
- High contrast on the paper and ink palette, with yellow and red accents.

## 5. Roles
The title names the act. The thumbnail shows the evidence.

**Example:** the title "They Scored on Themselves to Win" with a thumbnail of the real scoreline and a red arrow toward the wrong goal.

## 6. Honesty Limits
Packages may withhold the mechanism. They may not:
- imply illegality for a legal move
- imply a single cause when the record is mixed
- exaggerate the size of the rule change
- show a score or state that never happened

## 7. Familiarity vs Novelty
Prefer famous leagues. For lesser-known sports, lead with the act, not the sport. Use "Olympians Tried to Lose," not "The Badminton Scandal."

## 8. Process
1. Lock the one-line absurdity.
2. Write title options in three territories: act-led, consequence-led, and rule-led.
3. Draft object, diagram, and scoreboard thumbnail concepts.
4. Pair titles with thumbnails.
5. Check each pair against the script for truth.
6. Confirm the opening delivers the promise.

## 9. Strong Patterns
- **Act plus consequence:** "The F1 Car So Good It Only Raced Once." Avoid this pattern when the ban was gradual.
- **The rule you know:** "Why Keepers Can't Pick Up Back Passes." This pattern has search appeal.
- **Absurd number:** "The 19-18 Game Behind the Shot Clock." Use it only when the number itself is shocking.

## 10. Weak Patterns
- **Abstract:** "The Spirit of the Law."
- **Generic:** "10 Weird Sports Rules."
- **Overclaiming:** "The Cheat That Destroyed F1."
- **Context-dependent:** "The Chappell Delivery," which means nothing to non-cricket fans.

## 11. Thumbnails
**Strong:**
- An isolated object, such as the fan car's rear fan.
- A real absurd scoreboard.
- A minimal diagram with one glaring yellow actor.

**Weak:**
- Stadium panoramas.
- Shocked faces.
- Tiny rulebook text.
- Multi-sport collages.
- A BANNED stamp on every thumbnail.
- Red circles around nothing.

## 12. Opening Alignment
- The absurd act appears within 20 seconds.
- By 30 seconds, the opening has confirmed the move was legal or ambiguous and teased that the rule changed.

## 13. Experiments
- Proper-noun titles vs act-only titles.
- Redline vs clean object.
- Question vs declarative.
