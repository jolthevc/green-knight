## OTM-I0002: Louisville
- **Format/scope:** gateway/crossroads. Louisville airport and relevant regional business catchment; define historic decision period and present data separately during research.
- **Title alternatives:** Why Louisville Became a Delivery Crossroads; How a Delivery Hub Reshaped Louisville. Conditional on evidence.
- **Question/promise:** why this location, what made others cluster around it, and what the region retains from passing activity.
- **Who cares:** anyone tracking a package; evergreen question with dated operating facts.
- **Hypothesis:** location/operating constraints help explain the initial choice; accumulated facilities and complementary businesses help explain persistence. Unverified causal interpretation.
- **Hook concept:** a package's journey raises the puzzle of why Louisville is on its route. No invented actual tracking record and no claim all packages go there.
- **Three beats:** first hub choice and constraints; reasons for other businesses to co-locate; local value and dependence. Early payoff should explain a real routing incentive, then ask why a surrounding ecosystem develops.
- **Main audio challenge:** explain a network without accumulating airport names or unexplained operational terms.
- **Source leads:** https://www.jobs-ups.com/us/en/ups-worldport (company overview surfaced during planning, full episode passage checks pending); https://www.flylouisville.com/ (airport source lead, unopened for this project). Find original site-selection documents and independent regional evidence.
- **Risks:** a company facility tour instead of a place story; unsupported location explanations; scale/capacity confusion; historical versus current operations; unresolved local benefit claims.
- **Judgments (1–5):** broad appeal 5, familiar deliveries; curiosity 4, unexpected route; evidence 4, institutional records likely; visual feasibility 4, geography must be comprehensible; runway 5, comparable hub stories with distinct mechanisms.

## OTM-I0001: Dalton
- **Format/scope:** industry cluster. Dalton and northwest Georgia, with period/boundaries determined from historical evidence.
- **Title alternatives:** Why Did This Town Become a Carpet Capital?; The Household Craft That Changed a Georgia Town. Avoid current dominance claims until verified.
- **Question/promise:** how a local craft became an industrial concentration and what kept activity there.
- **Who cares:** familiar household product and an unlikely origin; evergreen historical core.
- **Hypothesis:** craft capabilities, mechanization and local commercial links reinforced each other. Research must establish chronology and links, not assume them.
- **Hook concept:** begin with the contrast between a bedspread and an industrial town, then explain the first documented connection. No fabricated founding scene.
- **Three beats:** actual starting craft economy; transition into mechanized production; ecosystem persistence and limits. First payoff connects product and technique; deeper question is why production stayed geographically concentrated.
- **Main audio challenge:** make the manufacturing transition clear without an engineering lecture.
- **Source leads:** https://www.daltonga.gov/523/Dalton-Carpet-Center and https://www.georgiaencyclopedia.org/articles/business-economy/carpet-industry/ (preliminary discovery leads; source passages and historical references require full inspection).
- **Risks:** promotional origin legend; over-crediting one person; old production shares treated as current; skipping labor, distribution or material environmental consequences. Broader issues require evidence and must serve the bounded question.
- **Judgments (1–5):** broad appeal 3, unfamiliar town but familiar object; curiosity 5, unexpected transformation; evidence 4, historical leads; visual feasibility 4, archival access to confirm; runway 5, many industry clusters with varied causes.

## OTM-I0003: Aspen
- **Format/scope:** destination/status. Aspen/Pitkin County and actual workforce catchment. Do not mix municipality, county and region statistics.
- **Title alternatives:** The Housing Problem Behind a Luxury Ski Town; How Do You Staff a Town Workers Struggle to Afford? Titles remain conditional on findings.
- **Question/promise:** how the destination economy depends on labor access, and what housing responses accomplish or fail to resolve.
- **Who cares:** travel, affordability and work; familiar human stakes.
- **Hypothesis:** destination demand and housing constraints interact with labor access; workforce housing is a response with trade-offs, not automatically a solution.
- **Hook concept:** ask where the people providing the destination's everyday services live. Establish actual conditions before making a claim about their experience.
- **Three beats:** demand and jobs; housing/commuting constraints; responses, costs and measured limits. First payoff identifies a supported dependence; deeper question asks who bears the cost of keeping the system functioning.
- **Main audio challenge:** explain housing rules and geography without policy jargon or a parade of statistics.
- **Source leads:** https://apcha.org/186/History and https://apcha.org/27/About (preliminary institutional leads). Add independent outcomes, resident/workforce evidence and comparable dated income/housing data.
- **Risks:** mission statement mistaken for impact; cherry-picked anecdote; all residents treated alike; speculative policy causality; current affordability statistics becoming stale.
- **Judgments (1–5):** broad appeal 5, housing and recognizable destination; curiosity 4, hidden support system; evidence 4, public records but impact attribution harder; visual feasibility 4, factual illustration without invented people; runway 4, avoid repetitive expensive-town stories.
