## FG-I0006 | The Man Who Rigged McDonald's Monopoly

**Alternative titles:** How McDonald's Million-Dollar Monopoly Prizes Were Stolen; The McDonald's Monopoly Winners Who Never Really Won.

**Question:** How could a promotion that looked like a randomized nationwide game keep awarding top prizes to a connected group, and who uncovered the control failure?

**Evergreen/human entry:** Everyone knows the feeling of peeling a fast-food sticker and hoping for a rare property or instant prize. Here the apparently random prize-draw step was compromised *before* the game pieces reached customers.

**Unverified editorial hypothesis (research must test):** The striking deception isn't a rigged computer algorithm or complicit McDonald's store employees: it is the outsourced **chain of custody** for high-value physical tokens, controlled by a security insider at the third-party operator. A network of recruiters and designated winners then converted the stolen pieces into apparent legitimate claims and split prize proceeds.

**Mechanism/status:** At 2001 indictment stage, federal authorities alleged Simon Marketing security worker Jerome Jacobson embezzled rare prize pieces, supplied recruiters, and designated winners falsely claimed legitimate prizes. DOJ reported more than **$13 million of high-value prizes** fraudulently claimed *as alleged by August 2001*. Contemporary Reuters reporting states Jacobson subsequently pleaded guilty and received 37 months in prison in January 2003. The overall case/later broader monetary totals and accomplice adjudications require further research and should not be merged carelessly.

**15–30s hook hypothesis:** “You opened a McDonald's bag and thought you had a chance at a million dollars. But for years, some of the winning game pieces were already spoken for. The man hired to protect the prizes was taking them before they reached restaurants. How did an apparently fair nationwide game become a private source of winnings?”

**Three story beats:** 1. Legitimate promotion, odds, physical prize production and safeguards. 2. Insider control of rare prizes and flow through recruiters to plausible winners. 3. Tip, FBI's Operation Final Answer, exposure, guilty plea and what security checks were actually missing.

**Audio challenge:** distinguish **the prize pieces** from the vast number of ordinary game tickets; define why only very rare top-value pieces had to be diverted; avoid overloading with an accomplice cast.

**Opened leads:** [DOJ original August 21 2001 arrests/official mechanism](https://www.justice.gov/archive/opa/pr/2001/August/422ag.htm); [FBI archived arrest announcement](https://archives.fbi.gov/archives/news/pressrel/press-releases/eight-arrested-for-defrauding-mcdonalds-corp.-and-its-customers-in); [Jan 2003 Reuters sentencing via LA Times](https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-2003-jan-11-fi-rup11.6-story.html). Historic McDonald's campaign imagery must be licensed before production.

**Risks:** 2020 HBO series McMillions already covered the story, so the YouTube episode must deliver a *different value*: the simple, auditable chain-of-custody weakness and real verification path, not repeat documentary characters or sensational anecdotes. DOJ 2001 press release uses **allegation language at filing date**, but later Jacobson guilty plea is established. Do not imply McDonald's itself organized the scam. Do not conflate 2001 $13m allegations with later ~$24m retrospectives.

**Originality and routing:** Related in theme only to FG-I0003 (fraud involving apparently legitimate inventory/collateral) and FG-I0005 (fake social proof). Here the forged signal is **physical winning ticket ownership and fraudulent prize claimant status**, not fake oil inventory or online reviews. Belongs in Fool's Gold, not a McDonald's Money Moves promo strategy.

**Editorial judgment out of 5:** broad appeal **5**, curiosity **5**, evidence **5**, feasible visuals **5**, 8–10m story runway **5**. Important narrative risk: cultural familiarity of McMillions, but profound mechanism and high-stakes FBI ending.

## FG-I0007 | The Nikola Truck That Couldn't Drive Itself

**Alternative titles:** The Truck Video That Made an Unfinished Prototype Look Real; Nikola's Electric Truck Had One Big Problem.

**Question:** What did a “truck in motion” promotional clip lead potential investors to believe, what was physically happening on the road, and how did investigators establish the discrepancy?

**Evergreen/human entry:** Everyone has seen a demonstration video offered as proof a new product works. The concrete revelation is that the shown moving truck could not propel itself; gravity was moving it down an incline.

**Unverified editorial hypothesis (research must test):** A tangible, easily understood *visual proof* can convert uncertain future technological claims into a feeling of completed engineering. The more valuable lesson is the difference between a concept/prototype, a working product, and the presentation of one as the other. Do **not** reduce every Nikola business activity or later developed truck to the same 2018 misleading video.

**Mechanism/status:** SEC July 2021 **civil complaint** asserts the Nikola One prototype was inoperable while a promotional video falsely implied movement under power: the truck was towed uphill then rolled down under gravity. DOJ later reported jury conviction and December 2023 sentencing for founder Trevor Milton. **Critical subsequent status:** A full and unconditional presidential pardon was granted **March 27, 2025** for his federal offenses (official DOJ clemency register and grant). Do not present 2023 conviction as an unqualified current legal status or imply the pardon retrospectively validates the promotional footage. Nikola, a company distinct from Milton, agreed to a **$125m SEC civil penalty in 2021 without admitting or denying** findings; company legal status is distinct from the individual's.

**15–30s hook hypothesis:** “A startup showed the world its futuristic truck moving along a desert road. It looked like proof that the machine could drive. But investigators say the truck had been hauled to the top of a hill, then simply allowed to roll down. So how did a promotional demonstration become part of a much bigger investor deception case?”

**Three story beats:** 1. Legitimate hydrogen/electric truck product development and why demonstrations matter. 2. Nikola One prototype reality versus filmed road scene; company and founder claims, investor credibility signals. 3. Public challenge, regulatory/court evidence, distinction between complaint and findings, corporate settlement, criminal outcome, **2025 pardon**.

**Audio challenge:** Explain *which truck and which 2018 video* without equating the entire company or every later product to a fake prototype; separate civil complaint, criminal adjudication, corporate settlement and presidential pardon in understandable chronology.

**Opened leads:** [SEC 2021 complaint, especially paragraphs 64–65](https://www.sec.gov/files/litigation/complaints/2021/comp-pr2021-141.pdf); [DOJ 2023 sentencing summary of adjudicated conduct](https://www.justice.gov/usao-sdny/pr/trevor-milton-sentenced-four-years-prison-securities-fraud-scheme); [DOJ official 2025 pardon register](https://www.justice.gov/pardon/clemency-grants-president-donald-j-trump-2025-present); [Nikola's SEC-filed 2021 settlement](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1731289/000119312521364159/d255053d8k.htm).

**Risks:** Topic known from short-seller coverage, danger of flattening *false demonstration* into all-company accusation; legally evolving status makes current checks mandatory. Avoid claiming any single staged video alone caused a quantified market capitalization or trading loss. Documentary licensing of the actual video separate.

**Originality and routing:** FG mechanism is **fabricated demonstration of product capability as investor verification**, not FA business-collapse cause analysis or FG-I0005 fabricated customer reviews.

**Editorial judgment out of 5:** broad appeal **5**, curiosity **5**, evidence **5**, feasible visuals **5**, 8–10m runway **4**. Visually and conceptually one of the best reveals, but harder legal framing.

## FG-I0008 | The Wells Fargo Accounts Nobody Asked For

**Alternative titles:** How Wells Fargo Opened Accounts Without Asking Customers; The Bank's Sales Numbers That Customers Never Authorized.

**Question:** How did sales quotas and weak verification turn supposed new customer bank accounts into records customers had never authorized?

**Evergreen/human entry:** A person trusts the bank to safeguard a checking account. The bank counts an extra customer product as success, but what if the customer never asked for it?

**Unverified editorial hypothesis (research must test):** Cross-selling is legitimate when customers consent. The breakdown occurs when a sales-product count is treated as proof of customer demand without separately verifying a valid authorization, giving staff and managers a reason to reward phantom openings.

**Mechanism/status:** On **September 8, 2016**, CFPB enforcement found bank staff opened deposit and credit card accounts and shifted funds from existing customer accounts without customer knowledge or consent. It cited **more than 2 million potentially unauthorized** deposit/credit accounts identified by the bank, **not** more than 2 million conclusively proven fake accounts. CFPB fined Wells Fargo **$100 million**, distinct from other penalties and restitution. The bank's independent board directors later published an April 2017 investigative report that identified sales-culture and performance-management failures; do not treat only individual staff as the sole causal problem.

**15–30s hook hypothesis:** “You go to your bank to open one account. Without asking, someone opens another one in your name. Now imagine a major bank reporting millions of accounts that might never have been authorized. Who benefits from an account a customer doesn't even know exists?”

**Three story beats:** 1. Legitimate cross-selling and why banks track products per customer. 2. Employee targets, authorized versus unauthorized account openings, movement of existing customer money to seed new accounts and incentive/oversight failure. 3. Regulatory probes, fee harm and restitution, bank board's findings, how permission differs from sales reporting.

**Audio challenge:** Keep *possibly unauthorized accounts*, *confirmed misconduct*, *customer fees* and *regulatory penalties* as **four different numbers/buckets**, not “bank made millions by opening millions of fake accounts.” Humanize without fabricated victim scenes.

**Opened leads:** [CFPB Sept 2016 enforcement explanation](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/archive/newsroom/consumer-financial-protection-bureau-fines-wells-fargo-100-million-widespread-illegal-practice-secretly-opening-unauthorized-accounts/); [Wells Fargo independent directors' April 2017 investigation, SEC filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/72971/000119312517118654/d375947ddefa14a.htm); [OCC Sept 2016 contemporaneous findings and restitution order](https://www.occ.gov/news-issuances/news-releases/2016/nr-occ-2016-106.html).

**Risks:** Scandal is familiar, and the original 2016 “2 million” screen was explicitly a *potentially unauthorized* filter. Later revisions to counts and ongoing company remedial issues require status updates before scripting; period-bound the episode to the 2016 exposure and 2017 board findings. Avoid portraying a victim as consenting or staff at every branch as complicit.

**Originality and routing:** FG-I0005 is manipulated *online review* reputation; FG-I0008 is **internal customer-account records and consent**. Not FA bank-collapse arc, not Pretty Penny ordinary bank fees.

**Editorial judgment out of 5:** broad appeal **5**, curiosity **4**, evidence **5**, feasible visuals **4**, 8–10m runway **5**. Excellent consumer relevance and institutional-system insight, but may feel less cinematic than prizes or a moving truck.
