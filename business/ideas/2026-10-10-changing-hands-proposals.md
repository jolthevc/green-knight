## DH-I0006: The Basketball Team That Stopped Playing and Kept Getting Paid
Alternatives: The NBA Deal That Outlived the Team; How Two Brothers Kept Getting Paid After Their Team Disappeared.
Question: How did the Spirits of St. Louis retain a claim on basketball television income after stopping play, and what did it take to restructure that claim?

Audience and stakes: Anyone who understands being bought out, losing a business or wishing an income stream would continue without the old workload. Basketball supplies recognizable context, with no sports-history prerequisite. Evergreen.

Hypothesis, unverified until full research: The decisive asset became a durable contractual participation in other teams' media income. Its duration and revenue definition became bargaining power as the media business changed. The strongest second act is the effort to regain control of that income stream.

Transaction, players, obligations and constraint: The Silnas and their Spirits partnership, four former ABA teams entering the NBA, and the NBA. Bound the story around the 1976 settlement and the 2014 restructuring, using the intervening growth only where it changes the economics. Investigate the four clubs' payment formula, duration, media definitions and any dilution protection. Then establish the debt, equity and distributions in the 2014 transaction, separating announced terms from closing. A recurring payment obligation cannot simply be eliminated by retiring the team.

First payoff and deeper question: The operating team and the income right were separable. Once the viewer understands that, ask: how do you buy back a payment obligation whose future cost keeps changing? Expected changed understanding: ownership of a business and ownership of a slice of its revenue are different assets; the latter can survive the former.

Hook concept, approximately 20 seconds: "In 1976, the Spirits of St. Louis stopped playing basketball. But one part of the team kept working: a contract entitling its owners to future television money. Years later, there was another deal to negotiate. How do you put a price on checks that were supposed to keep coming?"

Three possible beats:
1. The team disappears, the contract survives: explain what was exchanged in 1976, and who was obligated to pay.
2. The business changes around the agreement: establish the revenue growth and disputed media categories, without inventing foresight.
3. Buying control of the obligation: reconstruct the 2014 ownership-and-financing proposal and verify what closed, instead of ending with a pile-of-money anecdote.

Visual device: A midnight-blue position board. Remove the basketball court while leaving the contract and payment arrows in place; later rearrange ownership tiles around the entity holding that contract. No need for expensive game footage.

Original-source leads, opened 2026-10-10:
- Weltman v. Silna, 879 F.2d 425 (8th Cir. 1989), background section: https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F2/879/425/438606/ . Confirms the team ceased operations in 1976 and describes cash plus perpetual television participation. The case concerns partnership rights, not the later settlement.
- NBA official release, January 7, 2014, transaction paragraphs: https://pr.nba.com/nba-aba-spirits-settlement/ . Describes a conditional partnership, investment by the four teams, new Spirits debt and a distribution to existing owners. It does not prove closing or disclose an exact payout.

Risks: Avoid calling all receipts profit, assigning partnership proceeds entirely to the brothers, claiming a risk-free masterstroke, or treating the 2014 proposal as a simple complete cancellation. Original agreement and closing evidence remain research tasks. Avoid unsupported headline dollar amounts and claims that this was superior to owning an NBA franchise.

Deduplication: No matched subject or payoff among all 23 ideas. DH-I0005 also involves acquisition financing, but answers how a competitive corporate buyout was funded; this answers how a contractual payment stream survived operations and was restructured. No intentional revisit. Changing Hands owns the transaction; no family-succession or general sports-business premise.

Assessment, editorial judgments: broad appeal 5/5; curiosity 5/5; evidence 4/5; visual feasibility 5/5; runway 5/5. An immediately understandable contradiction develops into a second negotiation with substantial financing insight; exact terms require further sourcing.
Selection checks: familiarity 5/5; stakes 4/5; explanation feasibility 5/5. Strong 8-10 minute potential without a long league history.

## DH-I0007: Volkswagen Bought Rolls-Royce. So Why Does BMW Own It?
Alternatives: The Rolls-Royce Deal: One Buyer Got the Factory, Another Got the Name; Who Actually Bought Rolls-Royce?
Question: How did the 1998 sale produce Volkswagen-owned Bentley and BMW-owned Rolls-Royce, and which separately controlled assets shaped the outcome?

Audience and stakes: Anyone who recognizes a Rolls-Royce as an object of status and assumes buying a company includes its famous name. Rival bidders, incomplete ownership and a negotiated split make the stakes legible without finance training. Evergreen.

Hypothesis, unverified until full research: Bargaining power depended on assembling complementary rights and production capabilities, rather than simply winning the operating-company auction. Test whether engine-supply dependence and the transition period explain the settlement more convincingly than the popular claim that Volkswagen just forgot to buy the name.

Transaction, players, obligations and constraint: Vickers as seller of the car business; Volkswagen as buyer; Rolls-Royce plc as holder of separate name rights; BMW as brand-rights acquirer and engine supplier. Focus on the 1998 transaction and the agreed transition to 2003. Investigate the ownership of each asset, payments, licence duration and supply obligations. Separate a brand-rights payment from the cost of acquiring or building an entire operating business.

First payoff and deeper question: The car company and the Rolls-Royce name had different owners. Then ask: if BMW had the name and Volkswagen had the existing operation, why did neither simply walk away? Expected changed understanding: acquiring a company may leave essential rights and inputs controlled by others; leverage comes from what the other party needs and how quickly it can replace it.

Hook concept, approximately 20 seconds: "Volkswagen bought the business that built Rolls-Royces. BMW acquired the rights to the Rolls-Royce name. One deal delivered a factory. Another delivered the badge customers wanted on the bonnet. So what had each company actually bought, and how could either turn its piece into a working business?"

Three possible beats:
1. The apparent prize: reconstruct the auction and immediately separate operating assets from name rights.
2. The missing pieces: add engine supply and the costs and timing of building a new operation; test each side's alternatives.
3. The negotiated split: trace temporary arrangements through 2003, with Bentley and Crewe on one side and BMW's new Rolls-Royce operation on the other.

Visual device: One luxury car separates into labeled factory, brand-rights and engine tiles. Transfer the tiles across a negotiation table, then resolve them into two viable businesses. Rights are conceptual illustrations, not fabricated legal documents.

Original-source leads, opened 2026-10-10:
- Volkswagen Group, The History of Bentley, opening acquisition paragraphs: https://www.volkswagen-group.com/en/the-history-of-the-brands-17668/the-history-of-bentley-17674 . Describes Crewe/Bentley and temporary Rolls-Royce-name use, while presenting VW's own rationale.
- BMW, August 24, 2001, new-facility announcement, brand acquisition and production paragraphs: https://www.press.bmwgroup.com/usa/article/detail/T0022067EN_US/bmw-group-starts-building-the-new-rolls-royce-facilities . Describes obtaining the name rights from Rolls-Royce plc in 1998 and preparing the 2003 operation.
- Additional opened lead: BMW's July 1998 engine-supply termination announcement: https://www.press.bmwgroup.com/deutschland/article/detail/T0005642DE/bmw-ag-kuendigt-vertraege-mit-rolls-royce-motor-cars?language=de . The body dates the announcement July 9, 1998 and termination July 9, 1999; webpage date metadata is malformed. It also records VW's stated ability to replace supplies.
- Independent regulatory lead, not inspected beyond search excerpt because a bot challenge blocked the page: European Commission M.1283: https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:31998M1283 . Retrieve the actual decision during research.

Risks: Corporate histories conflict in tone and self-interest. Do not claim Volkswagen was unaware of the rights split, that BMW acquired the whole business for a brand-licence price, or that VW's Bentley outcome was worthless. Engine-supply leverage is a causal hypothesis, not established by a termination notice alone.
