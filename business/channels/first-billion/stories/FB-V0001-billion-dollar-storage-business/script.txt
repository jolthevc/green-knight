TITLE: What Would It Take to Build a Billion-Dollar Storage Business?

Think of a storage room the size of a spare bedroom. Someone pays for that empty space every month. Could enough rooms like it become a billion-dollar business? In our example, yes. But the person who assembles the buildings might still be nowhere near a billionaire. To understand why, follow the money from one door.

We're building a fictional American storage company, checked against real industry filings. Every property price, loan and ownership percentage in our example is an assumption. The finish line is one billion US dollars of modeled property equity, meaning property value after subtracting property debt. Not a billion dollars in rent. And not cash in anybody's pocket.

Start with one existing facility containing sixty-five thousand square feet available to rent. A ten-by-ten room is one hundred square feet. Some rooms are occupied, others empty. Instead of looking at a tempting advertised rate, let's measure what the whole property actually brings in.

Assume annual revenue averages eighteen dollars for each available square foot. For the area of a ten-by-ten room, that's effectively a hundred and fifty dollars a month, averaged across occupied and empty space. It's our assumption, not a national average. Multiply by the entire building and you get about one-point-two million dollars in annual revenue.

Then come taxes, staff, insurance, maintenance and advertising. Assume they consume thirty-five cents of every revenue dollar. The building is left with about seven hundred and sixty thousand dollars a year in property operating income. That isn't profit in the owner's bank account. We haven't paid the lender or the company running the buildings.

Does that sound plausible? In 2025, the mature properties reported by Public Storage retained about seventy-five percent of revenue as property operating income. CubeSmart's comparable properties retained roughly seventy-one percent. Our small fictional operator keeps sixty-five percent. Those giants have advantages a newcomer doesn't, so this is a benchmark, not proof our building would perform.

Now for the first surprise. An income stream of roughly seven hundred and sixty thousand dollars could support a property value of eleven-point-seven million dollars. Here's the reasoning: suppose buyers want annual property operating income equal to six-and-a-half percent of the price. Work backward from the income, and you reach eleven-point-seven million. That pricing ratio is called a cap rate. The rate is hypothetical, not a quoted market offer.

We've turned an unremarkable rental building into a nearly twelve-million-dollar asset on paper. But that's not twelve million dollars of earnings. If buyers demanded a higher income yield, the very same building would command a lower price. One number measures what customers pay; another measures what an investor would pay for those payments.

Now imagine you're trying to build the company. You find a facility priced at our eleven-point-seven million dollars. Buying an eleven-point-seven-million-dollar asset for that amount doesn't create eleven-point-seven million dollars of new wealth. It exchanges money for property. You can earn rent on the investment, but to build value beyond the price paid, you need better operations, attractive purchases, profitable development or favorable market changes.

And you need the purchase money. Suppose a lender finances half. Roughly five-point-nine million dollars is borrowed, leaving about five-point-nine million dollars of equity to raise before fees and reserves. If outside investors supply it, they expect ownership or contractual claims. One viable storage building is not automatically a viable financing strategy.

There are three broad routes. Buy occupied buildings and pay for existing income. Develop buildings and take construction and empty-unit risk, hoping the completed property is worth more than it cost. Or manage other owners' buildings for fees. That last path needs less property capital, but the managed buildings don't become yours.

This industry already operates at immense scale. Public Storage reported buying eighty-seven facilities for roughly nine hundred and forty-six million dollars during 2025. Extra Space said it managed more than eighteen hundred buildings for third-party owners that year. Those are actual reported numbers. They don't establish what a newcomer could acquire, earn or borrow.

So let's take the buying route to its extreme. Imagine assembling one hundred and eighty properties that each perform like our first. Real locations vary enormously. Competitors discount, construction oversupplies a market, and taxes rise. Extra Space described pricing pressure from new supply in its 2025 reporting. Identical buildings are a simplifying assumption, not a plausible acquisition schedule.

Under that assumption, one hundred and eighty buildings add up to roughly two-point-one billion dollars of property value. Surely that's enough? Not yet. If lenders are owed about half, that's around a billion dollars of debt. Subtract it, and all the equity holders together own a little more than one billion dollars of modeled property equity. We've reached our target, but only after accounting for the money we owe.

There's a deeper problem with celebrating. To assemble those buildings at their assumed values, someone had to provide roughly a billion dollars of equity capital as well as roughly a billion dollars of debt. If the acquisitions were at fair value, the act of buying them didn't create the billion. It created a portfolio with a billion dollars of capital at risk. Whether the business actually builds wealth depends on returns after paying for all that capital.

Even the operating cash deserves a closer look. Across our portfolio, annual property operating income is about one hundred and thirty-seven million dollars. At an assumed six percent interest rate, interest costs about sixty-three million. Allow illustrative headquarters costs and recurring capital reserves, and around forty-nine million dollars remains before taxes, principal repayments and other claims. That's a small annual cash remainder beside the billion-dollar equity headline.

Then imagine the market turns. Revenue per available square foot falls ten percent. Operating costs take forty percent of revenue instead of thirty-five. Buyers now demand a seven-and-a-half percent income yield rather than six-and-a-half. The buildings are still there, but they earn less and buyers pay less for each dollar they earn.

Our modeled property value drops from about two-point-one billion dollars to roughly one-and-a-half billion. But the existing debt hasn't vanished. Subtract it, and equity falls from a little over a billion dollars to about four hundred and sixty million. More than half its paper value disappears without selling a single building. That's a stress test, not a forecast. Fixed debt makes an operating setback more painful for shareholders.

And here's the question hidden inside our opening. Suppose you founded this business but outside investors now own eighty percent of its common equity. Your twenty percent of the successful billion-dollar base case is worth roughly two hundred million dollars on paper, assuming no further senior claims. Extraordinary, but not a billion. And without a sale or distribution, it's still paper, not spendable cash.

There's no contradiction. A business can own valuable property without creating an equal amount of founder wealth. The bank owns a debt claim; investors own equity rights; the operator may own only a slice. Promoted interests, preferred payments, tax and transaction costs can change the division further. We can't invent those terms for our fictional company.

So our answer is one viable building, repeated roughly one hundred and eighty times, funded with about half debt and half equity, under the specific rent, cost and valuation assumptions we've chosen. In real life, the challenge isn't multiplying the first unit. It's finding deals worth buying, finding capital on workable terms, and protecting the income when the market changes.

Return to that one rented room. Its monthly payment has to support the building, justify a purchase price, help service a lender and reward the people who financed it. That's the distinction behind the billion-dollar headline. Buildings generate income. Markets put prices on income. Contracts decide who receives the value. And the person who started it all may own much less than the headline suggests. For another business explained from its first unit, that's where we'll go next.
