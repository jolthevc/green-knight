TITLE: What Would It Take to Build a Billion-Dollar Storage Business?

A storage room is one of the strangest products in business. The company sells empty space. You bring everything that goes inside, and every month, you pay again. Enough rooms can be worth an extraordinary amount. But there's a trick hidden in the billion-dollar headline: owning valuable buildings, creating wealth, and getting rich yourself are three different things.

We're building a fictional American storage portfolio, checked against actual industry filings. Our property prices and loans are assumptions. The finish line is one billion dollars of modeled property equity: what remains after property debt. It is not annual sales, profit or a founder's personal fortune.

Start with a facility containing sixty-five thousand square feet available to rent. A ten-by-ten unit is a hundred square feet, roughly a small room. Some are occupied, others empty. Customers move in and out. The advertised rental rate alone cannot tell us what the building earns.

Assume eighteen dollars in annual property revenue for each available square foot, averaged across the whole building. For a ten-by-ten patch of space, that's effectively a hundred and fifty dollars a month. It's an average including vacancies, not every tenant's rent. Across the facility, annual revenue is roughly one-point-two million dollars.

Now pay for taxes, insurance, repairs, utilities, staff and advertising. Suppose those expenses consume thirty-five cents of each revenue dollar. The building keeps about seven hundred and sixty thousand dollars a year in property operating income. That isn't money the owner can take home. We haven't paid lenders or headquarters yet. But this is the number that drives the story.

The margin isn't completely out of the blue. In 2025, Public Storage's mature comparable facilities reported a property operating margin near seventy-five percent. CubeSmart reported about seventy-one percent for comparable stores. Our smaller fictional operator gets sixty-five percent. Those giants have advantages and locations we can't assume a newcomer could copy.

Here's the first twist. If buyers require annual property income equal to six-and-a-half percent of the price, they might value our building at eleven-point-seven million dollars. That's a cap rate: the income divided by the price a buyer would pay. The rate is hypothetical, not a market quote. A building selling empty rooms is suddenly an almost twelve-million-dollar asset on paper.

But put yourself in the buyer's shoes. An eleven-point-seven-million-dollar price isn't eleven-point-seven million dollars you've made. If you purchase the property at fair value, you've exchanged money for an asset. You might earn rent, improve it or eventually sell it for more. Signing the purchase agreement alone hasn't made you rich.

Where does the money come from? Assume a lender supplies half the purchase price. That's nearly six million dollars borrowed, while almost six million more must come from equity investors, before fees or reserves. If someone else writes that check, they expect ownership or other rights. Now the challenge looks less like renting rooms and more like raising capital.

So what could an operator do to actually create value? Imagine increasing the building's annual operating income by fifty thousand dollars, perhaps through fuller occupancy or better operations. At the same six-and-a-half percent cap rate, that added income would imply about seven hundred and seventy thousand dollars in extra property value. Before the cost of improvements. That's a hypothetical result, not an easy win.

Now there are two different growth engines. Buy more buildings, which costs capital. Or improve what you own, which might create additional value. Developing a facility is another route: you hope it will be worth more when leased than the land, construction and waiting cost. Managing facilities for fees requires less property capital, but someone else's buildings are not your equity.

Look at how big those choices can become. Public Storage reported buying eighty-seven facilities for about nine hundred and forty-six million dollars in 2025. Extra Space said it managed more than eighteen hundred properties for third-party owners at year-end. Those are real reported figures. Neither proves what our newcomer could acquire or borrow.

Now assume we somehow assemble one hundred and eighty buildings, all performing like the first. Real markets aren't identical. Some face higher taxes, slower lease-up or new competitors offering discounts. Extra Space described pressure from new supply in its 2025 reporting. A calculation you can repeat isn't necessarily a business you can repeat.

Still, look at the math. One hundred and eighty properties at eleven-point-seven million dollars each imply about two-point-one billion dollars of gross property value. Sounds like we've passed the target. But half was financed with debt. Lenders are owed around a billion dollars. Subtract that and a little over a billion remains for all property equity holders together.

Here's the uncomfortable part. To buy those assets at their assumed value, investors had to contribute roughly a billion dollars of equity alongside the debt. If you simply purchase fairly priced buildings with their money, you've assembled a huge portfolio. You haven't proved investors are any wealthier than when they started.

Remember that extra fifty thousand dollars of property income we hoped to create at one location? Across one hundred and eighty sites, the same improvement would add nine million dollars in annual income. At our unchanged cap rate, that could support around one hundred and forty million dollars of added property value, before improvement costs. This is what increasing the pie might look like, rather than just buying more pie.

Even a huge portfolio isn't an unlimited cash machine. Our buildings produce about one hundred and thirty-seven million dollars in annual property operating income. Assume annual loan interest costs sixty-three million. After illustrative headquarters costs and reserves for property work, less than fifty million remains before taxes, loan repayments and other claims. Annual cash is not the same as the billion-dollar valuation.

Now test a bad year. Revenue per available square foot falls ten percent. Expenses consume forty percent instead of thirty-five. Buyers demand a seven-and-a-half percent property-income yield rather than six-and-a-half. We still have the same buildings. They simply earn less, and buyers will pay less for the income.

Together those changes push modeled property value from about two-point-one billion dollars to roughly one-and-a-half billion. Debt hasn't vanished. Subtract it, and property equity falls from just over a billion to around four hundred and sixty million dollars. More than half the paper value disappears without selling a building. It's a stress test, not a prediction.

There's one more catch. Suppose you founded the company, found the properties and operate them. What share is actually yours? If outside investors provided most of the equity, a twenty-percent founder stake isn't automatic. It requires your own investment or a negotiated arrangement that rewards your work and results. Investors decide those rights in contracts.

If you did keep twenty percent of the billion-dollar equity base, that would be roughly two hundred million dollars on paper, before any senior investor rights, taxes or selling costs. Remarkable wealth, but not a billion dollars in cash. And under our downside case, that same percentage would be worth much less.

So what does it take? A facility that earns real property income. The ability to finance, find and operate around one hundred and eighty properties under these assumptions. The discipline to create value beyond what investors paid, and an actual contractual stake in the result. The hard part is not multiplying sixty-five thousand by one hundred and eighty. It's earning and keeping a worthwhile return on all the money required.

Go back to that ten-by-ten room. The customer pays for empty space. That rent covers costs, supports a property price, helps pay a lender and eventually reaches whoever owns the equity. A billion-dollar storage business isn't a secret recipe for a billionaire. It's an expensive machine for turning rent into value. The real question is how much new value the operator creates, and how much they have a right to keep. If you want another business taken apart from its first unit, subscribe for the next one.
