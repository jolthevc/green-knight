TITLE: What Would It Take to Build a Billion-Dollar Storage Business?

Picture a self-storage building on the edge of town. Roll-up doors, security cameras, and almost nothing on the shelves, because customers bring the stuff themselves. Could a business made of buildings like this really be worth a billion dollars? Yes. But how many buildings would you need? And who would actually own that billion?

We're going to build a hypothetical American storage company, using real industry filings to check the logic. Our finish line is one billion US dollars of equity value: what the owners' stake might be worth after subtracting debt. Not a billion in annual sales. Not cash in a founder's account. This is a worked model, not a company that secretly exists.

Start with one property containing sixty-five thousand rentable square feet. Customers rent units of different sizes, typically paying month to month. A ten-by-ten unit is one hundred square feet. You're not selling the boxes inside. You're selling access to a small piece of real estate, over and over again.

But a website's advertised monthly rent isn't what a building actually collects. Some units sit empty. People move out. New customers receive promotions. Our model assumes eighteen dollars in annual property revenue for each available rentable square foot, after those realities and other property income. That's an illustrative input, not a national average.

Eighteen dollars times sixty-five thousand square feet gives one million, one hundred seventy thousand dollars in yearly property revenue. Subtract taxes, labor, insurance, maintenance, utilities and advertising. Let's assume those property expenses consume thirty-five percent. That leaves about seven hundred sixty thousand dollars of net operating income, or NOI. It is not the owner's profit. Debt, head-office costs and major capital spending are still outside that calculation.

Is that plausible? Public Storage's mature facilities reported about a seventy-five percent NOI margin in 2025. CubeSmart's comparable portfolio reported about seventy-one percent. Those are experienced, large-scale operators. Our hypothetical margin is sixty-five percent, partly because a newcomer shouldn't automatically inherit the largest companies' efficiencies. Actual results could be worse.

Now comes the first powerful mechanism. Buyers often value income-producing property using a capitalization rate, or cap rate. Divide annual property NOI by the return rate a buyer demands. At an assumed six-and-a-half percent cap rate, seven hundred sixty thousand dollars of NOI implies a property value of about eleven point seven million dollars.

The building collects roughly one-point-two million dollars a year, but its modeled value is nearly twelve million. Not because it earned twelve million in profit, and not because anyone has offered to pay that price. It's because someone willing to buy an income stream at that valuation rate would pay roughly that amount. Change the NOI or cap rate, and the value changes.

Now repeat the property one hundred eighty times. That's eleven-point-seven million rentable square feet, roughly two hundred eleven million dollars of annual revenue, and nearly one hundred thirty-seven million dollars of property NOI. At the same six-and-a-half percent cap rate, the portfolio's real estate is worth about two-point-one billion dollars in the model.

Here's the catch: the buildings' value isn't what shareholders own free and clear. Suppose half that value is financed by debt. Lenders have a claim of roughly one-point-zero-five billion dollars. Subtract that from the modeled property value and the residual equity is roughly one-point-zero-five billion. We've reached the billion-dollar target on paper. Not with one extraordinary building, but with one hundred eighty ordinary ones and an enormous financing obligation.

Getting started is the first mountain. A single property valued at eleven-point-seven million dollars would need around five-point-eight-five million dollars of equity if financed halfway with debt, even before transaction costs and reserves. Somebody has to write those checks. At portfolio scale, investors collectively would have to support more than a billion dollars in equity and about the same amount in debt.

And you cannot simply copy and paste a building. Acquiring a mature property buys existing income, but you pay for it. Developing one requires land, construction, time and the risk of empty units. Managing properties for other owners takes much less property capital, but a management fee is not the same as owning the underlying real estate.

Look at the actual scale of acquisitions. Public Storage reported buying eighty-seven facilities with about six-point-one million rentable square feet for roughly nine hundred forty-six million dollars in 2025. That's about one hundred fifty-five dollars per square foot across those purchases. Our hypothetical stabilized property is valued at one hundred eighty dollars per square foot. The mix, locations and economics differ, so this is a reality check, not a verified asking price for our imaginary property.

The hundredth acquisition also may not look like the first. Every location has different demand, zoning, competition, property taxes and construction risks. Storage has faced new supply and pressure on move-in rates. Extra Space's leadership described that struggle in its 2025 reporting. One profitable site proves a market can work. It does not prove that a hundred more sites will work equally well.

Scale can create advantages. Marketing, customer service, revenue management and software can be shared across buildings. Extra Space reported managing more than eighteen hundred stores for third-party owners at the end of 2025. Its management business can generate fees without buying every building. But in our calculation, we've counted only equity in owned property, not a speculative premium for a management platform.

Now examine the cash. Our portfolio produces almost one hundred thirty-seven million dollars of property NOI. If its one-point-zero-five-billion-dollar debt costs an assumed six percent interest annually, the interest bill alone exceeds sixty-three million dollars. Add a hypothetical ten million for corporate overhead and fifteen million for recurring capital reserves. About forty-nine million remains before taxes, principal repayment and other possible claims. A billion-dollar equity valuation is not a billion-dollar annual cash machine.

And here's the stress test. Revenue per square foot falls ten percent. The NOI margin falls from sixty-five to sixty percent. Buyers demand a seven-and-a-half percent cap rate instead of six-and-a-half. Same buildings. Same debt balance. The modeled property value falls to about one-point-five-two billion dollars.

Subtract the original debt and equity is now worth only about four hundred sixty-three million dollars. More than half of our billion has disappeared without losing a building. That's not a forecast. It's what happens when weaker income and a less generous market price meet fixed debt. Leverage enlarged the owners' upside in the original scenario, and it magnifies their downside here.

There's a second trap in the phrase billion-dollar business. Who owns the equity? Suppose a founder organized the deals but outside investors own eighty percent. The founder's twenty percent of a billion is about two hundred million dollars on paper, before dilution, preferred claims, tax or selling restrictions. That's potentially extraordinary wealth, but not a billionaire. Fees and performance incentives may change the calculation, and only the actual agreements reveal who gets what.

Even a founder's paper stake isn't a bank balance. Turning it into spendable money requires dividends, a sale, refinancing or another liquidity event, each with costs and limits. Nor would a property-level valuation necessarily be identical to the sale price of the whole company after corporate obligations, transaction costs and ownership rights.

So here's the complete answer. One representative building creates about seven hundred sixty thousand dollars of modeled annual property income. One hundred eighty make a portfolio large enough to cross two billion dollars in property value under our assumptions. Debt absorbs roughly half. Outside capital has to fund the rest. And whoever owns the shares holds a claim on the remaining billion, with real risk attached.

The quiet storage building is interesting because it turns everyday monthly payments into a financeable stream of income. But the real engine isn't just rent. It's repeatable property economics, disciplined expansion, capital and ownership. The next time you pass rows of storage doors, ask what those doors earn, what someone would pay for that income, and how much is left after the lenders. That's the difference between a billion-dollar headline and a billion-dollar business. If you want another business built from its first unit, join us for the next one.
