TITLE: Nike Left Amazon. Why Did It Come Back?

In 2019, Nike walked away from selling directly to Amazon. The world's most famous sportswear company wanted more control over its products and customers. Six years later, it announced that it was going back. So what changed? The strange part is that, while Nike was away, its shoes never actually left Amazon.

Search for Nike sneakers during those years and you could still find them. Independent merchants were selling Nike products on Amazon's marketplace. Nike had ended its own supply relationship with Amazon, not erased its brand from the website. The distinction sounds technical. It is the key to this whole story.

Imagine you're looking for running shoes. You might open Nike's app because you know exactly which model you want. Or you might open a giant online store because you want to compare five brands. Those are two very different customers. One comes looking for Nike. The other might discover Nike, or somebody else.

To understand why Nike was willing to leave, we have to go back to 2017. Nike agreed to a limited pilot selling products directly to Amazon. Its chief executive, Mark Parker, said the goal included improving how Nike was presented and telling better product stories on the platform. Better control was part of the attraction.

The relationship also sat inside a thornier problem. Amazon had a marketplace filled with independent sellers. Some offered genuine shoes acquired through other channels; counterfeit concerns complicated the picture. Supplying Amazon directly might improve the selection and presentation. But supplying Amazon did not mean Nike could control everything that happened around its products.

Then came 2019. Nike announced it was ending that limited pilot to focus on closer, more personal relationships with customers. It would still work with other retailers and platforms. That detail matters: this was never a plan to make every Nike purchase happen on Nike.com. It was a decision about who should stand between Nike and the shopper.

There were real alternatives. Nike could keep the Amazon pilot, push for different marketplace terms, restrict the assortment, or concentrate on its own digital stores and selected partners. We have evidence of the pilot and the chosen exit, not a transcript of every option debated internally. Each path carried a different trade-off between reach and control.

The strongest argument for Nike's own stores is easy to understand. A customer visits Nike's website. Nike controls the photographs, the surrounding products, the launch, and much of the customer relationship. There is no rival sneaker sitting one click away in the same product grid. And Nike can learn more directly from how that customer shops.

There is an appealing financial argument too. When a brand sells through a retailer, the retailer needs a margin. When it sells directly, the brand may capture more of the final ticket price. But that does not mean every direct sale is more profitable. Nike must also pay for its own fulfillment, returns, stores, customer service, and the work of attracting the shopper.

So the gamble was not simply about keeping more dollars from a shoe. It was about doing more of the retail job. If customers already knew what they wanted and went straight to Nike, that job could be valuable. If customers needed help finding the product in the first place, the calculation could look very different.

The pandemic initially made a digital-first future seem close at hand. In 2020, Nike announced its Consumer Direct Acceleration, emphasizing its own digital properties and stores alongside selected partners. Its 2022 annual report said Nike Direct had reached about forty-two percent of Nike Brand revenue, and the company had reduced its number of wholesale accounts globally.

At this point the strategy seemed to offer the best of both worlds: a powerful brand, millions of consumers to reach directly, and less dependence on outside storefronts. But notice what the number does not tell us. A higher share of sales through your own channels does not, by itself, prove that total demand or profit is growing.

Now picture a different shoe buyer. She enters a sporting goods store wanting something for a first marathon. She does not know which brand fits. The clerk offers a few options. She tries them on. The next shelf contains a rival's new model. The store is not merely processing an order; it is helping create one.

That is the overlooked job of distribution. A retailer can offer convenience, comparison, staff recommendations, local presence, and the chance to encounter a product you were not seeking. Online marketplaces offer a different version of the same thing: a search box, competing options, reviews, fast delivery, and customers who arrived without choosing a brand.

Giving up a retail relationship may therefore cost more than the sales recorded at that account. It can also cost a brand moments of discovery. That is an analytical possibility, not a measurement of customers Nike actually lost. But it turns the original question around. What if the middleman was doing more than taking a cut?

Meanwhile, the market was changing. New running-shoe competitors gained attention. Nike had to manage product freshness, demand for established styles, inventory and promotional pressure. Those forces cannot be folded into a single story about Amazon. A company can have a distribution problem and a product problem at the same time.

Nike's fiscal 2025 numbers made the pressure visible. Total company revenue fell from about fifty-one billion dollars in fiscal 2024 to roughly forty-six billion in fiscal 2025. Nike Direct revenue fell thirteen percent. Nike Brand digital sales alone fell twenty percent. These are worldwide figures, not an estimate of revenue lost because Nike left Amazon.

In October 2024, veteran Nike executive Elliott Hill became chief executive. His turnaround focused on sport and rebuilding partner relationships. Nike later described those retailers as important for product feedback, not only for selling shoes. That is another role the narrow direct-versus-wholesale margin debate can miss: outsiders can tell you what customers are choosing.

Then, in May 2025, came the reversal. Nike announced it would again sell directly through Amazon in the United States, alongside an expansion to other retail partners. Amazon said it would begin sourcing a wider Nike assortment directly. The company also gave affected third-party sellers time to sell certain overlapping inventory.

Here is the twist. In 2017, Nike joined Amazon hoping for a better brand presentation and more control over the shopping experience. In 2019, it left that direct arrangement while sellers kept listing its shoes. In 2025, it returned, partly to meet customers where they preferred to shop. The same marketplace that created control problems also offered access Nike could not wish away.

And Amazon was not presented as some magic cure. On Nike's fiscal 2025 year-end earnings call, management described the new relationship as beginning small, not a material near-term driver of company sales. Treat Amazon as the clearest symbol of the larger distribution rethink, not as the single switch that could fix a global business.

By fiscal 2026, that broader shift was visible in the channel numbers. Nike Brand wholesale revenue rose six percent on a reported basis, to about twenty-seven and a half billion dollars. Nike Direct revenue fell six percent, to about seventeen point seven billion. That is movement in different channels, not proof the new strategy has restored Nike's fortunes.

The first quarter of fiscal 2027 added a warning against declaring victory. Globally, wholesale revenue was roughly flat on a reported basis and down one percent after adjusting for currency. Direct revenue fell eight percent. Yet North American wholesale grew nine percent. A rebuilding effort can show traction in one market while facing weakness elsewhere.

It is tempting to call the old plan a mistake and the return a correction. That is too easy. Direct channels gave Nike important capabilities, including its own stores, digital services and direct consumer relationships. Retailers have costs and incentives of their own. Counterfeits, discounting and inconsistent presentation are real concerns, not imaginary excuses.

The stronger answer is that the balance was wrong for the conditions Nike eventually faced. Not every customer wants to begin shopping inside a brand's own world. Some want to compare, browse, ask questions or buy wherever it is easiest. A great brand might pull people to its own door. But it can also benefit when other doors lead to the product.

Could Nike have avoided the pain by never ending its Amazon pilot? We cannot know. No public filing gives us the missing counterfactual. Nike's product choices, competitors, wholesale cuts, pandemic conditions and regional demand all moved at once. A clean, single-cause story would be satisfying. It would also be less truthful.

So the lesson is not that every brand should sell everywhere. Some products really do need controlled distribution, and a poor retail experience can damage trust. The useful question is what each channel actually contributes. Does it merely collect a sale that would happen anyway? Or does it introduce customers, offer feedback, and create demand that would not otherwise exist?

Remember the shopper searching for sneakers on Amazon in 2019. Nike had stepped away from the direct deal, but the customer had not stepped away from the search box. That is why this reversal matters. Nike was trying to control how its shoes were sold. The harder question was who could influence which shoes people wanted to buy.

And those are not always the same people.
