# Trades, deals and financial contests

The subject is a consequential financial position or transaction. The viewer asks “What did they put at risk, how did the money move, and why did the bet or deal turn out that way?”

Use the tension of a caper while preserving documentary accuracy. Do not imply that legitimate trading is theft or that every counterparty is a villain.

## Audience and runway

Start with recognizable stakes: control of a famous company, an enormous position, a scarce asset, a currency under pressure or a buyer trying to finance an acquisition. A curious adult should follow without investing experience.

| Series | Story engine |
| --- | --- |
| The Position | A trade whose payoff depended on a specific market mechanism |
| The Squeeze | Scarcity, positioning and forced buying or selling |
| The Takeover | A contest for ownership and control |
| The Buyout | Financing, bargaining and debt behind a consequential acquisition |
| The Standoff | A market contest shaped by resources, rules and timing |
| The Unwind | How positions and financing collided when a bet had to be closed |

Focus on well-documented historical episodes. Contemporary news, recommendations and stock picks are not the launch format. Do not manufacture “how to copy this trade” instruction.

## Boundaries

Changing Hands centers the financial transaction or position. Money Moves centers a strategic operating choice. Fallen Angels centers a business's decline. Fool’s Gold centers a deception mechanism. Pretty Penny explains an ongoing economic system. Silver Spoon centers inherited wealth and family control.

A takeover belongs here when the transaction mechanics, bidding or financing are the payoff. A company's decision to expand through acquisitions can belong in Money Moves when strategy dominates.

A fund's collapse belongs in Fallen Angels if organizational failure is central. Its position structure and forced unwind can belong here when that is the distinct question. Record the difference and related IDs. A fraudulent trade is routed to Fool’s Gold if the concealment is the story's main reveal.

## Required research

In addition to the shared evidence packet, build:
1. Event boundary: which trade, acquisition or connected sequence, and over which period?
2. Players and objectives: trader, buyer, seller, lender, regulator and relevant counterparties.
3. Starting positions: what each side owned, owed, controlled or was committed to deliver.
4. Mechanism: how the instrument, contract or ownership structure worked in plain language.
5. Funding and constraints: capital, debt, collateral, liquidity, time, supply and relevant rules.
6. Event timeline: position-building, negotiations, turning point, execution/closing and unwind where relevant.
7. Cash/ownership map: who transferred what to whom, before and after.
8. Outcomes: documented proceeds, gains, losses or changes in control, with units and period.
9. Counterevidence: disputed numbers, retrospective claims, alternative explanations and unavailable records.
10. Risk: what could have gone the other way and what participants knew then.

Prefer original disclosures, offer documents, court/regulatory records, contemporary reporting, verified historical data and credible participant accounts. Distinguish company announcements, preliminary offers and completed transactions. Disclose inaccessible source passages; do not treat a repeated anecdote as independent confirmation.

## Financial precision

Distinguish:
- Equity purchase price versus enterprise value.
- Position notional versus capital committed.
- Gross exposure versus net exposure.
- Borrowed money versus investor capital.
- Paper gains versus realized gains.
- Price change versus actual trading profit.
- Returns before fees/financing versus after them.
- Mark-to-market pressure versus final economic loss.
- An available bid versus an accepted/closed deal.

Use sources for contract terms and financing. A leveraged position needs the denominator and obligation explained. A simple illustrative example can teach the mechanism, but must be explicitly distinguished from actual historical positions and payoffs.

Do not reconstruct a precise position or dollar profit from a price chart alone. If the evidence supports only a range or disputed estimate, say so. Check currency, inflation adjustments and dates before making historical comparisons.

## Story judgment

A strong episode contains a comprehensible mechanism, visible constraints, an evidence-backed turning point and a meaningful outcome. Famous names alone are insufficient. Avoid a generic market-crash montage or a hedge-fund biography.

Explain both upside and downside. Do not present a winner's result as proof the bet was safe, or describe an outcome as inevitable. The closing teaches what decided this contest, not what the viewer should buy today.
