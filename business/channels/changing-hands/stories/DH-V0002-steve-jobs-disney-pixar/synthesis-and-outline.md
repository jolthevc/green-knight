## Thesis and surprise

The company that distributed Pixar's movies ended up buying the company that made them. The seller who controlled Pixar, Steve Jobs, wasn't primarily paid cash: he exchanged Pixar equity for a ~6.3% minority stake in the much larger Disney group and received a Disney board seat. Two competing shareholder groups became one, but the merger traded independent creative control for participation in Disney's larger earnings and franchise network.

**Newcomer expectation:** Disney financed a studio or paid Jobs $7.4 billion in cash. **Revealed model:** Disney created stock to acquire all Pixar shares, with new ownership rights and share-price exposure on both sides.

**Informed skeptic objection:** A large individual stake cannot control Disney; 2004 breakup did not end existing Disney film rights; merger wasn't inevitable or riskless. The script expressly defuses all three and acknowledges independent distributions as a credible alternative.

## Mechanism/positions map

Before: **Pixar public shareholders (Jobs ~50.6%)** hold Pixar equity. Disney shareholders hold Disney equity. Disney/Pixar bound by film contracts but remain separate.

At May 5, 2006 close: Disney issues **~277.9m** new Disney shares to the former Pixar holders. Disney owns all Pixar. Jobs converts 60,000,002 Pixar → 138,000,004 Disney (+3 existing) = **138,000,007** total, **~6.3%** Disney. Share issuance reduces other Disney holders' *percentage*, does not move dollars into Jobs' account.

Bargaining constraints: Pixar needed worldwide distribution and creative autonomy; Disney wanted a reliable studio/character pipeline. Both faced genuine alternative courses, negotiation risk, share-price risk and culture/integration uncertainty.
