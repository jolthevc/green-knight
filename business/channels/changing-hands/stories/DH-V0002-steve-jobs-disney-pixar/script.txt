TITLE: How Steve Jobs Became Disney's Biggest Individual Shareholder

In May 2006, Steve Jobs became Disney's largest individual shareholder. He didn't take over Disney or receive a giant check. Disney bought Pixar, the studio behind Toy Story, paying with its own shares. The man best known for Apple suddenly owned a sizable piece of Disney.

But that deal began with a problem hiding inside some of the most successful animated films ever made. Pixar created the characters. Disney helped finance the movies and sold them around the world. And the contract binding the two companies gave Disney more power over those characters than most viewers probably realize.

Think about Finding Nemo. Pixar made the world of talking fish feel real. Disney put it in theaters, sold merchandise and brought those characters into its wider business. Both made money. But inventing Nemo was different from controlling where Nemo appeared next.

The companies had partnered since before Toy Story in 1995. A 1997 agreement covered five original movies. Pixar made them. Disney marketed and distributed them. They shared profits after Disney recovered specified expenses and a distribution fee. Disney also had exclusive rights to distribute and exploit the covered films.

And there was a twist. If they disagreed about whether to make certain sequels or related projects, Disney's decision generally prevailed. Pixar also didn't receive the theme-park revenue generated by those movies. A character Pixar dreamed up could be attracting visitors to a Disney park, without Pixar receiving a share of the park tickets.

Pixar was powerful but not entirely free. It couldn't simply take Woody, Buzz or Nemo to another studio. When the agreement expired, it could seek a distributor for future films. The old characters and their contracts were another matter.

The five-film contract had a finish line: Cars, scheduled for 2006. By then Pixar would have made films from A Bug's Life to The Incredibles. The companies had to decide what happened after that final original film.

Negotiations broke down. Disney announced in January 2004 that talks about a new agreement had ended. Its finance chief publicly argued that Pixar was demanding terms that would take hundreds of millions of dollars away from Disney. That was Disney's position in a negotiation, not a neutral verdict on what Pixar deserved.

Jobs wasn't just Pixar's chief executive. He owned more than half its stock. He had enormous influence over its future, and Pixar could consider another distributor for new movies.

Pixar spoke with other major studios, but its filings call those talks preliminary. It still needed worldwide distribution. Disney already had major rights to the old films. What it risked losing was the team making the next ones.

For a while, that seemed to be where things stood. Then Disney got a new chief executive: Bob Iger. In June 2005, Disney and Pixar resumed talking about a future distribution arrangement. Another contract was still possible.

But in October, Iger put a different possibility on the table. Why keep renegotiating the relationship between two companies when Disney could buy Pixar itself? On October twelfth, he raised a potential merger with Jobs. They discussed the idea, but they did not settle a price that day.

Buying Pixar would solve one problem immediately. Instead of Disney and Pixar defending separate shareholder interests every time money or rights became contentious, one company would own the studio and its future films. But it would create a bigger problem if Pixar's filmmakers lost the freedom that made their movies successful.

This was where the deal became more than a financial calculation. Disney wanted the creative engine. Pixar wanted to know whether that engine could still work inside Disney. Jobs and Pixar's directors discussed culture, leadership, and how the studios might operate after a sale.

Disney could rebuild its own animation business. Pixar could remain independent with another distributor. In November and December, both sides studied those choices. Pixar brought in financial and legal advisers to scrutinize the merger.

On January twelfth, 2006, executives reached preliminary understandings about management and preserving Pixar's way of working. Two days later, Jobs and Pixar creative leaders presented the business to Disney's board. The people and the operating plan were coming into focus. One enormous question remained open: the price.

Only, this price was not going to be a fixed check. Disney wanted to pay in Disney stock. That meant Jobs and the other Pixar shareholders would need to agree how many shares of the buyer they should receive for each share of Pixar they gave up.

On January twenty-first, Jobs and Iger reached a ratio they were prepared to recommend to their boards. Three days later, the companies announced it: two point three Disney shares for every one Pixar share.

Imagine holding ten Pixar shares. Under the deal, they would turn into twenty-three Disney shares. You would no longer own stock in a stand-alone Pixar. You would own a small slice of Disney, which would in turn own all of Pixar. No one had to pretend those twenty-three shares were cash.

Disney's announcement valued the transaction at about seven point four billion dollars, using its stock price at the time and the fully diluted Pixar share count. That was a headline value for the whole transaction, not what Jobs personally took home. Because payment was in shares, the value could move with Disney's share price.

And Jobs had another piece of leverage. He owned just over half of Pixar. Under a voting agreement, he committed shares representing forty percent of Pixar's outstanding stock to supporting the merger. It didn't eliminate every remaining approval, but it showed how much of the proposed sale already had his backing.

The agreement also addressed the people making the films. Ed Catmull, Pixar's president, would lead the combined animation studios. John Lasseter would take a senior creative role across Pixar and Disney Animation. Pixar's operations and location were expected to remain in place. Those were meaningful promises, not guarantees that the cultures would merge smoothly.

On May fifth, 2006, the transaction closed. Disney issued new stock to Pixar shareholders, and Pixar became its wholly owned subsidiary. Years of arguing across a distribution contract ended with one company owning the other.

For Jobs, the result was striking. Roughly sixty million Pixar shares became about 138 million Disney shares. His securities filing reported that, together with three Disney shares he already held, he owned approximately six point three percent of Disney. Contemporary reports called him its largest individual shareholder.

Six point three percent isn't control of Disney. Jobs could not personally tell the entire company what to do. But it was a huge individual investment in a business far larger than Pixar. He also joined Disney's board, giving him a separate voice in its oversight.

Disney's existing investors now owned a smaller percentage of Disney because new shares had been issued. In exchange, their company controlled one of animation's most valuable creative teams.

Neither side received a guaranteed happy ending. If Pixar's people left or the films stopped connecting with audiences, Disney would have paid dearly for a weakened studio. If Disney's stock fell, the market value of what Pixar investors had received would fall too. Those risks were real when the documents were signed.

Here's the unusual thing about what Disney bought. It already had major rights to the old Pixar films. The deal was not simply about getting Toy Story onto another shelf or collecting more revenue from a familiar character. The critical missing asset was the ability to keep inventing the next Toy Story.

Jobs traded away control of independent Pixar. In return, he owned part of a far larger company with theme parks, television, film distribution and Pixar itself. Disney's investors accepted dilution to bring Pixar's future under the same roof as its past.

So next time the Disney name appears before a Pixar movie, remember the other transaction behind it. Disney bought the studio. Pixar's shareholders became Disney's shareholders. And Steve Jobs went from owning most of one extraordinary company to owning a meaningful piece of a much bigger one.
