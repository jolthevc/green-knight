TITLE: How Steve Jobs Became Disney's Biggest Individual Shareholder

In May 2006, Steve Jobs became Disney's largest individual shareholder. Not because he built a theme park. Not because he ran Disney. Because the company behind Toy Story was his, and Disney wanted to buy it. When the deal closed, Jobs received about 138 million Disney shares. He hadn't sold a single ticket to Disneyland.

Most people know Steve Jobs as the man behind Apple. But there was another company in his life: Pixar. And the remarkable part of Disney's purchase isn't just the multibillion-dollar price. It's how Disney paid, and what that payment made Jobs on the other side.

Imagine two companies making a hit movie together. One makes the film. The other markets and distributes it worldwide. Both benefit, until they disagree about the money and who controls what happens next.

Disney and Pixar had worked together since the early days of computer animation. Their first film agreement led to Toy Story in 1995. Then came A Bug's Life, Monsters, Incorporated, Finding Nemo, and The Incredibles. Behind those characters sat an increasingly complicated business partnership.

Under their 1997 agreement, Pixar made the films. Disney marketed and distributed them. They shared profits, but only after Disney recovered its distribution fee and agreed costs. Disney also controlled important distribution decisions.

That arrangement was powerful. Pixar could concentrate on making films, while Disney supplied a worldwide machine for selling them. But the better Pixar became at making hit movies, the harder the next question grew: why should Pixar keep accepting the same division of rights and money?

By 2004, their talks about extending the relationship had broken down. Disney announced Pixar would end negotiations while completing its existing films. Disney's chief financial officer said Pixar's proposed terms would have cost Disney hundreds of millions it believed it was already entitled to receive.

Pixar wasn't announcing that Toy Story and its other films would disappear from Disney's shelves. Existing rights and agreements were still there. The real contest concerned what came after the current contract. Pixar wanted a more favorable arrangement for its future films, and it could look for another distributor.

It did explore that possibility. Pixar's own securities filings say it held preliminary conversations with other major studios. But those discussions had not matured into detailed negotiations. Pixar had successful films and substantial financial resources. It still faced the challenge of finding a partner able to release its next generation of movies worldwide.

Disney had more than movie theaters in mind. A character could become a toy, a theme-park attraction, or another film. Owning its creator was different from negotiating another distribution contract.

In 2005, Robert Iger became Disney's chief executive. Talks between the companies resumed, at first about distribution. Then, on October twelfth, Iger told Jobs that Disney wanted to explore something much larger: a combination of the two businesses.

That changed the stakes. Pixar could remain independent and find a distributor. Disney could seek to rebuild its animation operation without Pixar. Or Disney could buy Pixar, ending the problem of two companies bargaining over the same characters while answering to different shareholders.

But a purchase carried risk. If Disney absorbed Pixar and damaged the creative team, it could undermine the very thing it wanted to acquire. So the negotiations concerned who would lead the animation business, not just price.

The merger filing records the talks. In December, executives debated leadership and Pixar's culture. Pixar's board considered independence. Disney's board studied alternatives without Pixar. Neither side had to accept the first proposal.

By January twelfth, 2006, the two sides had reached preliminary understandings on aspects of management and culture. But they had not agreed on the exchange ratio. That sounds technical. It was the number that determined how much of Disney Pixar shareholders would get.

On January twenty-first, Jobs and Iger settled on a figure they were prepared to recommend to their boards: two point three Disney shares for each Pixar share. Three days later, Disney and Pixar announced their agreement.

This was an all-stock acquisition. Disney did not give every Pixar shareholder a pile of cash. Instead, it would issue new Disney shares. If you owned ten Pixar shares, the deal would turn them into twenty-three Disney shares. Your investment would move from a stand-alone animation studio into the much larger Disney company.

Disney announced a transaction value of seven point four billion dollars, based on its share price and a fully diluted count of Pixar shares at the time. It also quoted six point three billion after subtracting the cash sitting inside Pixar. Those numbers are not the same thing as what Jobs personally received, or a cash payment made by Disney.

Now comes the twist that makes this deal different. Jobs was not just Pixar's chief executive. He owned just over half of its outstanding shares. A purchase of Pixar therefore meant negotiating with someone who could have enormous influence over whether the sale happened at all.

He agreed to vote shares representing forty percent of Pixar's outstanding stock in favor of the merger. Not all the shares he owned, but a large committed block. Disney still needed the necessary shareholder approval and regulatory clearances. The agreement gave it far greater confidence that its proposed acquisition could close.

And Jobs was not the only person whose role mattered. Pixar president Ed Catmull was designated to lead the combined animation studios. Creative leader John Lasseter would oversee creative work at both Pixar and Disney Animation. The deal promised to preserve Pixar's operations and culture. Whether that would work was still a risk.

On May fifth, 2006, the deal actually closed. Disney issued roughly 278 million new shares to former Pixar shareholders. Pixar became a wholly owned Disney subsidiary. And those new Disney shares changed the identity of one particularly important investor.

Jobs' own filing gives the precise arithmetic. His 60 million and two Pixar shares converted into 138 million and four Disney shares. He already held three Disney shares. Put them together, and his reported stake was 138 million and seven shares, about six point three percent of Disney.

He had gone from holding a majority of Pixar to holding a minority of Disney. Yet that minority was enormous for one person. Contemporary reporting described him as Disney's largest individual shareholder. The holding was valued around three point nine billion dollars at the time. That was stock-market value, not money Disney wired into his bank account.

Consider the trade on both sides. Jobs surrendered his direct ownership position in an independent Pixar and gained a claim on Disney's much larger collection of businesses. Disney's existing shareholders owned a slightly smaller percentage of Disney because new shares were issued, but Disney now owned Pixar outright.

Jobs also joined Disney's board. That gave him a voice in oversight, not the power to run Disney by himself. Meanwhile, the Pixar leadership team received significant creative responsibilities. These were related parts of one deal, but they were not interchangeable: shares determine ownership; a board seat provides governance influence; executives run operations.

Did Disney pay too much? The answer wasn't written into the contract. Pixar's board warned about integration and culture. Disney shareholders faced dilution. With a fixed share-exchange ratio, the dollar value to Pixar holders could also move with Disney's stock.

Pixar had another possibility: remain independent, negotiate a better distribution agreement, and keep its own future upside. Disney could have walked away and pursued other ways to revive animation. We know they chose the merger. We cannot know exactly how either alternative would have turned out.

The deeper reason the deal is so interesting is that Disney wasn't merely purchasing past box-office hits. It was trying to bring an uncertain stream of future stories, characters, and talent under one corporate roof. Jobs, in return, swapped concentrated control of that creative business for ownership across the broader Disney empire.

So the next time you see Toy Story under the Disney name, remember what else changed hands in 2006. The movies were the familiar part. The unfamiliar part was the share register. Disney got Pixar. And Steve Jobs got a piece of Disney.
