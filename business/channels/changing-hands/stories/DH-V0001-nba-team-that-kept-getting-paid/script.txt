The Spirits of St. Louis never played a game in the NBA. But in the 2010-11 season, a partnership holding the rights left behind by that team reportedly received seventeen and a half million dollars in NBA television payments. The basketball team itself had shut down thirty-five years earlier.

That is the puzzle. The Spirits were gone, but four NBA teams still had to surrender a slice of their television income to the former owners' partnership. And decades later, those paying teams would announce a plan to take control of the business receiving the money. How did an excluded team end up with a right worth negotiating over?

The story begins with two brothers who actually wanted to own an NBA franchise. Dan and Ozzie Silna first tried to buy the Detroit Pistons. According to Dan's later account, when that failed, they bought a team in the rival American Basketball Association and moved it to St. Louis.

The ABA was a separate professional basketball league. By 1976, it was running out of road. Four teams were about to join the NBA: Denver, Indiana, San Antonio and the Nets, then based in New York. The Spirits weren't invited. Dan later recalled that the struggling St. Louis club had sold just six hundred season tickets.

Another team left out of the deal, the Kentucky Colonels, accepted a one-time payment and folded. The Spirits' owners pressed for a different kind of compensation. The four teams going into the NBA had an immediate reason to resolve the departing clubs' claims; the Spirits had a reason not to walk away for nothing.

The eventual settlement included cash for player contracts and something that would prove far more durable: a share of television income. Their lawyer and business partner, Donald Schupak, helped negotiate the arrangement.

Here's what the Spirits got. Each of the four incoming clubs would give up one-seventh of its qualifying NBA television share. The NBA would handle the payments directly. Four separate slices, drawn from four teams' allocations, not one-seventh of the league's entire revenue.

Use invented numbers for a moment. Say each of the four clubs receives seven million dollars in qualifying TV money. One-seventh is one million. The Spirits get one million from each club's share. That makes four million dollars, even though the Spirits aren't playing.

This was not a seventh of everything the NBA earned. It was one defined revenue stream, and the Spirits didn't have to play to collect.

It was revenue, not profit. Even if a paying club had an expensive roster or a losing year, the Spirits' slice didn't depend on what the club had left after its bills.

There was also no ordinary expiration date. A later court opinion described the payments as continuing for as long as the NBA existed.

They failed to enter the NBA, but held a right to grow with its television business. They didn't have to sell another ticket.

In 1976, no one knew how valuable NBA television would become. The four teams needed to complete their move, and this future obligation helped settle the immediate dispute. Hindsight makes that trade look much easier than it was.

The first checks were not the fortune this story has become famous for. A court recorded about one point three five million dollars in payments for the 1987-88 season. By 2010-11, reporting put the annual amount at seventeen and a half million dollars. That's the figure from the opening, paid decades after the last Spirits game.

Those were payments to the partnership, not a proven tally of the brothers' personal profits. Other partners, costs and legal claims mattered. But the scale of the recurring income was unmistakable.

The agreement also anticipated a problem: the NBA might add more teams. Ordinarily, more clubs sharing television money means a smaller slice per club. The Spirits' formula could shrink as the league expanded, but only until the calculation reached twenty-eight teams. After that, expansion alone could not shrink its percentage further.

That protection did not guarantee a dollar amount. The covered television pot could still rise or fall. The contract controlled one way the fraction could get smaller.

And television was changing. As the league found new ways to distribute its games, a different question emerged: what money actually belonged inside that covered pot?

The agreement covered “visual media broadcasts.” But did that include international games, the league's own channel, and newer ways of reaching viewers? The meaning of an old phrase now carried money.

The Spirits argued that they were missing income from international broadcasts, NBA TV and other distribution. They also sought access to records to check the numbers. The dispute reached court.

The New York courts did not decide that the Spirits were owed the extra money. They ruled that the dispute belonged in federal court. So the fight over what counted as television revenue remained unresolved by those state-court decisions.

If more kinds of broadcast income counted, the Spirits might receive more. If not, the four clubs owed less. An enduring percentage is hard to value when the underlying business keeps changing.

In 2014, reporting placed a settlement at five hundred million dollars. Ozzie later confirmed a settlement had been made. That headline number wasn't a public breakdown of the transaction. The NBA's own announcement described something more involved than cancelling the old right.

In January, the league said the four former ABA teams expected to invest in the Spirits partnership and become controlling owners of its common equity. The existing owners would keep the rest. The partnership would still hold the television and visual-media income right.

There was a second piece. Under the announced plan, the Spirits entity would take on new debt and distribute some of the borrowed cash to its existing owners. The NBA did not publish the full borrowing terms.

Follow the money one more time. Before the proposed change, the four clubs gave up television revenue to a partnership they did not control. After the contemplated investment, those same clubs would control the entity receiving that revenue. They would be on both sides of the relationship: paying into the stream and owning the business collecting it.

Borrowing could also turn some expected future income into cash available sooner for the selling owners. But a loan has to be repaid. Debt proceeds are not the same thing as profit, and we don't know the private terms well enough to calculate anyone's final return.

The league called that framework conditional. Ozzie later confirmed a settlement, but the full closing documents are not public here. The important idea is that the proposed answer was to change ownership of the recipient, not simply make the income right disappear.

Why would the brothers agree after holding on for so long? Ozzie offered a very human answer. “I'm 81, I ain't gonna live another 25 years,” he told The Malibu Times.

One owner was eighty-one. A stream that might pay for decades can look different when the owner's time horizon is shorter. Taking cash today may matter more than preserving every possible future payment.

It's tempting to turn the whole episode into a story about two men who saw everything coming. Dan's account was less tidy. He told Forbes that they hadn't imagined how large the television business would become. They had hoped for growth. They hadn't foreseen the scale.

They also originally wanted an NBA franchise. We can't prove this was a better outcome than owning one, with all the costs, control and resale value that would have involved.

What survived was a contract to receive part of defined television revenues. It could earn money without a team, and that right had its own value.

By 2014, that value was significant enough for the paying clubs to propose controlling the recipient. It wasn't really about basketball games anymore. It was about ownership of basketball's income.

The Spirits never made it into the NBA. Their contract did. And long after the team played its last game, that was what the other clubs still had to negotiate over.
