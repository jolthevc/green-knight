The Spirits of St. Louis never played a game in the NBA. They shut down in 1976. Yet decades later, money from NBA television kept arriving. The players had moved on. The team had disappeared. The right to get paid was still there.

That right belonged to the partnership behind the Spirits, led by brothers Ozzie and Dan Silna. When their team closed, they kept a claim on part of professional basketball's future. Eventually, getting control of that claim would become a major deal of its own.

To see how they got it, go back to a time when owning an NBA team was exactly what the brothers wanted. According to Dan's later account, they tried to buy the Detroit Pistons. When that failed, they bought a team in the rival American Basketball Association and moved it to St. Louis.

The ABA was a separate league. By 1976, it was approaching its end, and four of its teams were headed into the NBA: the Nuggets, Pacers, Spurs and Nets. The Spirits weren't coming with them.

For the brothers, this was the door closing on the business they had hoped to own. For the four teams going through it, there were agreements and legal claims to settle. They wanted to get into the NBA. The Spirits needed a reason to accept being left outside.

The eventual settlement included cash for player contracts and something that would prove far more durable: a share of television income. Their lawyer and business partner, Donald Schupak, helped negotiate the arrangement.

Here's the part to keep straight. The basic deal gave the Spirits one-seventh of each of those four teams' television shares. One-seventh of four allocations, with the NBA authorized to make the payments directly.

Let's use invented numbers to make that visible. Suppose each of the four teams has seven million dollars in qualifying television revenue. One million from each allocation goes to the Spirits. Four teams, four payments, four million dollars.

The Spirits weren't getting a seventh of everything the NBA earned. They had a slice of a particular income stream. But that slice didn't depend on the Spirits winning, selling tickets, or even fielding a team.

And it was a share of revenue. If an active club spent heavily on players and had a bad financial year, that didn't automatically erase the Spirits' contractual slice of its television allocation. The check followed the defined revenue pool, rather than whatever profit was left after running the team.

There was also no ordinary expiration date. A later court opinion described the payments as continuing for as long as the NBA existed.

Imagine accepting compensation for a business you have to close, then discovering that part of your compensation can grow with the industry you just left. You no longer need to sell another seat to benefit from a larger television audience.

Of course, in 1976, the future size of that audience was unknown. The four incoming clubs were trying to complete a difficult move into another league. Accepting a future payment obligation solved an immediate problem. Calling the deal obvious now skips the uncertainty they faced then.

The contrast became much clearer as the years passed. A court recorded about one point three five million dollars in settlement payments for the 1987–88 season. Contemporary reporting put the annual payment at about seventeen and a half million dollars for 2010–11.

Those are receipts, not a calculation of the brothers' personal profits. The right belonged to a partnership. But they show how far the economics had traveled from the team that stopped playing in the seventies.

Even expansion had been anticipated. Usually, adding more teams means dividing a shared television pot into more pieces. The Spirits' arrangement limited that dilution: its calculation wouldn't keep shrinking just because the league grew beyond twenty-eight teams.

Picture more chairs being pulled up to the table. Everyone else now has another person to share with. For this calculation, the Spirits could stop counting at twenty-eight. The size of the television pot could still change, but one source of dilution had been fenced off.

That made the wording around the pot especially important. What exactly counted as television money?

The agreements used the broader term “visual media broadcasts.” As basketball reached audiences through more channels and across borders, a contract written in the seventies raised a very modern question: which of these new receipts belonged in the calculation?

The Spirits argued that they were missing income from international broadcasts, NBA TV and other distribution. They also sought access to records to check the numbers. The dispute reached court.

The New York courts said the dispute belonged in federal court, without deciding whether those extra payments were owed. The claims then continued there.

Still, the economic problem is easy to see. For the owners receiving the checks, a broader definition could mean more income. For the clubs funding them, it could mean a larger obligation. A permanent percentage is difficult to price when the business underneath it keeps changing.

By 2014, the parties had a new arrangement to announce. Contemporary reporting put the settlement at five hundred million dollars. In April, Ozzie confirmed to The Malibu Times that a settlement had been made.

But the most revealing part is in the NBA's own January announcement. It described the four teams investing in the Spirits entity and taking a controlling ownership interest. The existing owners would keep the remaining equity.

The plan also called for the Spirits entity to borrow money and distribute part of the proceeds to its existing owners. The NBA disclosed the design, but not the full financing terms.

Now look at the arrangement from both sides. The four clubs had been funding a stream of payments to an entity owned by somebody else. Under this structure, they would also become its controlling owners. The income right stayed in place; ownership of the recipient changed.

In that design, borrowing would supply cash sooner, instead of requiring the sellers to wait for years of future payments. The borrowed money would have to be repaid. It wasn't newly created profit.

This helps explain why the story can't end with a cartoon of the NBA tearing up an old contract. The announced solution brought the paying clubs into the business on the other side of it, while leaving the former owners with a smaller interest.

Why would the brothers agree after holding on for so long? Ozzie offered a very human answer. “I'm 81, I ain't gonna live another 25 years,” he told The Malibu Times.

A right can last longer than the person who owns it. The value of waiting changes when your own time horizon changes. That doesn't tell us the perfect price for the settlement. It does explain why an owner might prefer a large payment now over an uncertain sequence of future checks.

It's tempting to turn the whole episode into a story about two men who saw everything coming. Dan's account was less tidy. He told Forbes that they hadn't imagined how large the television business would become. They had hoped for growth. They hadn't foreseen the scale.

They also originally wanted an NBA franchise. We can't prove this was a better outcome than owning one, with all the costs, control and resale value that would have involved.

What we can see is the asset they actually preserved. A basketball team needs players, a schedule and people willing to watch. A contractual share of somebody else's television revenue needs a business that keeps generating the covered income, and an agreement that keeps entitling you to a piece.

The Spirits lost their place on the court. Their owners kept a financial connection to the game. Decades later, that connection had become valuable enough for the paying teams to seek control of it themselves.

The team disappeared. The asset that mattered was still on the other side of the contract.
