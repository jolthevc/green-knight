# Financial contest story: 8–10 minutes

Research the transaction before choosing the interpretation. Build the thesis and internal outline, write the complete spoken draft in one pass, then review and revise internally.

## Narrative architecture

| Approximate window | Story job |
| --- | --- |
| 0:00–0:25 | Concrete stakes, unusual position or consequential contest |
| 0:25–1:30 | Players, objective and an early useful explanation |
| 1:30–3:00 | Explain the transaction/position with a simple visual example |
| 3:00–5:00 | Show the build-up, funding, negotiations or constraints |
| 5:00–7:00 | Turning point and consequences; test the initial interpretation |
| 7:00–9:30 | Outcome, money/ownership transfers and the limits of the lesson |
| Final 5–20 seconds | Relevant next-video invitation when available |

Windows are planning aids. Finished runtime is 8–10 minutes including pauses and closing. Narrow a complicated crisis to one defensible event rather than compressing decades.

## Hook and engagement

Prepare two or three honest hooks internally. Choose one with recognizable stakes and a puzzle the episode can answer. No invented dollar figure, exaggerated certainty, greeting or subscription request before the story.

The first 30 seconds must deliver the title/thumbnail expectation. Explain something useful early: the position, the obligation or why the obvious move was difficult. Do not withhold the mechanism until the closing.

Keep tension through resources, supply, financing, rival responses and timing. Each beat changes what the viewer understands. About every 30–60 seconds check whether evidence, stakes or comprehension progressed; this is an editorial diagnostic, not an official platform rule.

## Make the mechanism comprehensible

Introduce the people and objectives before instrument terminology. Show who owns what, who owes what and what happens if the price or outcome changes. Explain one moving part at a time.

Use a clearly labeled illustrative example if needed, then return to the documented event. Distinguish fictional teaching amounts from historical figures. Avoid jargon lists, arbitrary trading-screen visuals and an unsupported recreation of a participant's exact portfolio.

Show how the financial constraint creates the turning point. Sequence alone is not proof of causality.

## Accuracy and interpretation

Trace consequential numbers, quotes, terms and causal claims to opened sources. Specify equity value versus enterprise value, notional versus capital, gross versus net and realized versus paper results.

Treat a quoted profit figure as a sourced estimate with its limitations, not a universal fact. Announced deals and completed deals are different. Avoid invented private conversations, objectives or deadlines.

Address the strongest competing explanation. A profitable outcome does not prove low risk; a forced unwind does not always prove the original thesis was economically wrong. Explain the distinction only when evidence supports it.

## Spoken delivery and visuals

Concrete, varied speech. Make numerical passages pronounceable and give the viewer time to follow diagrams. Keep exact source values outside narration where necessary.

## Payoff and CTA

Resolve the opening puzzle: which constraint, timing or mechanism decided the contest? Show the supported transfer of money/control and the remaining uncertainty.

Close on a specific insight about this event, not advice to imitate a leveraged bet or pick a current stock. One main CTA follows the answer, usually a related published story. Use a short channel invitation if no adjacent video exists.

## Internal review

Check positions/obligations, terms, funding, timeframe, units, accounting distinctions, turning-point causality, title promise, comprehension, counterevidence and tonal restraint.

Complete the shared story package with transaction/position map and event timeline in research/synthesis. Validate paragraph anchors and timing assumptions. Actual rendered runtime is verified during production.
