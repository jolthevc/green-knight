# Proposed financial-caper identity

Stylized financial stories on a clean midnight-blue canvas: cut-paper objects, simplified adult figures, brass accents, readable numbers and selective motion. Think a clear illustrated transaction unfolding on a tabletop.

This is a proposed pilot direction. No previously approved reference image, font kit or final visual system is assumed.

## Palette

| Color | Hex | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Midnight blue | #162636 | Main canvas and large scene backgrounds |
| Warm ivory | #F5F0E6 | Primary text, objects and lighter diagram panels |
| Brass | #C4A46A | Selected bid, transfer or turning-point accent |
| Muted teal | #70AAA5 | Supporting positions and paths |
| Brick | #B96050 | Pressure, obligation or opposing position |

Use warm ivory on midnight blue for the main reading pair. On light panels use midnight-blue text. Accents are restrained; verify contrast in the actual frame. Labels and shapes carry meaning alongside color. Avoid a red/green trading-screen wall.

## Typography

Barlow Condensed Semibold: short title cards and thumbnail headlines.
IBM Plex Sans Regular/Medium: explanations, captions and scene labels.
IBM Plex Mono Medium: prices, amounts and simple position tables.

Confirm tool availability and font licensing. Keep headings short and numbers editable. Avoid tiny financial-terminal text and long paragraphs. Typography and color values remain recommendations to test.

## Visual vocabulary

Contracts, ownership tiles, labeled counters, company tokens, simple maps, price lines, collateral blocks and simplified people. Show the transaction rather than decorative money raining down.

A miniature negotiation table can stage a takeover. A limited supply of labeled tokens can explain a squeeze. A clean two-party diagram can explain a position. Objects and scene composition follow the episode, not one compulsory poker table.

Avoid casino imagery that glamorizes risk or frames every trade as gambling. No permanent villain costumes, masks or fake crime scenes.

## Signature device: the position board

Track who holds what, what they owe, the constraint and what changes at the turning point. Build it progressively so the audience can follow one moving part at a time.

For deals, use ownership before/after and a funding bridge. For trades, use a simplified payoff or obligation diagram. Illustrative examples are labeled and cannot stand in for unavailable historical figures.

A small timeline or countdown is useful only when supported by the event. Do not invent exact deadlines or tickers for tension.

## Motion, data and evidence

Use deliberate transfers, swaps, changing counters and a momentary pause when a constraint binds. Let the meaning of a price movement register before cutting away.

Actual charts need correct scale, units, period and source. Keep a stylized conceptual curve distinct from historical data. Do not invent price paths, trading terminals, headlines or official documents as evidence. Verified inserts should remain legible and clearly sourced.

A dark scene can contain a light diagram panel. Keep the episode inviting and readable rather than uniformly gloomy. Maintain the same proportions, edge treatment, lighting and type hierarchy across scenes.

## Pilot and editor feedback

Request a 30–45 second integrated test with one position board, one transfer, one verified evidence insert and the proposed narrator. Include a title card and phone-size thumbnail preview.

Ask the editor to recommend the simplest sustainable implementation and push back on expensive animation or unreadable finance graphics. Reusable assets support consistency; each episode still requires original explanatory scenes.
