## Central mystery and answer

A McDonald's Monopoly winning piece could be completely genuine yet never have reached the player through the intended random distribution. McDonald's hired Simon Marketing to run its promotions. Jerome Jacobson, Simon's **director of security**, was responsible for disseminating the highest-value winning pieces and admitted stealing them. Government investigators described a recruiter/claimant network that presented authentic pieces and collected real advertised prizes; the FBI uncovered links with an anonymous tip and a monitored 2001 competition. Four other defendants' convictions were overturned, showing criminal knowledge cannot be assumed merely from ticket transfers.

**Appearance:** Official genuine prize redemption means someone won fair and square. **Reality:** An upstream security-insider diversion selected recipients before the public got a chance. **Proof:** DOJ/FBI investigations, 2004 federal appellate opinion, and 2007 California coverage appeal confirming Jacobson's job responsibilities.

## One clear financial mechanism

Claimant receives genuine payout → under the alleged 2001 scheme passes a share to an intermediary → some goes to Jacobson. Contemporary reporting gives an **alleged $70,000 portion from one $500,000 award**; it is **not** an audited formula or personal net total. Official 2001 **>$13m grand-prize values** are an accusation-period tally, not realized criminal proceeds. Some $1m annuity prizes were paid at $50,000 a year.

## Stakes and stage limits

Customers could lose a promised chance; McDonald's could lose promotion trust while a third-party specialist's authentic pieces were being misdirected. Company employees were not accused in the initial DOJ case and McDonald's cooperated. Jacobson's guilty plea and 37-month sentence do not make every redeemer culpable.
