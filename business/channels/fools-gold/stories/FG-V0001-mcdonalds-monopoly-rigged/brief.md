## Mystery and core promise

A customer had every reason to believe a game piece obtained through the official promotion could win a rare cash prize. Yet a trusted contractor's security employee diverted real winning pieces **before** their intended random distribution and routed them through recruiters to designated claimants. The counterfeit object was not a fake ticket. It was a false story of **provenance**, made believable by an authentic prize and an official payout. The FBI exposed the gap after a tip and a 2001 investigation; Jacobson later admitted the theft, while separate convictions of four people were overturned when knowledge of the stolen origins was not proved.

**Apparent reality:** ordinary customers had a chance at valuable Monopoly game pieces and genuine winners emerged across the country. **Evidence-backed reality:** rare top pieces were stolen out of contractor custody, with some prizes claimed by network-connected people. **Question:** What could McDonald's and its redemption system verify and what crucial point remained unverified?

## Limits and research caveats

The August 2001 Justice Department count of **more than $13 million in allegedly corruptly won grand prizes** was an initial-period face-value total, not cash profit or recovery and not definitive lifetime proceeds. Reuters/AP report Jacobson's guilty plea and 37-month sentence; his culpability is adjudicated differently from the four defendants named in `United States v. Chandler` (2004), whose convictions were vacated and judgments of acquittal ordered. Initial DOJ/FBI releases are accusation documents, not independent guilty verdicts. McDonald's cooperated with investigators and federal officials said no McDonald's employees were implicated.
