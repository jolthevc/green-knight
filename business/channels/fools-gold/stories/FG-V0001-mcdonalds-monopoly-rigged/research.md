## Timeline, event date versus report date

- **1987:** McDonald's Monopoly promotion launched according to retrospective coverage; exact origin is not essential to v1 narration.
- **1989 onward:** Reuters/AP January 2003 coverage states Jacobson admitted taking valuable game tickets back to 1989. Do not conflate this with DOJ's 2001 criminal complaint's **“as early as 1995”** prosecuted scheme timeframe.
- **1995 onward:** DOJ complaint allegations about recruitment/redemption network and increasingly valuable game pieces. Cases encompassed Monopoly, *Who Wants to Be a Millionaire*, and other McDonald's promotions.
- **By 2000:** Contemporary Washington Post reporting identifies anonymous informant reaching the Jacksonville FBI office; McDonald's informed May 2000, early tip initially met skepticism. Do **not** falsely say FBI first investigated in 1995 (a 2001 UPI report contains possible timeline shorthand).
- **July 11–August 9, 2001:** McDonald's contest proceeded with FBI investigation active. WaPo Aug 22 says John Davis claimed $1m Monopoly prize; government alleged recruiters connected the claim to corruptly diverted piece.
- **August 21, 2001:** DOJ/FBI announced **eight arrests** across states and called it Operation Final Answer, alleged more than **$13 million in grand-prize face value** corruptly redeemed. Initial release specifically cautioned that accusations did not constitute guilt. McDonald's cooperated.
- **September 2001:** Indictment broadened, reported by UPI. Not a finding against every named participant.
- **April 2002:** According to January 2003 Reuters article Jacobson pleaded guilty.
- **January 2003:** Contemporary Reuters/AP reports 37 months in federal prison; AP mentions a restitution order but figures differ between later reports, so script does not assert a final realized restitution recovery.
- **July/October 2004:** Reversal of four separate non-Jacobson defendants' convictions was reported July; substituted 11th Circuit published opinion dated October 19, 2004, `United States v. Chandler`, 388 F.3d 796. On rehearing government acknowledged lack of proof regarding membership in stolen-stamp conspiracy; four acquittals mandated. Distinguish reversed cases from other plea outcomes.

## Credibility and verification graph

Promised value: McDonald's widely marketed rare prizes -> printing/packaging chain -> Simon custody of scarce high-value pieces -> **integrity failure in trusted inside access** -> recruiter(s) -> apparent claimant -> real official prize payment -> part of payout allegedly passed backward along recruitment chain. 
The key weakness was that **object authenticity** was separable from **custody integrity**. Showing a genuine game ticket does not establish a path of legitimate random distribution. This does **not** establish that a specific overlooked obvious control would certainly have stopped Jacobson; the public record is insufficient to reconstruct every seal, auditor check, or supervision decision.

## Counterarguments and uncomfortable truths

1. **“McDonald's itself picked winners.”** Not established. Contemporary DOJ/FBI said McDonald's cooperated and no McDonald's staff were charged; theft occurred at an outsourced contractor. Simon Marketing entrusted with game security was distinct from McDonald's direct operating staff.
2. **“Every winning ticket was fake.”** False; the game pieces were genuine, and the deception concerned the path and claiming circumstances.
3. **“Every prize claimant knew tickets were stolen.”** False generalization, contradicted by the 2004 `Chandler` court's acquittal order for four appellants.
4. **“More than $13m was money in Jacobson's account.”** False denomination. DOJ 2001 alleged total prize face value, and prizes could be annuities with splits.
5. **“Scam was exclusively Monopoly.”** No; other McDonald's games were involved.
6. **“Every customer had zero chance at every prize.”** Too broad. The fraud targeted rare top pieces in covered games and periods; lower-value legitimate prizes still existed, and attribution of all top pieces is not proved by initial 2001 press announcement.
7. **“No one ever suspected anything.”** False; anonymous tip reached FBI by 2000 and McDonald's cooperated. The integrity issue was unmasked with investigation, not a miraculous blind guess.
8. **“A transfer necessarily means criminal intent.”** Legally incorrect for affected appellants; mere rule-breaking without proof of knowing theft did not sustain charged federal fraud conspiracy.
