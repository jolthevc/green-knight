TITLE: The Man Who Rigged McDonald's Monopoly

Imagine peeling a Monopoly sticker from a McDonald's drink. You might be one rare property away from a million dollars. But for years, some of the most valuable pieces never had a chance of reaching ordinary customers. The man responsible for protecting them was taking them first.

Here is what makes this case strange. A winner could hand over a real McDonald's game piece, have it verified, and receive a real prize. The ticket wasn't counterfeit. The missing part was how it had reached the person claiming it.

Most Monopoly pieces offered a small instant prize or an easy-to-find property. Only a handful were worth enormous cash awards or cars. McDonald's wanted those pieces scattered through the promotion so that customers had a genuine chance at the advertised jackpots.

Getting those rare tickets onto cups and into newspaper inserts required special custody. McDonald's hired an outside promotions company, Simon Marketing, to run the contests. Simon was supposed to protect the winning pieces until they were placed where customers could find them.

Jerome Jacobson was Simon's director of security. His job included distributing the high-value winning game pieces across the country. Think about that assignment. He wasn't merely standing near a locked cabinet. He was entrusted with getting the rarest prizes from the security process into the game itself.

And that gave him an extraordinary opportunity. Jacobson stole valuable pieces before their intended distribution. He could then decide who received a ticket that was supposed to reach an ordinary customer by chance. The paper was genuine; the route it traveled had changed.

Two things can be true at once. A ticket can be an authentic winner, and the person presenting it may not have obtained it through the game's intended distribution. Checking the first fact does not establish the second.

According to federal investigators, Jacobson gave pieces to friends and associates who acted as recruiters. They found people willing to submit the prizes in their own names. Instead of one suspicious insider repeatedly claiming jackpots, the published winners could appear to be different people in different states.

When McDonald's or Simon paid the prize, the government alleged that money flowed backward. A claimant kept some, passed some to a recruiter, and a portion made its way to Jacobson. The alleged deception used legitimate prize payments; it didn't require anyone to print fake million-dollar stickers.

One example from court papers, reported in August 2001, shows the incentive. From a five-hundred-thousand-dollar prize, investigators alleged that seventy thousand dollars was earmarked for Jacobson. The rest would be divided among the claimant, recruiter and taxes. That was one alleged split, not a formula for every prize.

Some million-dollar awards were paid over many years, rather than arriving as one giant check. So a winner's headline prize, the money actually received that year and the amount allegedly shared with the network were different things. A jackpot could be worth a million dollars without putting a million in anyone's pocket that afternoon.

The first public accusation covered far more than one winner. Prosecutors alleged the operation had run since at least 1995, across Monopoly and other McDonald's promotions. When the arrests came in 2001, officials said more than thirteen million dollars in advertised grand prizes had been corruptly claimed. That wasn't a measure of Jacobson's personal profit.

One early example showed what those networks could deliver. In a 1995 claim described by investigators, a winner received a Dodge Viper sports car through an intermediary in Georgia. Years later, federal officials described at least thirteen separate million-dollar tickets among the disputed claims. It wasn't one clever trick repeated at a single restaurant. It was a prize-distribution problem stretching across years, games and states.

And yet a million-dollar winner in another state was not, by itself, suspicious. Rare prizes were meant to turn up in unexpected places. The scheme hid behind the very improbability that made the game exciting. What eventually exposed it wasn't a fake sticker. It was a clue about the people receiving the real ones.

By 2000, an anonymous tip had reached the FBI's Jacksonville office. Contemporary reporting says agents initially viewed the information skeptically. They had to test whether the winners were simply strangers enjoying incredible luck, or whether a network connected them to someone who controlled the ticket supply.

That difference matters. A genuine game piece alone couldn't reveal who had touched it before the customer. But investigators could examine the links among claimants, recruiters and the promotions contractor. If the same connections kept appearing behind distant winners, random luck stopped being an adequate explanation.

McDonald's learned of the investigation in May 2000 and cooperated with federal authorities. Rather than cancelling every promotion immediately, it agreed to let a 2001 Monopoly game proceed while investigators watched a suspected attempt to divert a major prize. The contest now had an audience its participants didn't know about.

The 2001 game ran that summer. According to the FBI's account, a million-dollar winning piece ended up being claimed by John Davis, a man in Texas. Officials alleged that his route to the piece went through recruiters, including a man in South Carolina. Investigators could compare the claim with the relationships they had been tracing.

On August twenty-first, the Justice Department announced eight arrests across multiple states. It called the investigation Operation Final Answer. Jerome Jacobson was among those arrested. The discovery wasn't that McDonald's had printed counterfeit prizes. It was that the man charged with placing real top prizes had helped direct them away from the public.

McDonald's said it was a victim alongside its customers, and authorities said no McDonald's employees were implicated. The company ended its relationship with Simon Marketing and announced another giveaway. The bigger injury was to the promise that an ordinary customer might actually receive one of the rare pieces.

The case kept developing after that announcement. Jacobson pleaded guilty and, in January 2003, received a thirty-seven-month prison sentence. He admitted stealing high-value game pieces. His theft was no longer simply a theory presented in an arrest document.

But that didn't establish that everyone further down the chain knew the pieces were stolen. Four other men's conspiracy convictions were later overturned by a federal appeals court. The judges concluded prosecutors hadn't proved those defendants knowingly joined the charged scheme involving Jacobson's theft.

At trial, Jacobson testified that he had used ten recruiters and had kept them separate. According to the appellate opinion, he said they did not know one another or even about his theft. The network could be connected through him without everybody inside it understanding the whole arrangement. That became important when prosecutors tried to prove a single criminal conspiracy.

The court noted that people sometimes transferred legitimate Monopoly pieces to one another. Receiving a ticket from somebody else, or falling afoul of a contest rule, was not automatically proof of a criminal conspiracy. The judges ordered acquittals for those four men. That ruling did not overturn Jacobson's guilty plea.

It's an unusual legal ending to a straightforward theft. A stolen winning piece was real. A payout might be real. But the evidence needed to prove an individual recipient's criminal knowledge was a separate question. The authenticity of a ticket could not answer everything about the people involved.

Now return to that sticker on a McDonald's cup. The problem wasn't that the million-dollar prize had been invented. It was that the person trusted to place a scarce winning piece into the game could secretly decide where it went. The deception happened before the customer ever had a chance to play.
