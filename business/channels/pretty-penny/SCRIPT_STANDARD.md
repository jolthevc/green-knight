# Complete 8–10 minute script standard

## Narrative shape

Use this as a starting architecture, not a quota:

| Approximate window | Job |
| --- | --- |
| 0:00–0:25 | Familiar scene + specific contradiction + clear promise |
| 0:25–1:15 | First useful answer; orient the viewer without a history lecture |
| 1:15–3:00 | Follow the money with a concrete example |
| 3:00–5:30 | Add the hidden incentive and evidence; change the viewer's model |
| 5:30–7:30 | Test the explanation against a complication or counterargument |
| 7:30–9:30 | Resolve the central puzzle and show the implication |
| Final 5–20 seconds | Brief relevant next-video invitation when available |

Windows are estimates. Actual recordings determine timing; the total must be 8–10 minutes. Do not stretch a simple answer or speed-read an overcrowded one.

## Opening and retention

Prepare two or three hooks internally, then choose the strongest honest one. Begin with an observable situation, meaningful stakes and a question the viewer wants answered. No welcome, logo sequence, subscription request or generic “in today's world.”

The first 30 seconds must deliver the title/thumbnail expectation. Pay off some curiosity early. Each beat adds evidence, changes understanding, tests the model or answers a question. Remove loops that merely repeat “but there's more.” Keep an explicit promise/payoff map in the outline.

About every 30–60 seconds, ask whether understanding or stakes have progressed. This is a diagnostic, not a requirement to interrupt the explanation. Retention devices include contrast, a concrete calculation, a visual transformation, a surprising evidence-backed detail and a counterargument. Avoid unrelated jokes, arbitrary zooms and misleading suspense.

## Write for speech

Use clear sentences, varied rhythm, concrete verbs and short explanations of technical terms. Spell amounts and abbreviations as they should be read when that avoids TTS errors. Keep exact underlying figures in the research/source map. Avoid lists that sound like a slide deck, nested qualifications and a Wikipedia chronology.

Use “we” to guide a calculation, not to imply reporting we never did. Do not claim “we spoke to” or “we investigated” unless true. No fabricated scenes, dialogue, quotes, motives or false causal certainty.

## Substance

The script must reveal a mechanism: customer → intermediary → supplier/owner, incentives, costs, risk and profit where supported. At least one concrete example should let a viewer explain the mechanism back. A number without period, scope or meaningful comparison is usually not a useful number.

Do not turn the thesis into an unsupported hot take. Include the strongest relevant complication economically, without burying the viewer in caveats. Tie interpretation to evidence in the claim map.

## Ending

Answer the opening question. Show the most consequential implication without pretending to offer individualized financial advice. The viewer should leave with a changed understanding, not a recap of section headings.

Use one main CTA after the payoff, usually an adjacent published video. If none exists, a short specific channel invitation is sufficient. A genuine discussion question can be optional. Avoid a stack of requests and a drawn-out goodbye.

## One-go delivery and review

The full first draft is written at once after research and internal outlining. Then revise it internally before handoff. Final script.txt contains only what should be spoken, with blank lines between paragraphs. Sources, headings, timestamps, stage directions and cue tags belong elsewhere.

Review facts, causal reasoning, opening promise, early payoff, progression, pronunciation, repetition, production feasibility and estimate. A second review must test the thesis against the counterevidence, not just polish language.

Save the complete draft plus research, sources, synthesis, cues, visuals, package and QA. Clearly distinguish editorially approved wording from audio not yet produced or measured.
