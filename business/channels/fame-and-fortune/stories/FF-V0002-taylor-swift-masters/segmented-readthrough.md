## [B | THE PROMISE | TWO VERSIONS OF ONE SONG | approx 00:40–01:30 | NOT SPOKEN]

**VOICE (NON-SPOKEN):** Accessible and brisk, reveal the two types of ownership.

**VISUAL (NON-SPOKEN):** Sheet music divides into composition and master recording; two waveform tracks.

**PARAGRAPHS:** P004-P006

### SPOKEN NARRATION

The entire story turns on one distinction most people never think about. A song has an underlying composition: its melody and lyrics. It also has a particular recording: the sound you actually hear through your headphones.

The master recording can belong to a record label even when the singer wrote the song. That does not mean the artist can immediately remake it; contracts often impose waiting periods. But once those restrictions end, an artist with the necessary rights can create a new recording.

For most musicians, that possibility changes very little. Re-recording costs money, takes time, and asks listeners to care about a second version of something they already own. For Taylor Swift, those obstacles became the foundation of an extraordinary business strategy.

## [C | ACT I | THE DEAL SHE SIGNED AS A TEENAGER | approx 01:30–02:40 | NOT SPOKEN]

**VISUAL (NON-SPOKEN):** Big Machine, debut images, label investment, 2018 Republic deal.

**PARAGRAPHS:** P007-P010

### SPOKEN NARRATION

In 2005, before she was a global superstar, Swift signed with Big Machine Records. Her debut arrived the following year, followed by Fearless, Speak Now, Red, 1989 and Reputation.

Big Machine invested in launching and distributing her career. In return, under the recording agreements, it owned the masters for those first six albums. That arrangement was familiar in the music industry, where labels often supply money and infrastructure in exchange for long-term rights.

But as Swift became one of the biggest stars alive, the ownership question changed. The recordings were no longer simply products from the past. They were valuable assets that could keep earning through streaming, sales and licensing. Even years after an album release, a song can play in a television show or reach a brand-new generation of listeners. Someone still owns that recording, and someone still gets paid.

When she signed a new deal with Universal’s Republic Records in 2018, she secured ownership of the masters she would create going forward. Her future was hers to control. The first six albums, however, were still another company’s property.

### SPOKEN NARRATION

In June 2019, Scooter Braun’s Ithaca Holdings acquired Big Machine in a deal reported at more than three hundred million dollars. That price covered the label business, not just Taylor Swift’s recordings.

Swift said she had wanted to purchase her masters outright and had not been offered that chance on terms she could accept. Big Machine founder Scott Borchetta disputed her account, arguing she had been offered a path to ownership. Her side countered that the proposal required committing additional new albums to the label.

Those are competing accounts of a disputed negotiation, not proof that one side secretly committed a crime. But for Swift, the outcome was unmistakable. Someone else now owned the original recordings tied to the first thirteen years of her public career.

Then came another twist. In 2020, Braun’s company sold those masters to investment firm Shamrock Capital for a price widely reported above three hundred million dollars. The buyer had acquired a valuable catalog. Swift still did not own it.

And now she was preparing a move those buyers could not simply block forever.

## [E | ACT III | THE WORKAROUND THAT WAS NOT A LOOPHOLE | approx 03:50–05:15 | NOT SPOKEN]

**VOICE (NON-SPOKEN):** Aha moment; articulate two distinct recordings and rights.

**VISUAL (NON-SPOKEN):** Original master and Taylor-owned master compete for fans/licensing without destroying original royalties.

**PARAGRAPHS:** P016-P020

### SPOKEN NARRATION

Swift could not undo the old record deal with a social-media post. But she could create a new product with much of the same music once her re-recording restrictions expired.

Think of the original album and Taylor’s Version as two separate recordings of many of the same compositions. The lyrics remain familiar. The songs remain recognizable. But each recording has its own owner, its own earning potential and its own place on a streaming service.

That meant fans could choose which recording to hear. A movie or advertisement seeking to use a Swift song might also have a new version to license, subject to the necessary permissions. Picture a film producer wanting one of her biggest hits. Before, the original recording might have been the obvious choice. Now there could be a recording Swift owned herself. Suddenly, the owner of the old master no longer had the only recorded option.

The original recordings did not vanish, and their owners could still earn from them. It would be wrong to claim Taylor destroyed their value. What she did was create credible competition, backed by the one audience advantage no investment firm could purchase separately: the fans who wanted to support her.

But a clever strategy is not a victory until people actually choose the replacement.

Fans were not merely tolerating substitute recordings. They were buying, streaming and celebrating them at blockbuster scale. But there is an important limit to the headline. Huge Taylor’s Version sales do not tell us exactly how much money the original owners lost, or whether their investment ultimately performed poorly. Those private economics are not fully public.

The more defensible conclusion is also more interesting: Swift had proved that she could recreate commercially valuable versions of her own work, without controlling the old masters.

Then, just when that seemed like the ending, the story turned again.

### SPOKEN NARRATION

On May 30, 2025, Swift announced something her fans had waited years to hear. She had purchased the original masters of her first six albums from Shamrock Capital. Her announcement also covered related videos, artwork, concert films and unreleased material.

The exact contract price was not publicly disclosed. Billboard reported a figure around three hundred sixty million dollars, citing sources, but that is a reported estimate, not an official purchase price.

Perhaps the most surprising detail is how Swift described the seller. She thanked Shamrock for dealing with her honestly and respectfully. This was not a courtroom verdict or a Hollywood revenge scene. It was a negotiated sale that put the original rights back in the artist’s hands.

Swift credited her fans and the success of the Eras Tour with helping make the purchase possible. And she had already put out four re-recorded albums. The new recordings had created a competing business. The final transaction gave her control of the old one too. In her letter, she even explained that she had not finished re-recording Reputation. After the buyback, the remaining releases no longer had to carry the burden of reclaiming what she did not own. They could simply be music.

We cannot prove that the re-recordings forced Shamrock to sell at a particular price. But we can see how Swift changed her position: she went from asking for ownership to building valuable alternatives, then acquiring the asset she had wanted all along.

### SPOKEN NARRATION

This is why the Taylor Swift story is bigger than a celebrity feud. It exposes a strange truth about creative businesses. Making something famous, writing it, performing it and owning its most valuable rights are not necessarily the same thing.

Big Machine made an investment in a teenage musician. Later owners saw a catalog worth hundreds of millions. Swift saw the recordings of her own life. Each position makes sense on its own terms, but only one person could convincingly make the music again and bring millions of listeners with her.

She did not erase the originals. She built alternatives, turned their release into events, and ultimately bought the originals back.

The real power move was not recording the same songs twice. It was making sure someone else no longer had the only version of her past.
