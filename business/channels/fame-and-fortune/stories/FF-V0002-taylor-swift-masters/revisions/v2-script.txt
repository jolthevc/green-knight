TITLE: They Bought Taylor Swift's Music. She Recorded It Again.

In 2019, investors bought control of Taylor Swift's original recordings. Four years later, she released a competing version of one of those albums. It moved more than 1.6 million album-equivalent units in the United States in its first week.

Think about that. Someone owned the hits that made her famous. And she found a way to make those hits into new products they didn't own.

The story ends with Swift buying her original recordings back. But the truly extraordinary part is what happened in between. How do you compete against the owner of your own music?

Here's the secret hiding in almost every song you hear. The song itself, its melody and lyrics, is one kind of intellectual property. The actual recording on Spotify is another. They can have different owners.

A label may own the master even when the singer wrote the song. And owning the master means controlling the use of that particular recording, not necessarily every future performance of the composition.

Artists can't always re-record immediately. Their contracts may require them to wait. But once Swift's restrictions expired, the owner of her original masters faced a possibility most music investors never seriously have to worry about: the star making a rival version, and millions of fans choosing it.

Back in 2005, Swift signed with a new record label, Big Machine. It helped bring a teenage country singer to market. Over the next decade came six albums, from Taylor Swift to Reputation, and one of the biggest careers in modern music.

Big Machine owned the original masters. That was not an unusual arrangement. Labels often spend heavily on production, marketing and distribution, then hold the recordings as assets that can pay out for decades.

But success changed the stakes. A song released when Swift was sixteen could still earn money when she was thirty. In 2018, when she signed a new record deal with Universal, she negotiated ownership of the new masters she would create. Her future recordings were hers. Her first six albums were not.

And then the company that owned them was sold.

In June 2019, Scooter Braun's Ithaca Holdings acquired Big Machine in a deal widely reported to be worth more than three hundred million dollars. That was a price for the record-label business, not simply for Swift's six albums.

Swift publicly objected. She said she wanted to purchase her masters outright and had not been offered a way to do that on terms she could accept. Big Machine founder Scott Borchetta disputed her account, arguing that a route to ownership had been offered. Her representatives disputed what that route required.

Later reporting complicated the picture further. Music Business Worldwide examined emails and confidentiality agreements indicating that Swift's representatives and Braun's company discussed a possible sale in 2019 and 2020. There was a potential deal. The parties disagreed over terms and protections, and it did not happen.

In 2020, Braun's company instead sold Swift's masters to investment firm Shamrock Capital in another transaction reported to be worth hundreds of millions. To Shamrock, the catalog was a financial asset. To Swift, it was a recording of her own history. And for the moment, the two sides couldn't agree on who should own it.

So Swift built an alternative instead of waiting for the original owners to sell. After her contractual restrictions ended, she returned to the studio and recorded her old songs again.

A Taylor's Version track is not a remaster with a fresh cover. It is a newly performed recording of a familiar song. The original master remains owned by its existing rights holder. The new master belongs to Swift.

That distinction created a choice. On a streaming service, fans could play the old recording or support the new one. And when a film or advertisement wanted to license an actual recording, there was now another option, although using the composition could still require separate permissions.

Swift could not delete the originals or stop their owners from earning money. Nor could anyone simply assume the old catalog became worthless. But she could compete for attention, streams, sales and some licensing opportunities using music she controlled.

And she had an advantage a normal startup would pay almost anything to acquire: an audience personally invested in the outcome. That audience, however, still had to show up.

In 2021 came Fearless (Taylor's Version), then Red. In 2023 came Speak Now and 1989. Each release was treated not as a repeat but as an event: new cover art, physical editions, unreleased vault tracks, and the familiar songs recorded again.

Then 1989 (Taylor's Version) delivered the proof. In its first U.S. week, it earned 1.653 million equivalent album units, according to Billboard and Luminate. Of those, 1.359 million were actual album sales. The re-recording sold more copies in its opening week than the original 1989 had sold in its own opening week nine years earlier.

That is the reveal investors could hardly have modeled: a nine-year-old album effectively getting another blockbuster launch. Fans weren't settling for an inferior substitute. They were choosing an experience that let them revisit the music while supporting the artist's ownership effort.

Did the original owners lose a precise amount because of those sales? Public records don't tell us. And the Taylor's Versions weren't the only thing driving Swift's extraordinary popularity. But the commercial premise was now undeniable: she could make valuable new recordings out of songs associated with somebody else's catalog.

The re-recordings gave her a business she could control. The next twist was that she would not have to choose between those recordings and the originals after all.

On May thirtieth, 2025, Swift announced that she had purchased the original masters of her first six albums from Shamrock Capital. The transaction also covered related music videos, album art, concert films and unreleased material. The financial terms were not disclosed.

Remember the earlier conflict over whether Swift could buy back her music? Here is the beautiful complication. In her announcement, she thanked Shamrock for dealing with her fairly and respectfully. This was not a verdict against the former owners or proof that the re-recordings forced a sale. It was a negotiated transaction.

Swift credited her fans and the Eras Tour's success with helping make the purchase possible. And there was another surprising admission. She had already finished re-recording her debut album, but had made little progress on Reputation. Now, she said, any future release of those recordings could simply be a celebration, not a campaign to recover what she didn't own.

Six years after the first sale, the two businesses finally met in one place. Swift owned the new versions. And she had acquired the old ones.

It's tempting to tell this as a story about defeating the people who owned her recordings. But the more interesting lesson is about leverage.

The record label had made an early investment. Later buyers saw a valuable catalog. Swift understood something they couldn't separately buy from her: the willingness of an enormous audience to follow her into a second version of the same music.

She didn't rewrite the original contract. She didn't make the original recordings disappear. She created a new set of assets, proved there was demand for them, and eventually bought the first set back.

For artists, the distinction between creating a hit and owning its master has never looked more consequential. For investors, the story shows how vulnerable an asset can become when its value depends on a person who can create a compelling alternative.

They bought the recordings that made Taylor Swift famous. What they couldn't buy was the only artist who could make those recordings famous all over again.
