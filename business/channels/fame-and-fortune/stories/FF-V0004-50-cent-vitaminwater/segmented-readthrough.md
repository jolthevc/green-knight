### SPOKEN NARRATION

Go back to 2004. 50 Cent is everywhere. His debut album has made him a superstar, and his image is helping sell everything from sneakers to clothing.

At a growing beverage company called Glacéau, marketer Rohan Oza spots something interesting: 50 Cent appears with a bottle of Vitaminwater in a Reebok advertisement. According to contemporary reporting, he already drinks the stuff. This is not a celebrity awkwardly discovering a product five minutes before a photoshoot.

Oza sees a chance to reach millions of people who might never notice a niche wellness drink. But 50 Cent has a manager, Chris Lighty, who sees a different opportunity.

In an account later published by The Washington Post, Lighty pushes back on the idea of a conventional endorsement. He wants to invest. Instead of getting paid only for a commercial, he wants a piece of the company.

That sounds obvious after the company sells for billions. In 2004, it is a genuine gamble. The ownership could become enormously valuable. Or it could become very little.



## C | THE DEAL WAS BIGGER THAN A CELEBRITY | 01:45-02:50 | P009-P012 | NON-SPOKEN

### SPOKEN NARRATION

Vitaminwater was already a real business. Darius Bikoff had founded Glacéau in the 1990s. Mike Repole was helping turn it into a beverage company with serious distribution. Oza was building its marketing operation.

And what they were selling was easy to recognize. Brightly colored bottles. Flavors with distinctive names. A pitch built around vitamins and an active lifestyle. None of that meant the drink was a medical miracle. It meant the brand could stand out in a refrigerator full of nearly identical alternatives.

But a recognizable bottle still needs shelf space. Stores need a reason to stock it. People need a reason to try it. And once they try it, they need to buy it again.

A massive recording artist can generate attention. He cannot personally deliver bottles to thousands of stores. Glacéau already had people working on that part. What the partnership promised was to connect the two.

### SPOKEN NARRATION

Then comes the detail that makes this story feel almost scripted. Glacéau had been developing a drink whose formula provided fifty percent of the recommended daily amount of several vitamins. Its marketing team already had a name: Formula 50.

Now imagine presenting that product to a rapper literally named 50 Cent. The name was an astonishing fit. In October 2004, Glacéau announced its partnership with him and the new grape-flavored Formula 50.

The grape flavor was no accident either. In a detailed account of the negotiations, Lighty argued for grape because it connected the product to the drinks he and 50 Cent knew growing up. The result was a Vitaminwater flavor that felt tied to the artist rather than an anonymous celebrity sticker on a bottle.

Soon his image appeared in promotions. Vitaminwater turned up in his music and videos. A fan could recognize Formula 50 before knowing much about the company that made it.

But one detail remained hidden behind the advertising: 50 Cent had taken a minority ownership interest. The bottle was the visible part. The stake was the bet.

### SPOKEN NARRATION

Three years later, the bet reaches its most dramatic moment. On May twenty-fifth, 2007, Coca-Cola announces a four point one billion dollar agreement to acquire Glacéau.

Not just Formula 50. Not even just Vitaminwater. The whole business: Vitaminwater, Smartwater, Fruitwater, Vitaminenergy, its employees, its distribution relationships, and its place in a fast-growing beverage category.

Why would Coca-Cola spend that kind of money? Because the giant behind Coke and Sprite wanted more growth outside traditional carbonated soft drinks. Glacéau had already made enhanced water culturally relevant. Coca-Cola believed its distribution network could make the business much bigger.

The acquisition closes in June. Glacéau’s founders and operators have built something that attracts one of the world’s biggest buyers. And 50 Cent owns a slice of the company being sold.

Then comes the headline that changes everything. Reports suggest he has made four hundred million dollars. It sounds spectacular. It is also where the story gets mathematically messy.

### SPOKEN NARRATION

The four-hundred-million figure came from multiplying the entire sale price by a rumored ten percent ownership stake. But that was not a verified picture of his eventual proceeds.

In 2008, Forbes reported that 50 Cent had made about one hundred million dollars after taxes from the Glacéau sale. A later account by business-of-hip-hop author Dan Charnas placed estimates in a range of roughly sixty to one hundred million.

Either way, we are talking about an extraordinary amount of money. But those are reported estimates, not a publicly released closing statement showing his precise final payment. We cannot independently establish his exact ownership percentage or take-home amount from Coca-Cola’s acquisition documents.

The crucial distinction is simple: four point one billion was the price of the company. It was not the price of 50 Cent’s stake. And the rumor that he owned a tenth of the whole thing should not be presented as proven.

Now rewind to that first choice in 2004. A normal endorsement could have earned him money up front. A minority stake let him participate if the entire company became worth vastly more. That is why this partnership produced such a famous outcome.

### SPOKEN NARRATION

And this was not just one celebrity having a clever idea in isolation. Lighty helped negotiate the ownership structure. Oza understood how to make a product culturally relevant. Bikoff, Repole and their team had built the product and distribution that made the equity worth anything at all.

The parties were betting on each other. Glacéau would get the credibility and reach of a superstar who actually used its drink. 50 Cent would get exposure to the success of the whole company instead of only a one-time advertising fee.

That is also why the lesson is not simply “always take equity.” Private company shares can become worthless. They may be difficult to sell. A flashy brand can disappear long before a global buyer comes calling.

Here, the pieces came together at an unusually fortunate moment: a fast-growing beverage category, a distinctive product, a strong operating team, a gifted marketer, a powerful celebrity partner and a buyer prepared to pay billions.

Without the business, the endorsement is just attention. Without the ownership, the celebrity helps create value that may belong almost entirely to someone else.

### SPOKEN NARRATION

So what made 50 Cent rich from water? It was not a secret ingredient. It was not a magic marketing slogan. And it was not collecting a four-point-one-billion-dollar check.

It was the decision to put the value of his attention into an ownership stake, and to work with a company that could turn attention into bottles people actually bought.

A single drink in a sneaker ad introduced the relationship. Formula 50 made the partnership visible. Coca-Cola’s acquisition gave that invisible minority stake a chance to become a fortune.

The most valuable part of 50 Cent’s Vitaminwater deal was never the name printed on the bottle. It was his place among the people who owned the company behind it.

The bottle made him a spokesman. The contract made him an owner.
