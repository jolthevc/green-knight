The transaction closed in June. So where did 50 Cent fit into that mountain of money? That's where the story gets both more interesting and less certain.

### SPOKEN NARRATION

Most celebrity partnerships are designed to borrow credibility. A company pays for a recognizable face. The face appears in ads. Then the deal ends and the company keeps whatever business was built.

This one aligned their incentives differently. Glacéau wanted someone who could make an unknown drink feel culturally relevant. 50 Cent wanted more than another check. The reported equity arrangement meant their interests could rise together if the brand succeeded.

There was real risk in that bet. A minority stake in a private beverage company is not cash. It's difficult to sell, its value can fall, and not every fashionable drink ends up in a Coca-Cola acquisition. You only hear about the spectacular exits, not all the celebrity investments that never pay off.

And there were plenty of other people who earned that outcome: the founders who created the products, the salespeople who persuaded stores to carry them, the team that operated the company, and the investors who financed its growth.

50 Cent's role wasn't to replace that machine. It was to put his cultural reach into it, with a contract that let him participate in the result.
