## [NON-SPOKEN] A. COLD OPEN / HOOK | approx 00:00-00:40

**Voice direction, NOT SPOKEN:** Immediate, incredulous, measured.

**Visual direction, NOT SPOKEN:** Forbes billionaire headline -> authentic Coty $600M announcement -> Forbes reversal, three fast verified visual beats.

**Paragraphs:** P001-P003.

### SPOKEN NARRATION ONLY

In March 2019, Forbes called Kylie Jenner the youngest self-made billionaire in history. Then she agreed to a beauty deal worth six hundred million dollars. And just months after that deal closed, Forbes took the billionaire title away.

Read that again. A major beauty company had agreed to pay a staggering amount for control of Kylie's business. The transaction looked like proof that her fortune was real. But in the paperwork behind it, Forbes saw numbers that told a very different story.

So how can a six-hundred-million-dollar deal make someone's billionaire status less believable? To find out, we have to follow one lip kit, one spectacular business agreement, and two versions of the same financial story.

## [NON-SPOKEN] B. PROMISE / THE TWO NUMBERS | approx 00:40-01:40

**Voice direction, NOT SPOKEN:** Urgent investigative curiosity, first definite reveal.

**Visual direction, NOT SPOKEN:** Display 2019 Forbes estimated $360M 2018 sales beside Coty Nov 2019 $177M trailing-twelve-month estimated net revenue; label differing periods/sources.

**Paragraphs:** P004-P006.

### SPOKEN NARRATION ONLY

Here's the first clue. In 2019, Forbes reported an estimated three hundred sixty million dollars of Kylie Cosmetics revenue for 2018. Later that year, Coty, the buyer, presented an estimate of only one hundred seventy-seven million dollars of revenue for the twelve months leading up to its deal.

Those are not identical reporting periods, and Coty's figure was itself an estimate. But its presentation also described growth of more than forty percent compared with 2018. That combination led Forbes to question the much larger numbers behind its own billionaire calculation.

And Kylie publicly disputed the resulting article. So this is not a story about a court finding that someone lied. It is a story about a booming brand, a real nine-figure transaction, and what happens when a celebrity wealth estimate meets corporate disclosures. The strangest part is that the company at the center of it was genuinely impressive.

## [NON-SPOKEN] C. ACT I / A LIP KIT AND A BUILT-IN AUDIENCE | approx 01:40-04:00

**Voice direction, NOT SPOKEN:** Lively and admiring of real work, build momentous growth.

**Visual direction, NOT SPOKEN:** Kylie’s early makeup photos, genuine 2015 launch pages, licensed packaging, Seed Beauty reporting, Shopify/direct checkout, Ulta storefront.

**Paragraphs:** P007-P013.

### SPOKEN NARRATION ONLY

Go back to 2015. Kylie Jenner was already famous before she had a business to sell. Millions of people watched her life, her clothes, her makeup, her every new look. For an ordinary cosmetics startup, attention is brutally expensive. You need stores to give you shelf space, advertising to persuade customers to notice you, and enough capital to keep people coming back.

Kylie started with something different: she already had the crowd. The product was unusually specific. A twenty-nine-dollar lip kit, matching liquid lipstick and liner, built around the look people associated with her. The first launch sold out almost immediately. A tiny product had become a cultural event.

That matters because selling makeup and creating demand for makeup are two very different jobs. A manufacturer can make a good lipstick. A retailer can put it on a shelf. But getting hundreds of thousands of people to care about a particular lipstick at precisely the same time? That is expensive, uncertain, and extremely hard to manufacture.

Kylie's attention supplied the missing ingredient. A new shade became a social post. A post became a rush to buy. A sellout became another story people wanted to share. The product didn't simply ride on the celebrity. The launch itself gave the celebrity another reason to be watched. It was a reinforcing loop.

And Kylie did not build a factory empire from scratch. Seed Beauty manufactured and packaged products; outside partners handled important operational work. This is not a criticism. It is one of the most interesting parts of the business. The valuable asset was not a warehouse full of machines. It was the connection between a recognizable face and customers willing to purchase immediately.

By 2018, another piece had arrived: Ulta Beauty. The company that had thrived on the internet was suddenly selling in physical stores. Forbes later cited an analyst estimate of more than fifty-four million dollars of Ulta sales in six weeks. More customers could finally see, touch and try the product.

But a new question was forming underneath all that excitement. If the brand could sell out online and fly off store shelves, just how big was the actual business? And how much of the value came from Kylie herself, rather than from the companies manufacturing, stocking and delivering the products?

## [NON-SPOKEN] D. ACT II / THE $600 MILLION DEAL | approx 04:00-06:30

**Voice direction, NOT SPOKEN:** Escalating high-stakes, lucid on transaction terms.

**Visual direction, NOT SPOKEN:** Nov 18 2019 Coty headline -> $600M cash -> 51% stake -> $1.176B implied valuation -> licensing rights and distribution map.

**Paragraphs:** P014-P021.

### SPOKEN NARRATION ONLY

Enter Coty. Not a mysterious investor, but an established international beauty company with experience in fragrances, cosmetics, manufacturing and distribution. In November 2019, Coty and Kylie announced that it would buy a fifty-one-percent interest in the beauty partnership for six hundred million dollars.

Pause on that number. Six hundred million dollars for just over half. Divide the price by fifty-one percent and you get an implied value of about one point eighteen billion dollars for the whole partnership. This was not someone guessing her company's worth on social media. A real publicly traded buyer had signed a deal at that price.

And the deal was not just a pile of cash handed over in exchange for a logo. Coty would gain majority ownership and extensive licensing rights. The parties described a division of labor: Kylie and her team would lead the creative side and communications; Coty would bring industrial capacity, global distribution, research and product development.

Why would Coty want this so badly? In the company's own November presentation, it highlighted more than two hundred seventy million followers across Kylie's personal and brand social accounts. Those followers were not two hundred seventy million paying customers. But they represented a potentially extraordinary way to launch products without starting from zero.

Coty also presented a business with estimated trailing-twelve-month revenue of one hundred seventy-seven million dollars, and an EBITDA margin above twenty-five percent. EBITDA is a rough operating-earnings measure before interest, taxes and several other costs. Even on those smaller revenue figures, Coty was seeing a strong brand with attractive potential economics.

So the buyer could make a reasonable strategic argument. What if the brand's greatest limitation wasn't demand, but how far the products could travel? What if a global beauty network could take a social-media-native company into more stores and more countries? The price was a bet on that expansion, not just on the sales already made.

On January sixth, 2020, the transaction closed. Kylie still had an ongoing commercial role; Coty had majority ownership. The headline said six hundred million dollars. For most people, that sounded like the end of the question about her fortune.

Except it was really the beginning. Because Coty had to explain its purchase to investors. And those explanations put a second set of numbers in public view.

## [NON-SPOKEN] E. ACT III / THE SALES THAT DID NOT FIT | approx 06:30-09:30

**Voice direction, NOT SPOKEN:** Slow for each financial contrast, tension from evidence rather than accusation.

**Visual direction, NOT SPOKEN:** 2018 Forbes original estimate highlighted $360M -> Coty investor slide $177M TTM and >40% growth -> implied ~$125M 2018 comparison, clearly source-qualified; Kylie Skin $25M projection; dates and units always on screen.

**Paragraphs:** P022-P029.

### SPOKEN NARRATION ONLY

Remember the three-hundred-sixty-million-dollar sales estimate that Forbes had published for 2018? That figure was a major building block in its billionaire story. A business generating that kind of revenue could plausibly command a very large valuation, especially when a famous founder appeared to own all of it.

Now look at Coty's presentation. Around one hundred seventy-seven million dollars in estimated revenue over the preceding twelve months. And growth above forty percent versus calendar 2018. If the later period was more than forty percent higher, the comparable 2018 base would be roughly one hundred twenty-five million dollars, using forty percent as an illustrative calculation.

One hundred twenty-five million. Three hundred sixty million. Both were associated with the same celebrity beauty empire and with the same year, but they were radically different accounts of its scale. Even allowing for differences in reporting periods, net versus other sales measures, and company scope, the gap was too big for Forbes to ignore.

And there was another discrepancy involving Kylie Skin. Forbes reported that Kylie's representatives had previously described enormous early skincare sales. Coty's presentation instead described the newer skincare business as on track for about twenty-five million dollars in 2019 sales. One figure was a claim about an early launch period; the other was a full-year projection. Forbes nonetheless regarded the accounts as sharply inconsistent.

Here is the important distinction. Public deal materials tell us what Coty represented to its investors. They do not, by themselves, prove that anyone fabricated an earlier number or intentionally misled a reporter. The original estimates also weren't published on perfectly identical bases. What they did provide was a much more concrete benchmark than a celebrity fortune interview.

Why does any of this matter? Because a person's net worth is not the same thing as the size of a company. If I tell you I own a business worth nine hundred million dollars, you have to ask how that number was calculated. Was it based on verified revenue? Was it based on a multiple of profits? Are there partners or trusts? Does the founder own all of it? And how much money has already been taken out?

Forbes had presented Kylie as a billionaire in March 2019. But the Coty documents forced it to revisit several assumptions at once. The mystery was no longer just whether her company was impressive. Clearly, somebody had paid an enormous sum for it. The mystery was what portion of that company was truly hers, and whether the old arithmetic had ever been sound.

Then, in May 2020, the disagreement moved from a financial model into full public view.

## [NON-SPOKEN] F. ACT IV / FORBES TAKES THE TITLE BACK | approx 09:30-12:10

**Voice direction, NOT SPOKEN:** Controversial but fair; weight on disputed claims, never sneering.

**Visual direction, NOT SPOKEN:** May 29 2020 Forbes headline labeled as Forbes reporting -> Kylie's contemporaneous reply -> 51/49 split and rough ownership / gross versus net explained, without falsely attributing sale proceeds.

**Paragraphs:** P030-P037.

### SPOKEN NARRATION ONLY

On May twenty-ninth, Forbes published a new investigation and reversed its earlier billionaire assessment. Its headline was confrontational. The publication alleged that financial representations associated with the Jenner family had painted an inflated picture of Kylie Cosmetics, and it made the serious allegation that tax documents it had seen were likely forged.

That last point is an allegation by Forbes, not a proven legal finding. We should not confuse a magazine's investigation with a criminal charge, a court decision, or an independent forensic audit. The public record does not justify pretending those things are the same.

Kylie responded publicly and forcefully. She disputed the article's accuracy, rejected what she called unproven assumptions and said she had never tried to misrepresent her way into a title. She also challenged the suggestion about forged tax returns. Her response matters: the person at the center of the story did not accept Forbes' account.

But separate from that argument, there is a mathematical question that is fascinating in its own right. Suppose a buyer pays six hundred million dollars for fifty-one percent of a company. Does that mean the celebrity founder now has six hundred million in her personal bank account? No. The purchaser paid the selling parties. The final amount belonging to one person depends on ownership, taxes, deal structure and other details.

And what about the part not sold? Forty-nine percent of a business valued at about one point eighteen billion dollars is worth roughly five hundred seventy-six million dollars on that transaction's implied valuation. That is not automatically one person's stake, and it is not a liquid pile of money. You cannot simply add the full sale price and the full retained value and declare the founder worth more than a billion.

Forbes' revised analysis estimated that Kylie personally held a smaller remaining percentage than its earlier work had assumed, and that her take-home cash from the deal was substantially less than the six-hundred-million-dollar headline. Forbes estimated roughly three hundred forty million dollars after tax from the transaction, while also accounting for declining beauty-company valuations during the pandemic.

Those personal proceeds and ownership estimates are Forbes' calculations, not figures we can independently verify from the public acquisition price alone. That distinction is crucial. The verified acquisition, the buyer's revenue disclosures, the journalist's interpretation and the subject's rebuttal are four different kinds of information.

The public argument was loud. But once you strip away the volume, the deeper business question is more interesting than who won the headline battle. Did Coty pay too much? Or had Kylie built something that traditional financial statements could not fully capture?

## [NON-SPOKEN] G. ACT V / WHAT COTY WAS ACTUALLY BUYING | approx 12:10-14:35

**Voice direction, NOT SPOKEN:** Reframe, measured optimism and sharp insight, no premature victory claim.

**Visual direction, NOT SPOKEN:** Brand-to-distribution chain, 2021 reformulation/Ulta/global retail, 2025 Coty 1.5x quote, 2026 fragrance release; distinguish corporate claims from independent sales figures.

**Paragraphs:** P038-P046.

### SPOKEN NARRATION ONLY

Consider the deal from Coty's side. A traditional beauty company needs a constant stream of products that people want, a distribution network to move them, and a reason for customers to choose those products over a hundred alternatives. Kylie brought an unusually powerful version of that third ingredient.

Coty brought much of the machinery needed for the first two. In a conventional licensing arrangement, a star might collect a fee or royalty while somebody else bears the burden of making and selling the product. Here, the partnership included equity, majority ownership and long-term rights. That structure was part of why the deal was so consequential.

Importantly, a high sale price does not prove that every past revenue claim was accurate. Buyers can pay large multiples for brands they believe will grow. Coty was making a commercial bet on future distribution, product categories and continued cultural relevance. The share price was evidence of what a buyer was willing to pay, not a certified audit of every earlier press estimate.

And the business did not disappear after the controversy. In 2021, Coty announced a relaunch of Kylie Cosmetics with reformulated products, new packaging, an expanded online store and wider international retail distribution. It later pursued skincare, baby products and fragrances with Kylie's name attached.

In March 2025, Coty publicly said Kylie Cosmetics had grown by one and a half times over the preceding two years. That was a company statement, not a disclosed complete set of standalone accounts. But it is important evidence against the simplistic idea that the billionaire controversy ended the beauty business.

By 2026, Coty was still describing Kylie Cosmetics as one of the brands anchoring its prestige portfolio, alongside other established fragrance names. It was still selling and developing products tied to her audience. That is a very different ending from a vanished empire.

Does this prove Coty earned a brilliant return on its six-hundred-million-dollar investment? No. That requires far more: cash flows, integration costs, investment over time, and the value of the stake today. The public brand updates are not enough to calculate a definitive return.

But it does reveal why the whole episode is so unusual. The company's value was never only about tubes of lipstick. It was also about distribution, an identity people recognized, products people would buy, and the possibility of taking an online audience into a global retail system.

That leaves us with a surprising final distinction. The billionaire label was an estimate made by a magazine. The cosmetics company was a real operating business. A headline could be revised in one morning. The brand still had customers, partners, products and decisions to make the next day.

## [NON-SPOKEN] H. CLOSER / THE PRICE OF A HEADLINE | approx 14:35-15:30

**Voice direction, NOT SPOKEN:** Patient, poignant, final insight.

**Visual direction, NOT SPOKEN:** Return to three-image opening, replace 'BILLIONAIRE?' with 'WHAT WAS REALLY OWNED?' then close on single lip kit photo.

**Paragraphs:** P047-P050.

### SPOKEN NARRATION ONLY

So did Kylie Jenner lose hundreds of millions of dollars when Forbes took away the billionaire title? Not because the headline changed. Forbes changed its estimate. The Coty deal had already happened. Ownership, taxes, cash and future business performance remained more complicated than a magazine cover could ever show.

And it would be just as misleading to say the whole business was imaginary. Kylie turned enormous public attention into a company somebody paid six hundred million dollars to control. That is a remarkable commercial achievement, regardless of which side of a billion-dollar line a wealth estimate lands.

The twist is that the deal that appeared to confirm the most famous number in her life also brought forward the information that made people question it. The six-hundred-million-dollar transaction did not erase her fortune. It changed what outsiders could examine.

Because the most valuable thing in this story was never the billionaire title. It was the difference between being famous, being valuable, and actually owning the value.
