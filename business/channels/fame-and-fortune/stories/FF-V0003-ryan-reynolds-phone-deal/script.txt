TITLE: Ryan Reynolds' $1.35B Phone Deal

Ryan Reynolds invested in a phone company that didn't own its own cellphone towers. Four years later, T-Mobile agreed to buy its parent business for up to one point three five billion dollars.

But here's the strange part. Mint Mobile already ran on T-Mobile's network. T-Mobile was buying a company that sold phone plans using T-Mobile's own towers.

So what was the carrier really paying for? And how did the guy who plays Deadpool end up owning part of the answer?

First, one number needs decoding. The one point three five billion dollars was the maximum announced price for Ka'ena, a group that included Mint Mobile, Ultra Mobile and a wholesale business called Plum. It wasn't Ryan Reynolds' personal payout, and it wasn't a price for Mint alone.

Reynolds was an investor and a recognizable face for Mint. Founders David Glickman and Rizwan Kassim had built and operated the wireless business. His role was something different: turning a phone plan, one of the least exciting purchases imaginable, into something people wanted to talk about.

And he'd already practiced that trick with a product you would never expect to lead to a phone company. Gin.

In 2018, Reynolds became a co-owner of Aviation American Gin. His marketing partner, George Dewey, helped build a creative operation called Maximum Effort. Their ads felt less like commercials and more like jokes someone might voluntarily send to a friend.

In December 2019, a Peloton holiday commercial had become a huge online conversation. Reynolds' team quickly recruited Monica Ruiz, the actress from that ad, for an Aviation Gin follow-up. She appeared at a bar with friends, silently processing the moment. The internet understood the joke immediately.

The remarkable thing wasn't just that the ad was funny. It was how quickly Maximum Effort connected a brand to something people were already discussing. Instead of interrupting the conversation, the brand became part of it.

Then came a very real deal. In 2020, Diageo agreed to buy Aviation Gin and the wider Davos Brands portfolio for as much as six hundred ten million dollars: three hundred thirty-five million upfront, plus possible additional payments tied to Aviation's performance.

That figure covered several brands. Reynolds was one of Aviation's co-owners, not the recipient of the whole amount. But the transaction showed why an actor might prefer a stake in a product over a one-time fee to endorse it.

In November 2019, Reynolds took an ownership stake in Mint Mobile. He described the investment as unconventional. Celebrities normally attached themselves to luxury products. Here was a company whose pitch was that your wireless service could cost less.

Mint had an unusual model. It didn't build its own national cellular network. It bought access to T-Mobile's network and sold prepaid service directly to consumers, often in multi-month plans. At the time, it advertised introductory pricing as low as fifteen dollars a month, subject to plan terms.

For Mint, skipping the expense of building towers changed the kind of company it needed to be. The product still had to provide reliable coverage. But winning customers online, keeping service simple and standing out against much bigger brands were especially important.

And Reynolds knew one thing the telecom industry wasn't famous for: making people laugh.

Consider the Super Bowl in 2020. While major brands were spending millions on thirty-second TV spots, Mint bought a full-page ad in The New York Times explaining that it wasn't spending that kind of money on the game. Instead, it promoted a limited offer of free service for eligible sign-ups.

That was more than a stunt. It made Mint's promise visible. Why spend a fortune selling you an affordable phone plan, when saving money is supposed to be the whole point? The ad was an example of the thing being advertised.

Maximum Effort's approach fit the brand: fast videos, minimal fuss, jokes that could travel online and offers people could actually understand. Not every ad became a sensation, and there was no magic formula. But it gave a small wireless brand a personality in a category full of nearly identical messages.

There was a catch, though. A funny video cannot keep a customer's phone connected. It cannot fix billing problems. And it doesn't guarantee that acquiring a customer costs less than the money that customer will eventually spend.

That's where the rest of Mint mattered. The operating team built the product and service. Reynolds and his creative collaborators helped people notice it. Put those two things together, and you might build a much more valuable customer business than either could alone.

In March 2023, T-Mobile announced the acquisition of Ka'ena, with a maximum price of one point three five billion dollars in cash and stock. There were three businesses in the package. Mint was the consumer brand that made the headline irresistible.

Here's why the deal is so interesting. T-Mobile already had the towers. It already supplied Mint's network access. So it wasn't buying new national coverage from a tiny competitor. It was buying established prepaid brands, their customers, and the digital sales and marketing capabilities built around them.

In fact, T-Mobile singled out Mint's direct-to-consumer customer acquisition as one of the strengths it wanted. It could potentially bring those capabilities into a much larger carrier, while securing the wholesale relationship with Mint for the long term.

The deal finally closed in May 2024. T-Mobile's filings later recorded about one point one four billion dollars as the acquisition-date fair value of consideration exchanged, including estimates of contingent and deferred payments. That is a different accounting number from the original maximum deal headline.

And Reynolds' individual proceeds? They were not separately disclosed in those filings. A headline about a billion-dollar acquisition does not reveal what any particular minority investor took home.

But it does reveal the thing a huge carrier thought was worth owning: not another set of towers, but a more effective way to reach customers. The company's distribution engine was part of the prize.

There's one last twist. The advertising operation behind the campaigns also became a business. In 2021, advertising technology company MNTN acquired Maximum Effort Marketing, the agency Reynolds had co-founded with Dewey.

In 2025, MNTN transferred the agency to an affiliate of its original owner while retaining a creative-services relationship. The ownership details matter less than the pattern: the team wasn't merely making advertisements for the businesses in which Reynolds invested. Its creative work was itself an asset that other companies wanted.

And that helps explain why copying the playbook is harder than putting a celebrity in a commercial. It takes a product worth buying, an operating team that can deliver, creative work people choose to watch, and the judgment to make those pieces reinforce one another.

So how did Deadpool end up in a billion-dollar phone deal? He didn't invent the towers. He wasn't the only owner. And the buyer wasn't paying him a billion dollars for telling jokes.

Reynolds helped make a useful but ordinary service impossible to ignore. Instead of only collecting an endorsement fee, he participated as an owner alongside the people building the business.

Aviation Gin showed what entertaining ads could do for a consumer brand. Mint Mobile showed why that talent could matter even more when the product was as unglamorous as a monthly phone bill. And Maximum Effort turned the creative skill itself into a business.

The twist is that Reynolds wasn't just selling attention. He was helping build businesses that could keep earning after the joke was over.

The biggest deal wasn't about the phone. It was about who owned the customers, the story, and a piece of the value that story created.
