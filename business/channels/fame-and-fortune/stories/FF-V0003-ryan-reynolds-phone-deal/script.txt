TITLE: Ryan Reynolds' $1.35B Phone Deal

Ryan Reynolds is famous for playing Deadpool. But one of his strangest business wins had nothing to do with movies. He bought into a discount cellphone company. Then T-Mobile announced a deal worth up to one point three five billion dollars to acquire its parent business.

A movie star. A phone plan. A billion-dollar headline. And here's the twist: Reynolds didn't build a cellular network, and he didn't receive that entire check.

So what exactly was he selling? And why would a giant phone carrier want to buy a business that had been making fun of giant phone carriers?

Start with the number everyone remembers. In 2023, T-Mobile agreed to pay as much as one point three five billion dollars for Ka'ena Corporation, which included Mint Mobile, Ultra Mobile and a wholesale business called Plum. That was the maximum price for the group, not Ryan Reynolds' personal payday.

But Reynolds really was an owner. He had purchased a stake in Mint in 2019, and he wasn't simply reading lines in a celebrity endorsement. He helped sell the idea that paying less for wireless could actually be interesting.

To understand why that was worth so much, go back to a different kind of product: a bottle of gin.

In 2018, Reynolds became a co-owner of Aviation American Gin. Plenty of celebrities had liquor partnerships. His approach was unusual because he treated the advertisements like entertainment people might watch voluntarily.

There was the deadpan delivery, the carefully timed jokes, the feeling that the person selling you the product knew perfectly well how ridiculous celebrity advertising could be. Behind that voice was a creative operation Reynolds built with marketing partner George Dewey: Maximum Effort.

One especially memorable campaign moved at internet speed. After an awkward Peloton holiday commercial became a viral conversation in 2019, Aviation released a quick follow-up starring the same actress, Monica Ruiz. It wasn't a lecture about exercise bikes. It was a clever response to a cultural moment, and people shared it because the ad itself was the joke.

Then came a serious business outcome. In 2020, drinks giant Diageo acquired Aviation Gin and the wider Davos Brands portfolio in a deal worth up to six hundred ten million dollars. That included three hundred thirty-five million dollars upfront and up to two hundred seventy-five million more, contingent on Aviation's performance.

Important: Diageo bought multiple brands. Reynolds was one co-owner, not the person entitled to all six hundred ten million dollars. Still, the sale proved the central idea could work. Instead of renting a famous face for one campaign, put that face inside the ownership and marketing machine.

Now swap gin for something almost comically unglamorous: your monthly phone bill. In November 2019, Mint Mobile announced that Reynolds had purchased an ownership stake.

Mint wasn't building cell towers across America. It was a mobile virtual network operator, meaning it bought access to an existing carrier's network, then packaged and sold service under its own brand. Its service ran on T-Mobile's infrastructure.

That matters. A traditional carrier has to spend extraordinary amounts on spectrum, equipment and network operations. Mint could focus much more of its energy on acquiring customers and delivering a low-cost, digital-first experience.

It sold prepaid plans, often with multi-month commitments, and advertised entry pricing around fifteen dollars a month. That wasn't a promise that every customer would pay fifteen dollars forever. It was a simple, memorable comparison against a phone bill that could feel painfully expensive.

And Reynolds had a natural way to explain it. Not as a technical breakthrough. As a joke about how much people were paying for something they already needed. But Mint wasn't his invention. Founders including David Glickman and Rizwan Kassim had built the company and its wireless operations. Reynolds was bringing attention, creative work and ownership to an existing business.

Here is where the marketing gets interesting. During the 2020 Super Bowl, when giant advertisers paid millions for television spots, Mint used a newspaper ad to explain why it wasn't buying one. It put money toward a promotion for customers instead.

That single stunt told the entire business story. Mint was the company that supposedly spent less on unnecessary things so customers could spend less on wireless. The advertisement wasn't just promoting the product. The advertisement demonstrated the product's promise.

Reynolds and Maximum Effort became known for reacting quickly to the news, making simple videos and using jokes to explain offers that would otherwise be forgettable. Speed mattered. By the time a conventional marketing team approved its clever response, the internet might already have moved on.

But don't confuse a funny ad with a complete business. The service still had to work. Customers had to get coverage, pay their bills and stay long enough to make acquisition costs worthwhile. Mint's founders and operating team managed the company, while Reynolds made its value proposition easier to notice.

This was the important combination: a real low-cost wireless model, a distinctive brand and a marketing engine that could turn a price promotion into something people shared with friends. The face was famous. The business still needed fundamentals.

In March 2023, the unlikely partnership reached its biggest headline. T-Mobile announced it would buy Ka'ena Corporation for up to one point three five billion dollars. The deal covered Mint, Ultra Mobile and Plum. It wasn't a one-point-three-five-billion-dollar purchase of Ryan Reynolds, or even of Mint alone.

Why buy a company that already used your network? Because Ka'ena wasn't selling T-Mobile towers. It brought prepaid customers, recognizable brands and a direct-to-consumer marketing system. In its announcement, T-Mobile specifically praised Mint's digital customer acquisition capabilities.

The transaction closed on May first, 2024. A later T-Mobile annual report placed the acquisition-date fair value of the consideration exchanged at about one point one four billion dollars, including the estimated value of contingent payments. The much bigger headline was the maximum possible deal price, dependent on future performance and other terms.

Reynolds' exact investment return was not publicly broken out in those filings. So anyone multiplying the billion-dollar headline by a guessed ownership percentage and calling it his payday is skipping crucial details.

Still, think about what the buyer was paying for. Mint's original advantage was that it could lease access to a bigger carrier's network and focus on selling wireless service in a smarter way. T-Mobile ultimately acquired those customers and the marketing approach along with the business.

There is one more piece that connects the gin bottle to the cellphone bill. Maximum Effort wasn't only an informal collection of funny commercials. It became a marketing business in its own right.

In 2021, connected-TV advertising company MNTN acquired Maximum Effort Marketing. And in 2025, MNTN transferred the agency to an affiliate of its original owner, while keeping a creative services relationship. Those transactions are documented in the company's public filings. The financial terms aren't a basis for guessing Reynolds' personal earnings.

The pattern is what matters. Reynolds and his partners built a way of making brands feel human and culturally relevant. That work was useful when he had equity in the product, and it could also exist as an agency with other clients.

None of this means every celebrity can repeat it. Reynolds brought unusual fame, timing, creative collaborators and access to companies with real products. Without the underlying service, clever advertising would have had nothing lasting to sell.

So how does Deadpool end up attached to a billion-dollar phone deal? Not by inventing a cellphone network. And not by collecting a billion-dollar check for starring in commercials.

He invested in an operating business, helped make its ordinary value proposition impossible to ignore, and shared in ownership while a larger company eventually bought the group around it.

Aviation Gin showed the strategy in a bottle. Mint showed it could work on a monthly bill. Maximum Effort revealed the machinery connecting both stories.

The cleverest thing Ryan Reynolds sold wasn't gin or wireless service. It was attention that actually made people care about a business. The difference was that, this time, he owned a piece of what they cared about.
