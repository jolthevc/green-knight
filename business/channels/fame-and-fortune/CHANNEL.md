# Fame & Fortune: editorial constitution

## Why viewers click
A universally recognizable name plus an unresolved, consequential financial surprise. The viewer's question should be understandable without business literacy: "Wait, how did THEY get paid?" "Who owned the deal?" "Why did they give that up?" "How did this backfire?"

## Why viewers stay
The star has something to gain or lose, an apparent deal hides a second mechanism, an unlikely antagonist or constraint appears, and each act reshapes the viewer's first assumption. Show the *specific* terms, ownership, distribution and power, not a life-summary with dollar figures.

## Six editorial engines
1. **The secret money machine:** Endorsements versus royalties versus equity versus licensing.
2. **The bet no one expected:** Reputation or cash staked on an unfamiliar industry.
3. **The deal behind the deal:** Distribution agreements, venture stakes, partners, control rights, contingent consideration.
4. **The fight for control:** Masters, catalogs, ownership, brand or likeness rights, contracts and leverage.
5. **The fortune at risk:** A business that struggles, dilutes, disappoints or forces a reversal, without assuming any allegation of misconduct.
6. **Fame as distribution:** How an audience becomes cheap customer acquisition, but also how celebrity does *not* automatically create product demand.

## Required episode question
Who is the star? What precisely was at stake? Who owned what, paid what, and bore the risk? What was the turning point? Which common account of the success is incomplete? What changed because of the choice? What could an informed investor or founder learn that is not the stock explanation?

## Lane boundaries
- **Fame & Fortune:** the celebrity, their bargaining power, image and commercial economics drive the causal story.
- **Silver Spoon:** familial wealth transmission/control across generations is the central question.
- **Money Moves:** a corporate move wins center stage, even if a star appears.
- **Pretty Penny:** the economics of a familiar product or purchase are the center.
- **Fool's Gold:** proven or alleged fraud and how it passed scrutiny, with careful legal status.
- **Fallen Angels:** the decline of a company and its failure mechanism.
- **Business Rivals:** competition itself, if later configured separately.

Cross-channel reuse requires a demonstrably different question and mechanism, plus related IDs.

## Growth-first but not dishonest
Be provocative, even salacious in the **real financial stakes**: betrayals supported by sources, power struggles, hidden economics, damaged partnerships, extraordinary payouts, underestimated risk. Never invent feuds, intimate gossip, illegal acts, motives, quotations, contract clauses, dollar amounts or present net-worth estimates as cash proceeds. Hypothesis, rumor, allegation, opinion and proven fact cannot be blurred. Never promise a scandal if the episode only documents a routine investment.

## Viewer reward
A casual celebrity fan gets a clear irresistible story. A sophisticated viewer gets the actual contractual, distribution, valuation or unit-economics mechanism and its limits. If neither is there, do not commission the story.
