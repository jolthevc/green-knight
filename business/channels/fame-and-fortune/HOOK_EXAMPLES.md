# Fame & Fortune: illustrative opening mechanics

**Not episode scripts.** These are hypothetical constructions, not verified claims. Before reuse, substitute documented contract language, exact dates, actual parties, amounts and source documents. Do not use a sample sentence as a purported real celebrity fact.

## 1. The headline wasn't the payday
BAD: "Today we're looking at a famous person's incredible business journey."
BETTER SHAPE: "Everyone saw the sale price. Almost nobody asked the far more interesting question: How much of it did the celebrity actually own?" Then promptly show a real percentage from a verifiable document or state clearly that the ownership is unknown.

## 2. A familiar face, an unexpected partner
SHAPE: "The celebrity got the spotlight. The company behind the brand got something else: [documented contractual right]. And that detail explains nearly everything that happened next."

## 3. Fame looked like a superpower until the numbers arrived
SHAPE: "Millions of fans were watching. But the business still had to persuade people to buy again. And those are two very different challenges."

## 4. The real fortune was not the famous product
SHAPE: "The public associated the star with [visible entertainment career]. The agreement that changed their economic position was somewhere the cameras rarely pointed."

## 5. The ownership fight
SHAPE: "The song was everywhere. But who controlled the recording? To answer that, you have to separate three different rights that sound like the same thing."

## 6. The risk nobody noticed
SHAPE: "The deal sounded like free money. But there was a trade-off buried in how [distribution/equity/licensing] worked."

## Sample structure for the first 45 seconds
1. **Sentence one:** subject, documented contradiction, high-stakes tangible object.
2. **Sentence two:** what ordinary viewers reasonably believed.
3. **Sentence three:** one concrete new source-backed fact, with a visual document/graphic.
4. **Sentence four:** bigger open question, whose answer is in the actual episode.

Do not use these as empty rhetorical formulas. Every episode's first 45 seconds must be unusually specific to its person, moment and commercial mechanism. The real opening should read like a gripping true story, not marketing copy.
