TITLE: How a Dutch Town Became the World's Flower Marketplace

A rose grown in Kenya can be sold to a European florist without ever entering the Netherlands. So why does a Dutch town still matter to the flower business? In March 2026, its trading cooperative abandoned plans to make more auctions independent of physical locations. Buying and selling could happen on a screen. Getting flowers to the buyer turned out to be harder. To understand why, we have to visit Aalsmeer, a town outside Amsterdam.

Aalsmeer is one important center in a much larger flower network. Its original advantage wasn't a global airport or uniquely perfect soil. It was something growers built themselves: a marketplace. As traders and warehouses gathered around it, the reasons for doing business there began to change. But first, why did farmers need an auction?

Aalsmeer was a farming and fishing community long before the flower trade. It bordered a lake called the Haarlemmermeer. When the Dutch drained the lake in 1852, local livelihoods changed. Farmers grew vegetables and increasingly flowers. Canals connected them with customers in Amsterdam. Heated greenhouses widened what they could grow, and a specialist school developed local expertise. This was an existing community with crops, transport routes and nearby demand, not an empty site awaiting an airport.

The trouble was selling what they grew. Flowers spoil, and farmers cannot wait forever for a buyer. Traders often knew more about demand than the growers. Some growers complained about prices and unreliable payment. Imagine the choice: accept an offer today, or keep a delicate crop and hope somebody pays more tomorrow. That imbalance made collective action attractive.

In late 1911, a group of growers began auctioning flowers in a local café. Another group started a rival auction in early 1912. Two competing marketplaces in one small town. They hadn't invented auctioning: Dutch produce farmers were already using cooperatives. But bringing buyers close to the greenhouses meant growers could pool their supply instead of negotiating alone.

The clock made transactions fast. Picture a lot of roses offered at a deliberately high starting price. The price falls. Buyers wait. One presses a button, and takes the lot at the displayed price. Wait longer and it might get cheaper. Wait too long and someone else gets it. That's a Dutch descending-price auction. It compresses a negotiation into seconds, especially useful for goods that won't stay fresh forever.

But a marketplace is more than a clock. Growers could offer many kinds of flowers together. Traders could compare quality and collect purchases in one visit, rather than going greenhouse to greenhouse. The auction also organized payments. More sellers meant more choice for buyers; more buyers meant a better reason for sellers to participate. Aalsmeer was accumulating something that couldn't be dug out of its soil: a concentration of trading relationships.

After the Second World War, demand and European trade expanded. Aalsmeer's two auctions agreed to merge and needed more space. Relocation outside town was considered, but the municipality fought to keep the operation, offering land with room and connections. The enlarged complex opened in 1972. By then, the auction was an anchor for jobs, roads, exporters and nearby services. It had become a local economic asset worth competing to retain.

Exporters, warehouses, packaging suppliers and transport businesses gathered around the Dutch trading system. Nearby Schiphol Airport helped connect it internationally as air freight grew. But the order matters: Aalsmeer's growers and auctions came first; aviation amplified an existing market. And it wasn't the only Dutch center. The later Royal FloraHolland network includes major hubs at Naaldwijk and Rijnsburg.

Then flower growing itself began moving. Heated greenhouses consumed energy, and changing costs made some overseas farms attractive. Kenya and Ecuador offered different climates and labor costs. Growers expanded abroad, while Dutch firms remained active in trading and distribution. The region could lose some production and retain important business relationships. Its advantage was no longer just growing a flower.

Take a hypothetical batch of Kenyan roses. Some imports travel through European airports and then by truck to Dutch trading hubs. Others are sold directly before arrival, or bypass the Netherlands. Royal FloraHolland says its African shipments use airports including Brussels and Liège, not only Schiphol. No single building or runway is a compulsory detour. So what does this place actually sell? Access to buyers, sorting, and dependable delivery arrangements.

Consider its scale. Royal FloraHolland reported about five point four billion euros in product turnover in 2025, counting flowers and plants. That's the value traded through the system, not the cooperative's profit. Its reported profit after tax was about twelve million euros. Farmers, freight firms and retailers have their own businesses and costs. A marketplace can coordinate an enormous trade without keeping the full selling price.

And some buyers increasingly bypass the clock. A supermarket can negotiate directly with a grower for specific flowers on a set schedule. Auction prices may help inform negotiations, but direct sales and auctions coexist. That is a real threat to the older model: a large buyer may prefer certainty over the chance of a lower price at the morning auction.

That brings us back to 2026. Royal FloraHolland explored auctions with a more unified price across its hubs, and studied sales made straight from farms. In March, it said it would not expand those models for now. Centralizing prices had not clearly improved them overall, while flowers shifted between hubs and logistics costs rose. The hoped-for independence from location was creating new problems.

Here's why. A buyer might need several flower varieties together, early enough to fill customers' orders. A digital screen can agree on prices instantly. It can't put flowers from different farms on the same truck. The cooperative said buyers' demand at individual hubs was too unpredictable for its proposed location-independent system to work broadly. This doesn't mean every purchase requires Aalsmeer. It means coordinating the physical goods remains expensive and difficult.

The concentration has problems of its own. During the spring rush in 2026, Aalsmeer buyers sometimes received flowers late. Royal FloraHolland reported a peak day of roughly one hundred and two thousand flower transactions. Smaller individual purchases meant more handling, while staffing and capacity came under pressure. Buyers don't just need an attractive price. They need flowers before the shop opens or the order leaves.

There are alternatives. Direct contracts avoid some auctions. Importers can use other airports. More buyers can purchase remotely as Royal FloraHolland introduces tools on Floriday. Yet someone still has to inspect, combine and deliver the goods. Aalsmeer can't rely forever on people coming into an auction hall. Its services have to remain better than sending the flowers another way.

The trade also has costs that the auction clock doesn't show. Growers use water and labor; heated greenhouses use energy; freight creates emissions. Some workers face low pay or safety risks. A Fairtrade-commissioned study found certain Kenyan rose supply chains could have lower environmental impacts than some Dutch greenhouse routes, depending on energy and shipping. It isn't a verdict on every rose. Nor does the region's trading success mean everybody along the chain benefits equally.

Now return to that rose. Whether it travels through Aalsmeer is no longer the test. More than a century ago, growers here made it easier for buyers to find their flowers. The trade drew in transport, warehouses and expertise, and the local market became part of a global system. When the cooperative later tried to loosen the link between auctions and locations, it discovered the enduring constraint: agreeing on a price is not the same as delivering fresh flowers on time. Aalsmeer became a flower capital by gathering the market. It stays relevant only as long as it can make that market work.
