# Research packet: OTM-V0001

**Research as of:** 2026-10-11. **Question:** Why did Aalsmeer, within the Dutch floriculture region, become a major flower-trading center, and why did its function persist when growing became international? **Scope:** Aalsmeer and the wider Royal FloraHolland/trade catchment, not the claim that all flowers physically pass through one building. **Period:** Mid-1800s to October 2026; historical origins in 1911–12.

## Executive findings

**PROCEED, with calibrated thesis.** The usual 'the airport made Aalsmeer' claim reverses the chronology. Aalsmeer had an extensive local horticulture base and flower market before Schiphol grew as a global freight gateway. Lake drainage, Amsterdam access, commercial skills and heated growing enabled local production; cooperatives arose in 1911–12 to change bargaining, sorting and payment economics. Later mergers, municipal siting, distribution and nearby transport made a specialized trading system durable. Scholarly account S001/S002 is the causal backbone, corroborated by local S011, logistics S005, and other operational evidence.

**Central correction:** Aalsmeer is an important center inside a multi-hub Dutch and international marketplace; a digital auction transaction is not proof the flower entered Aalsmeer; the Netherlands is not an obligatory detour for every African flower (S005/S006/S010).

## Starting position and first foothold

- Aalsmeer had established livelihoods, including fishing around Haarlemmermeer, and vegetables on drained/peat-rich land. Haarlemmermeer drainage completed in 1852; flowers grew late 1800s. No 'empty land' myth. See S001 5.1–5.2.
- Aalsmeer already shipped to Amsterdam by barge; greenhouse heating and specialized training helped early flowers. Site selection cannot be retroactively explained by a modern global airport. See S001 5.2, S002 6.2.
- Growers lacked negotiating power and reliable settlement versus traders dealing with perishable stock. Cooperative flower auctions, established 1911–12, pooled selling and improved buyer matching. Two different local auctions competed before merger. See S001 5.3 and S011.
- At a Dutch descending-price clock, first purchase at the displayed falling price ends that allocation. This compresses negotiation time; no guarantee of a fair outcome for every grower or shopper. See S004.

## Revenue, cash flow and what the euro amount means

**Royal FloraHolland 2025:** S003 reports about **€5.4bn product turnover** for flowers **and plants** handled within its trading system; company management also reports approximately **€12m after-tax profit**. These are not comparable denominators for a 'margin on flowers': product turnover belongs to goods sold through a cooperative platform, whereas net profit belongs to the cooperative entity and its service economics. Do not calculate 12m/5.4bn as growers' profit margin, or use it as a unit margin for a rose. The 2025 annual landing reports €12.4m but full annual PDF access is not complete (S014 partial). Speech rounds to €12m, grounded by opened S003.

**Independent statistical contrast:** S009 CBS reports 2024 €12.18bn domestic exports and €2.55bn re-exports in the broad **flowers and plants** category. Different year, transaction boundary and coverage from RFH turnover, so excluded from speech rather than stacking impressive numbers. S013 approximately half clock/direct, but measure and 2025 total monetary share differ; narration says substantial direct trading without a ratio.

**Simplified actors and cash economics:**
1. Grower invests in land/greenhouse, energy, water, labor and packing, sells product at auction or by contract and bears cultivation and unsold quality risks.
2. Cooperative coordinates trading, settlement, quality/logistics and digital tools, charges growers/buyers via services and tariffs. Its product throughput is not own net sales or profit.
3. Import agent/forwarder and warehouse collect handling/transport income while bearing timing, temperature and customs compliance obligations.
4. Exporter/wholesaler bundles, grades and distributes; retailer sells at consumer price and bears inventory waste and demand uncertainty.
5. Workers receive wages and bear workplace risks; Dutch and foreign localities obtain selected jobs and fiscal benefits, not the whole consumer flower price.
This is a transaction-structure description, not a verified numerical retail-price split.

## Rival explanations, exceptions and losing cases

- **'It is all Schiphol':** Historically false as origin chronology; Schiphol helped persistence and global scale. S002 documents rail/canal before early aviation and later national mainport investment.
- **'Everything passes through Aalsmeer':** False as a physical assertion. S005 explicitly separates Aalsmeer/Naaldwijk/Rijnsburg and routes from Schiphol, Brussels, Liège, Frankfurt, Paris and Maastricht. Digital and direct activity further separate sale from shipment.
- **'The cooperative controls all prices':** Overstatement; large direct-contract customers have leverage, other hubs exist, and daily clock prices can influence but not dictate negotiations (S002).
- **Competing location:** Liège, Belgium competes with Schiphol according to 2019 logistics research S010. Direct shipment may avoid unnecessary transit; logistics and reliability, not historical fame, determine viability.
- **'Local flowers are always greener':** Cannot be inferred from distance. S007 commissioned Swiss supply-chain LCA reports results that depend on greenhouse energy and carriage mode. A blanket environmental ranking is unsupported.
- **'Cooperative success implies prosperity for everybody':** No. S008 documents living-wage, gender, chemical and water risks in exporting regions. Cross-place incidence matters.

## Human and spatial incidence

Aalsmeer gained a market institution and subsequent warehousing and trading services; Schiphol and neighboring Dutch hubs are complementary. Regional governments faced land/traffic decisions and neighboring residents could face truck traffic (S001 5.4). Producing countries gain cultivation jobs, but Fairtrade S008 reports risks to wages, safety, environmental resources and gender equity. Large retail buyers can bypass clock pricing; smaller sellers value pooled demand. This is not proof of homogeneous local household prosperity.
