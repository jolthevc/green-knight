## Editorial thesis

The founder's descendants sold an increasingly contested company in two halves. Giorgio and Roberto's first sale brought an outside investor; a real 1988 board-control contest exposed the gap between near-half ownership and usable voting influence; Maurizio and that investor stayed divided until his 1993 buyout. The Gucci name, protected as a company brand, outlasted its family ownership.

**Obvious account overturned:** “family feuds made them lose it” is incomplete. People **sold shares**. Succession litigation constrained votes, and operating losses/restructuring led to an investor partnership dispute. The murders, scandals or later turnaround do not automatically establish the sale's precise motivation.

**Strongest counterargument:** A loss-making business and capital constraints might have led to an outsider's purchase even without family feuds. The public record cannot assign each factor a causal percentage. **What survives:** the legal transfer, board block, ownership change and commercial brand continuity.

## Hook alternatives reviewed

1. **Chosen contradiction:** the Gucci family owns no Gucci in 1993, but the name remains. Sharp first-listen clarity.
2. **Failed control vote:** Investors buy nearly half, cannot win board majority. Excellent middle reveal, too much procedural complexity for 0:00.
3. **Born Gucci, blocked from selling Gucci:** High curiosity but overweights Paolo's US trademark case and gives wrong title promise.
