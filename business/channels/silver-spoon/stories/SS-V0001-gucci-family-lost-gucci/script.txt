TITLE: How the Gucci Family Lost Gucci

In 1993, the Gucci family sold the last piece of Gucci. The name stayed on the handbags. The stores stayed open. But the descendants of the man who founded the company no longer owned it.

This was not one dramatic takeover in which somebody seized the business overnight. The family sold it in two halves, years apart. And for a time, the outsider who bought the first half could not fully control the company, while the family member holding the second half could not easily steer it either.

So how did one of fashion's most famous family fortunes end up in somebody else's hands? And how did Gucci keep growing after its founding family was gone?

Start in Florence, where Gucci traces its beginning to Guccio Gucci's shop in 1921. His sons expanded the leather-goods business into an international luxury name. By the time the next generation arrived, Gucci was more than a workshop. It was stores, factories, trademarks, and shares that could be inherited or sold.

Guccio died in 1953. Two family branches matter most to our story. One led through his son Aldo, whose sons included Giorgio, Roberto and Paolo. The other led through Rodolfo, whose only son was Maurizio. These were relatives, but that did not mean they wanted the same future for the company.

In 1983, Rodolfo died, leaving Maurizio a claim to roughly half the business. Suddenly a young heir had extraordinary influence over the company that carried his grandfather's name. But his inheritance was challenged by relatives in Italian legal proceedings. The shares became tangled in a dispute over who could exercise control.

Pause on that. Owning a large stake is powerful. Being able to vote it, install directors and make a company move is another matter. For Gucci, the difference was about to become painfully real.

In April 1988, the company announced that investment bank Morgan Stanley had assembled nearly forty-eight percent of its shares for a client it did not identify. Most came from Giorgio and Roberto Gucci, two of Aldo's sons. The family had not just argued about its inheritance. Some heirs had actually sold much of theirs.

Why would they sell? The surviving accounts offer family disputes, disagreements over management and commercial direction, and different interests among the cousins. Giorgio later told a reporter that new management was not interested in his ideas or the old standards. That is his explanation, not a window into every seller's private motives.

Then the buyer's identity emerged: Investcorp, an investment firm based in Bahrain. It had purchased a position almost equal to Maurizio's. Gucci was no longer simply a conversation between two branches of the same family. Half of the ownership was now aligned with professional investors.

And in June 1988, the stakes became visible inside the boardroom. Reports said Investcorp had four representatives on an eight-member board. It sought to expand the board and take a fifth seat, which could have given it a majority.

That attempt failed. Two court-appointed custodians overseeing Maurizio's disputed shares blocked the proposal. Think about the odd balance. The investor had bought nearly half the company but could not secure a board majority. Maurizio still claimed the other half, yet his ownership was under legal challenge. The Gucci name sat above a company with no uncomplicated path to control.

Consider Maurizio's cousin Paolo, a designer who had spent years working for Gucci. In a 1988 American court case, he fought to identify himself on products he designed. The court allowed that, under restrictions, but barred him from using Paolo Gucci as an independent brand that customers might mistake for the company's goods.

A man born Gucci could still be prevented from putting his own name on a competing Gucci label. It was an unusually sharp illustration of what the family was fighting over: being related to the founder did not make the trademark, or the business built around it, yours to use however you wished.

But the largest question remained Maurizio. He had inherited the other half and wanted to reshape Gucci's future. He was trying to restore a luxury identity that, according to later accounts, had become stretched by licensing and a sprawling range of products. Making a brand feel exclusive again can mean selling fewer kinds of things, changing stores and giving up easier revenue today.

That was a real business challenge, not merely a family argument. A famous name could still be attached to an operation that was losing money. Repositioning it required management, investment and time. The investor next to Maurizio had an economic stake in the outcome and its own ideas about how the company should be run.

For several years, Maurizio and Investcorp remained owners of opposite halves. Their disagreements did not resolve themselves. In July 1993, the dispute brought the partners into a New York courtroom. Investcorp accused Maurizio of mismanagement. Maurizio argued that the investors were putting profit ahead of craftsmanship. Neither accusation, on its own, proves who was right.

Then, in September 1993, Maurizio sold his remaining half to Investcorp. Contemporary reports estimated the price at between one hundred and fifty and two hundred million dollars. That was the reported price of the stake, not a verified account of his personal proceeds after taxes or debts.

With that purchase, Investcorp owned the whole company. There was no longer a family half and an outside half. The long shareholder standoff had ended by the simplest possible mechanism: one side bought the other.

Maurizio left the chief executive's job, although the company said he would remain a senior adviser. In a statement reported at the time, he said a single owner would better assure Gucci's future. Investcorp said the deal would bring new capital and liquidity. Those were the participants' claims, not guarantees that a turnaround would work.

The family that had created the brand had sold its ownership. But no one needed to rename a store. The protected commercial name belonged to the company, not to whichever descendants happened to own its shares that year. Investcorp could keep selling handbags marked Gucci because it now owned the business behind them.

Here is the detail that can distort the entire story. Maurizio Gucci was murdered in 1995, two years after the final sale. His death made the family famous for another, darker reason. But it did not end family ownership. That had already happened in 1993, through a transaction.

And the business did not fade away when the family left. Gucci's own corporate history dates a turnaround from 1994, with creative director Tom Ford and executive Domenico De Sole central to the recovery. The company began building a different future under owners and managers who were not the founding family.

In October 1995, Investcorp sold forty-eight percent of Gucci Group through a public offering. In March 1996, it sold the remainder. After decades of family ownership and several years under a financial investor, Gucci's shares were now in public hands.

By 1998, Gucci brand revenue had passed a billion dollars. Revenue is sales, not profit. Still, the change creates an uncomfortable question. Had the family sold just before the valuable part of the story?

It is tempting to answer yes. But there is a problem with hindsight. We can see the company that emerged under new management. We cannot see the alternate Gucci in which divided owners kept their shares, somehow financed the changes and produced the same result. A later recovery does not prove the family could have waited for it.

The logo would pass through another corporate battle when LVMH bought a large stake in 1999 and Gucci aligned itself with PPR, the group later renamed Kering. But that is a different contest. By then the original family was no longer a shareholder in the company it founded.

What happened to their money is another question. Selling a company stake does not mean becoming poor, and a transaction price is not the same thing as cash a person can spend. We do not have a reliable ledger of every family member's private wealth.

Go back to the handbag. Its label tells you the brand. It does not tell you which family owns the shares. The Gucci family's story is not that its name disappeared. It is that a name can survive almost anything, while ownership depends on whether the people who inherit it can, and choose to, keep it.

This is Silver Spoon, where we follow the fortunes families build, pass down and sometimes let go.
