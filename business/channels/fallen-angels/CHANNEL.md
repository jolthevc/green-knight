# Business postmortems

The question is “How did this business lose, and what could people actually see at the time?”

A company can survive while a product, division or advantage fails. Define the thing that failed before deciding the story. Bankruptcy, liquidation, acquisition, retreat, poor returns and lost leadership are different outcomes.

## Broad appeal and runway

Use familiar products, iconic brands, ambitious launches, vanished services and visible changes in everyday life. Viewers should care before hearing a balance-sheet term. The subject can span retail, media, technology, food, travel, consumer services and industrial businesses when the stakes are intelligible.

| Series | Story engine |
| --- | --- |
| Lost Advantage | How did a leader lose the position that made it successful? |
| Failed Launch | Why did a well-funded or ambitious product fail to find a viable audience? |
| The Slow Unraveling | Which interacting pressures gradually damaged the business? |
| The Breaking Point | What turned a fragile situation into a crisis? |
| The Wrong Diagnosis | Which familiar explanation fails to account for the evidence? |
| The Rescue That Wasn't | Why did a plausible turnaround fail or arrive too late? |

These are internal series. Avoid an endless slate of “Company X failed because it ignored innovation.” Each episode needs a distinct failure mechanism and specific evidence.

## Boundaries

Fallen Angels centers deterioration, failure or squandered advantage. Money Moves centers a choice and its trade-offs, even when a decision fails. Pretty Penny explains an ongoing economic system. Changing Hands centers a consequential transaction. Fool’s Gold centers how deception worked. Silver Spoon centers family fortunes and inheritance.

Fraud can be a documented cause here, but if the viewer mainly wants to understand the trick, route the story to Fool’s Gold. A family-controlled business can feature here when its operating collapse is central, rather than inheritance or dynasty control.

Avoid a second version of the same postmortem with a new title. A broader industry decline needs a materially different question and mechanism, with links to related episodes.

## Required forensic research

Add the following to the shared research and synthesis:
1. Definition of failure: which entity, product, advantage or financial outcome, and over which period?
2. Baseline: why did the business originally work, and what sustained it?
3. Timeline: early pressure, warning signals, management response, accelerating damage and eventual outcome.
4. Mechanism: how did the pressure affect demand, costs, margins, financing or execution?
5. Symptoms versus causes: what was merely visible evidence of a deeper problem?
6. Structural fragility versus trigger: what made the business vulnerable, and what precipitated the crisis?
7. Contemporary knowledge: which warnings were observable then, rather than obvious only in hindsight?
8. Alternatives and constraints: could a different response realistically have changed the outcome?
9. Competing explanations: technology, leverage, competition, leadership, regulation, external shocks, timing or execution as relevant.
10. Human consequences and limits: documented effects without invented personal stories.

Use filings, financial statements, bankruptcy/court records when relevant, contemporary reporting, company announcements, credible interviews and industry evidence. Corporate explanations and later memoirs can be self-serving; identify the perspective. A complaint or accusation is not an adjudicated finding. Keep allegation, documented fact and interpretation distinct.

Do not accept a repeated internet anecdote as the cause. Examine original evidence and inconvenient counterexamples. Mark unresolved issues honestly. If current proceedings or outcomes matter, recheck them near production and avoid presenting an ongoing case as settled.

## Causal reasoning

A product failure may result from several interacting causes. Show which explanation is best supported and which contribution remains uncertain. Do not force a single fatal mistake or villain into a complicated history.

Use appropriate metrics: revenue, cash, operating profit, debt obligations, customer retention or market share as the story requires. Separate a financing crisis from an unviable product and temporary demand shocks from structural decline.

For counterfactuals, establish a feasible alternative and acknowledge uncertainty. “They could have saved it” needs more than hindsight or an unverified offer anecdote.

## Payoff

The viewer should understand the failure mechanism, which warning mattered, why the obvious story is incomplete and what remains uncertain. A warning sign is useful only if the script explains how it relates to the failure. Avoid generic “adapt or die” advice and unsupported claims that collapse was inevitable.
