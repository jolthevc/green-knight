TITLE: Blockbuster: The Netflix Rival It Could Not Afford to Keep

In 2007, Netflix reported losing subscribers in a quarter. At the same time, Blockbuster was gaining ground with a service that combined DVDs by mail with something Netflix couldn't offer: thousands of physical stores.

Blockbuster had spotted the threat. It had built a response. For a moment, it looked as if the supposedly doomed giant might fight its way back.

Three years later, Blockbuster filed for bankruptcy.

We're usually told a simple story about that failure: a complacent video chain ignored the future and lost to Netflix. Except Blockbuster didn't ignore Netflix. That is what makes the real story more interesting.

To understand it, start with what made Blockbuster enormous. For a generation, the store solved Friday-night entertainment. You drove over, wandered the aisles, chose a movie and carried it home. Convenient, as long as the shelves had what you wanted, the store was open, and you remembered to return the disc.

But the very thing customers liked came with expensive infrastructure. Stores needed leases, workers and shelves of inventory. Those bills arrived whether anyone rented a movie or not.

Then Netflix offered something different: DVDs delivered through the mail, no trip to the store and, eventually, a flat subscription without traditional late fees. Redbox put cheap rental kiosks in places people already visited. Blockbuster was being challenged from two directions: less expensive convenience on one side, no-store convenience on the other.

The famous story says Netflix's founders offered to sell the young company to Blockbuster in 2000 for fifty million dollars, and were laughed out of the room. That account appears in later retellings. Former Blockbuster chief executive John Antioco has disputed the suggestion that serious acquisition talks occurred. It makes a memorable anecdote, but it can't carry the explanation for what happened next.

Because Blockbuster fought back. It launched a DVD-by-mail operation in 2004 and eliminated most late fees in early 2005. That second decision was costly. The company warned that abandoning late fees could mean giving up hundreds of millions of dollars in annual operating income. It was replacing a dependable source of money while investing in a business that might not pay off for years.

Then came Total Access in 2006. A customer could receive a disc by mail and return it to a Blockbuster store, getting another movie without waiting for the mail. Suddenly, the store network had a new purpose. Netflix couldn't offer an immediate in-person exchange because it had no stores. The response gained real traction. In one quarter in 2007, Netflix lost about fifty-five thousand subscribers, according to contemporary reporting, while Blockbuster's combined subscription offering was growing. That didn't mean Blockbuster was winning the entire market. It meant the rival had created a response strong enough to force an uncomfortable question: what if thousands of stores weren't just a cost, but a weapon?

This was a genuinely clever response: turn yesterday's enormous fixed-cost burden into a convenience advantage.

But there was a catch. Total Access was expensive to operate. According to subsequent reporting, the free in-store exchange cost Blockbuster money, while its online business required heavy investment. The faster the new service grew, the more cash it could demand before that growth paid off.

And this is where the story stops being about technology and starts being about financing.

When Viacom separated Blockbuster into an independent company in 2004, the transaction left it carrying substantial debt, roughly a billion dollars by the figures discussed in contemporary accounts. One reason the debt mattered was the way Blockbuster had been separated from Viacom: a large payout accompanied the spin-off. A familiar retail business that needed money for transformation began its independent life with much less financial flexibility. The company couldn't simply ignore interest payments while waiting to see whether a new subscription system won customers.

Debt changes how long a company can wait. A business funded by patient investors can accept near-term losses while building something new. A company with creditors has scheduled obligations, even when its new strategy needs more time.

Blockbuster was funding a turnaround with two clocks running. One counted the years it might take to win back customers. The other counted the cash it had to find now.

The pressure reached the boardroom. Billionaire investor Carl Icahn became a major shareholder and director. Antioco and directors clashed over spending, strategy and the chief executive's compensation. Those disputes were real, but the participants have described them differently. Some viewed Total Access as essential investment; others saw losses and costs Blockbuster could not sustain.

In 2007, Antioco agreed to depart. Jim Keyes, formerly of 7-Eleven, succeeded him. Under the new leadership, Blockbuster pulled back from elements of Total Access and changed its pricing. The aggressive exchange offer that had helped distinguish it from Netflix became less generous. Investors who favored an immediate reduction in losses had reason to welcome the retreat. Customers who had signed up for the special convenience had reason to see it as a weaker deal. Either way, the company paid for both directions: expensive expansion first, then a product that became harder to sell once the investment was scaled back.

So the company finally had an answer to Netflix, but couldn't maintain the same answer under its financial and leadership constraints.

It's tempting to say that one bad boardroom decision killed Blockbuster. That would be too easy. Total Access was costly, and nobody can prove it would have become profitable. Meanwhile Netflix began streaming in 2007, changing the contest again. Even winning the DVD-by-mail battle might not have won the next one.

The store network was another double-edged sword. It offered convenience when customers wanted to exchange discs instantly. But every additional shift toward mail, kiosks and eventually streaming threatened the traffic that paid for those stores. What had made Blockbuster powerful was becoming a cost it couldn't shed quickly.

By 2010, that squeeze had become a financing crisis. In September, Blockbuster filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection, reporting substantial debt and striking a deal with bondholders intended to reduce it. Stores closed. Netflix kept advancing. What had once been a national habit became an increasingly expensive legacy operation. Consider the sequence from a customer's point of view: Blockbuster's stores had made movie night easy, Total Access briefly made that network useful again, and then the experience moved toward getting a film without leaving the sofa. Each new wave made the same real estate less central to the purchase, while the leases and inventory were slow to disappear.

Carl Icahn later acknowledged that Total Access might have helped, over time, and described Blockbuster as his worst investment. Those retrospective comments don't prove an alternative future. They do show why the decision was not as simple as the popular story suggests.

Blockbuster didn't lack the imagination to compete with Netflix. It had a plausible counterattack. But it was attempting to fund a difficult transition while its original business was declining, the debt clock was running and management was divided over how much pain to accept.

That's what makes this failure different from the usual 'adapt or die' lecture. It wasn't enough to recognize the new business model. Blockbuster had to survive long enough to make it work.

It saw the future coming. The harder question was whether it could afford to meet it.
