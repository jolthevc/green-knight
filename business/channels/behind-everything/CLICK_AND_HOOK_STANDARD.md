# Click, hook, suspense and payoff: the operating playbook

## Develop the package BEFORE writing the script
1. Identify the unusual but supportable observation. One sentence a friend would actually repeat.
2. Produce **10+ titles** from at least three distinct angles: contradiction, consequence, and familiar-thing mystery. Do not just change synonyms.
3. Produce **three distinct thumbnail concepts**. Each needs one visual anchor, one clear visual contrast and ideally 0-3 words of text. Legible on a phone, credible at a glance.
4. Select the best three **paired** title/thumbnail promises; consider whether title and image add complementary information rather than repeat one another.
5. Draft two alternative openings for each leading *angle*, cold-read as a complete stranger.
6. Verify against actual research BEFORE committing to a title. A false punchline is worse than a weaker honest one.

## A strong title
Lead with the familiar object/brand or universal frustration and a surprising consequence: "Where Your Returned Clothes Really Go"; "Why Every City Is Starting to Look the Same." Specific, simple, conversational. Avoid 'The Fascinating Economics of...', 'The Rise and Fall...', 'Everything You Need to Know...', 'The Shocking Truth...' and vague 'Secrets...' by default. Do not assert impossibly absolute claims such as 'all', 'never' and 'every' without nuance.

## Opening obligations (diagnostic targets, not fixed formula)
- **0:00-0:05**: Show the actual recognizable thing and an intriguing, *true* paradox or consequence. No logo, greetings, channel intro, scene-setting essay or jargon.
- **0:05-0:15**: Human stakes. Establish what doesn't add up; do not say 'you won't believe'.
- **0:15-0:30**: Offer the first concrete evidence/partial answer and specify the bigger question. Convince the viewer the thumbnail wasn't a trick.
- **0:30-1:30**: Move the story forward, with an actual discovery or action, not a table of contents.
- **Early movement**: Pay off an important piece quickly, then let the answer introduce a better question.
Timing must follow what the real film can support. No arbitrary jump-cuts, reveal quotas or mandatory cliffhangers.

## Holding attention in the body
- Follow a real item/customer/decision through a journey where the rules change as evidence accumulates.
- Use escalating consequences and *earned* re-interpretations, not rhetorical questions sprinkled over unchanged facts.
- Switch modes intentionally: concrete person/object -> visual evidence -> simple mechanism -> cost/objection -> real consequence.
- Simplify statistics to one comparison at a time. Do not stack three unspoken graphs.
- Tell the real objection at the moment it hurts the argument most; don't hide it until the footnotes.
- Always know the viewer question, what changed, what remains unresolved and why the next minute is worth watching.
- The middle is often the kill zone: cut repeated history, definitions, timeline filler, generic company biography and redundant qualification. If the middle lacks stakes, rebuild the causal spine.
- Quiet or emotion beats are allowed. Motion without discovery does not create retention.

## Ending
Resolve the advertised contradiction clearly. Return to the object/scene from the cold open but with a changed meaning. A beautiful thought that doesn't answer the title fails. Avoid a long generic speech; after real payoff a single connected next-watch CTA can follow.

## Packaging quality gate
Each candidate has: factual question, exact promised reveal, possible thumbnail, first-frame visual, 15-second hook, first payoff, midfilm escalation, final payoff, evidence risk, overlap risk, expected asset difficulty. Score for understandable curiosity and truthfulness. Do not choose the 'most viral' by gut confidence alone; make testable hypotheses and learn from real uploads.

## Packaging change after script
Changing the title/thumbnail can change the factual promise. Recheck hook, sources and closer; reapprove scripts if a new substantive claim is introduced. Actual Studio tests and analytics, when available, supersede guesses, never documentary evidence.
